Case 5 — The Reference Call for a Difficult Ex-Colleague

509 words

The Situation

A former colleague — someone who was talented but difficult, and whom you were relieved when they left — has listed you as a reference. The company calls. They specifically ask: 'Would you rehire this person?'

Your Move — Decide Before Reading On

Option A

Lie warmly. 'Absolutely, they were great.'

What likely happens:

They get the job. Six months later, they exhibit the same difficult patterns at the new company. The new company eventually traces back to you and remembers the recommendation. Your credibility as a reference — and as a professional — takes permanent damage. If you're ever asked for references from now on, you are silently marked as unreliable.

What you gained:

Short-term relief. Avoided an awkward moment.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Long-term professional credibility. Also: the new colleagues at the target company, who now have a difficult person on their team partly because of you.

What you should have noticed:

Reference calls are one of the higher-stakes ways your professional reputation is tested. Warmth is not the same as honesty.

Option B

Decline to give the reference. 'I don't think I'm the best reference for [X]. I'd rather have them find someone who knows their work better.'

What likely happens:

The company reads this as what it is — a soft no. Your ex-colleague may or may not learn you declined. If they learn, they may be angry, but they cannot legitimately complain — you warned them by declining. Your credibility is preserved.

What you gained:

Honesty preserved. Reputation intact. Awkwardness avoided.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Possible loss of the ex-colleague as a professional relationship. Some awkwardness with your shared network.

What you should have noticed:

Declining to be a reference IS a form of reference. Any experienced recruiter reads it correctly.

Option C

Give the honest reference. Specific, factual, both strengths and weaknesses.

What likely happens:

The company gets the full picture and can make an informed decision. If they hire anyway, they're aware and can prepare. If they don't hire, the ex-colleague may trace it back to you and be angry. But you have done the job of a reference correctly.

What you gained:

Professional integrity. Real value to the calling company. Clean record.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Possibly the ex-colleague as a relationship. Possibly awkwardness in shared circles. Emotional intensity of the honest call.

What you should have noticed:

Honesty in reference calls is what makes the whole institution of references useful. You are contributing to a shared professional infrastructure.

The Wrap

The default answer is B — decline to be a reference. If you're asked to give a reference for someone you can't fully endorse, decline. If you feel obligated to give it, give it honestly (C). Never do (A). The world of professional references only works because most references are, if imperfect, at least honest. When you contribute to the erosion of that norm, you damage the entire system future you will need to rely on.
