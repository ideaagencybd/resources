The Figure-It-Out Loop

When you do not know, you still have a sequence.
The idea

The Hook

The board is in a language you cannot read. Your phone is at twelve percent. The connection you were supposed to make left twenty minutes ago, and the only English in this terminal was a recorded apology.

You are not stupid. You are not helpless. You have no script.

Most people wait for a feeling of readiness that does not arrive. They stand in the wrong queue because it is a queue. They refresh the airline app until the battery dies. They ask a stranger a question so vague that help is impossible.

A smaller number of people do something less photogenic. They look. They name the actual job. They find one source better than a guess. They take a small action. They notice what it taught them. They go again.

That sequence is this entire course, compressed. Levels 1 and 2 gave you a pause and a clean mind. This level is what you do with both. The promise is not "I already know." The promise is "I can figure things out."

The room

The Scenario

Thursday, 9:40 a.m. Mara's third day.

Elena pings between meetings: "Need Q3 vendor spend in a one-pager for Friday steering. The last person who owned this left in June. It's in the system. Keep it simple — leadership just wants the picture."

Mara opens "the system." Email works. The finance tool returns access denied. Intranet search yields a 2022 deck titled Vendor Process v3 and a broken link. Jonah is on a call. The person who left is not answering LinkedIn.

Friday is tomorrow. Elena will be in The Room with people who decide budgets. Mara has never seen a vendor-spend one-pager here. She does not know which vendors count, which currency, or whether "Q3" means calendar or fiscal.

She has six hours of real work before she looks like the new person who cannot do a simple ask.

What does she do in the next twenty minutes?

Your move

Your Move

Do not keep reading yet. Write the first three things you would actually do.

Common move. Wander. Click every menu until a lockout warning. Email Elena "could you send a bit more context?" Google "how to make a vendor spend report." You are moving. You are not aiming.

Better move. Ask Jonah, "Hey, do you know how vendor spend works here?" He might know. He might also give you a ten-minute rant after which you still cannot log in. Vague questions produce vague tours.

Expert move. List the unknowns before you hunt: access, definition of "vendor spend," time window, audience, a previous one-pager, owner of the data. Attack in order of what unblocks everything else — usually access and definition, not design.

Resourceful move. Run a named loop on paper in twenty minutes: See what is in front of you. Think what the job really is. Find the smallest sources that could answer it. Choose one next action. Act. Notice what broke. Adjust. The list is the output of the first two steps, not a personality trait.

Why the common move fails: motion feels like competence. Unguided clicking creates lockouts, wrong extracts, and a Slack trail that makes you look lost. Elena asked for a picture, not evidence that you tried hard.

What can go wrong with the expert move: a beautiful list of unknowns and a blank page at 4 p.m. A list is not a loop. A loop includes a small action before you feel ready.

The idea

The Big Idea

Resourcefulness is not a talent you have or lack. It is a sequence you run when the map is missing.

People who "just figure it out" look lucky from the outside. Up close they are boring. They see the scene. They refuse the wrong job. They find one better source than their guess. They take a step small enough to survive being wrong. They update.

You met a short version of this in the front of the course. This is the working copy — new job, unfamiliar tool, airport at twelve percent. Everything later in this level (search, verify, ask, sprint, practice, constraints, enough) is a zoomed-in piece of the same loop. If you keep one tool from the entire course, keep this one.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Seven moves. Not a personality. A circuit.

See. What you can point at, before the story. Login fails. Deck is from 2022. Elena's sentence has four nouns and no definition. The Pause from Level 1 belongs here: if you are hot, you will "see" a humiliation instead of a missing password. Facts vs Stories from Level 2 belongs here too. "I am going to fail Friday" is a story. "I cannot enter the finance tool" is a fact.

Think. What is the actual job, and what would "done" look like? Elena does not need a procurement expert by Friday. She needs a one-pager leadership can use. That is not "understand the whole vendor system." First principles, used lightly: someone has the numbers; the numbers have a definition; The Room has a question.

Find. Who or what already knows a piece of this? People, documents, the system, a previous file. Finding is a skill — the next lesson. You are not required to invent knowledge that already lives in a shared drive.

Choose. One next action, not twelve. It should teach you something even if it fails. "Email Elena a memoir of my confusion" teaches little. "Get a working login, or a named person who can grant one, in the next hour" tells you whether the blocker is access or something else.

Act. Do the small thing. Now. Not after one more article about being proactive.

Adapt. The world talks back. The login works but the export is empty. The previous one-pager uses a different definition. That is not failure. That is data. Change the next step.

Reflect. Two minutes. What did I think was true? What showed up? What changes in the next loop? Skip this and you will repeat the same twenty minutes tomorrow with more panic.

Herbert Simon spent a career on a blunt fact: humans do not optimize. They satisfice. They search until they have something good enough for the decision in front of them, because the world will not pause for a complete model. This loop is how a resourceful person satisficing looks in practice. You are not building a perfect map. You are taking a walk that gets less stupid each lap.

John Boyd's OODA loop — observe, orient, decide, act — is a cousin from aerial combat. This course adds Find, Adapt, and Reflect on purpose. In civilian life the missing piece is rarely "decide." It is "someone already knows" and "the first action taught you the wrong lesson and you did not notice."

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Figure-It-Out Loop

Write it on a card. Seven words.

See → Think → Find → Choose → Act → Adapt → Reflect

  1. See (2–5 min). List only what you can point at. "Login returns 'access denied.' Intranet has a 2022 deck. Elena's message was 9:40. Friday steering is 10:00 a.m." Stories go in a second column.
  2. Think (3–8 min). Four questions in writing: What is the actual job, in one sentence? What would "good enough for this audience" look like? What is not the job? What would have to be true for this to be possible by the deadline?
  3. Find (5–15 min). Three buckets, not the whole internet. People: who has done this, who owns the tool. Artifacts: previous files, tickets, emails with the old owner on them. Systems: the tool, help desk, official docs for this version. One candidate in each bucket.
  4. Choose (1 min). One next action that unblocks the most, or teaches the most. Circle it. If you cannot circle one, you are still in Think.
  5. Act. Do that one thing. A timer if you are a wanderer. Fifteen minutes of acting beats an hour of preparing to act.
  6. Adapt (2 min). What did the world say? Keep / drop / change. "Help desk can grant access by 2 p.m. if Elena approves" is an adaptation. "I clicked around and now I am more scared" is not.
  7. Reflect (2 min). One line: what I believed, what I learned, next loop's first step. Then back to See with better eyes.

Worked example — airport.

You missed a connection in a terminal you cannot read. Phone at 12%.

  • See: Board unreadable. Boarding pass shows a missed flight. Battery 12%. A desk with the airline logo and a queue of twelve. A paper sign with a QR code on a pillar.
  • Think: The job is not "understand aviation." It is "get on a later flight today, or a protected overnight, without losing the bag." Good enough: a confirmed booking and a gate, or a hotel voucher. Not the job: arguing about whose fault the delay was.
  • Find: The desk; a staff member with a tablet; a passenger from the same original flight. Artifacts: the pass, the QR, the airline app if you dare spend 3% of battery. Systems: the airline's official rebooking page, not a random blog.
  • Choose: The desk, because a human can rebook and issue a voucher. App as backup if the queue is theatre.
  • Act: Join the right queue. On the walk, screenshot the pass and the QR so the phone can die.
  • Adapt: Desk says the flight is full; stand-by or reroute through a city you did not plan. You now have a real choice, not a fog.
  • Reflect: The QR was a hotel voucher you almost missed. Next time, photograph every sign before the battery conversation.

Same loop. Different room. That is the point.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

When you have ninety seconds — elevator, Slack, a gate agent looking at you — four questions:

  1. What do I actually see?
  2. What is the job in one sentence?
  3. Who or what might already know a piece of this?
  4. What is the smallest action that would teach me something in the next ten minutes?

Then do number four. The rest of the loop can catch up while you move.

A holding sentence if someone is watching: "I am going to confirm access and definition before I build the page. I will have a first version or a clear blocker to you by 2:00." That is the loop, spoken in a language Elena can use.

The hack

The Hack

Name the loop out loud, or in a message to yourself, before you open the tenth tab.

The cheap failure in unfamiliar systems is silent wandering. The moment you say "I am in Find — I need a previous one-pager and a login, not another article," you have already beaten most of the room.

If you work with other people, it scales: "I am still in See. I do not have a definition of vendor spend yet." Status-safe. Also how you stop a team from decorating a number nobody defined.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is a fast intern for Think and Find. It is a dangerous intern for See and Verify. It has never been in your finance tool. It will invent a plausible vendor taxonomy and a confident page structure that does not match how this company books spend.

Use AI for: turning a messy ask into unknowns; generating search queries and "who might know" roles; drafting a one-sentence job definition you can test on Elena; a premortem: "If this page is wrong on Friday, what will have been true?"

Do not use AI for: pretending it can see your screen; inventing numbers, policy, or "typical Q3 vendor spend"; sending Elena a plan the model wrote that you cannot execute.

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Context: I have an unclear task in an unfamiliar system.
The ask, verbatim:
"""
[paste]
"""
What I can actually see (facts only):
[bullets]
Deadline: [time]
Audience: [who will use the output]

Goal: Help me run a Figure-It-Out Loop.
1) Restate the actual job in one sentence, plus what is NOT the job.
2) List the unknowns in order of what unblocks everything else.
3) Suggest three Find targets: one person-role, one artifact, one system.
4) Propose ONE next action I can finish in 15 minutes, and what I should learn even if it fails.
5) Draft a 4-line update I could send my manager by [time].

Constraints: No fake confidence. No invented tools, numbers, or company processes. Flag guesses as guesses.
Output: Numbered lists, short.
Verification: Tell me what I must confirm with a human or a primary source before I act.

What to verify: every proper noun the model uses. If you did not type it, treat it as a rumor.

Do not paste: customer lists, payroll, health data, credentials, anything that would be a problem on a café screen.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "give the loop a place to live so it does not dissolve into tabs."

Current examples of the category, not a shopping list: a single note titled LOOP with the seven words as headings; a timer for Act, because Find expands to fill the afternoon; screenshots of the unfamiliar system so you can search error text later; a password manager and a help-desk ticket, not logins in a draft email; offline copies of boarding passes, because airports eat batteries.

When not to use technology: building a dashboard of your unknowns is not See. It is interior decorating. The loop should fit on an index card.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Skipping See.

The body wants to hunt or to perform. You open search. You message five people. You start a slide titled "Q3 Vendor Spend." You have not yet noticed that you cannot define the noun in the title.

The other skip is Reflect. You act, something weird happens, you act again in the same way with more force. That is not a loop. That is a rut with sweat on it.

Recovery: if you are already twenty tabs in, stop. Write five facts you can point at. Write the job in one sentence. Choose one action. You do not need to confess that you wandered. You need to stop wandering.

A third failure: treating the loop as a personality test. "I am not a resourceful person." Resourcefulness is the sequence, not a mood. Run it while you still feel like an impostor.

Defense

The Defense

The loop fails when:

  • Seconds matter and a protocol already exists. Fire, bleeding, a production outage with a runbook. Do the runbook. Do not workshop the job definition while the room fills with smoke.
  • Guessing is malpractice — electrical, medical, legal, anything that can harm a body or a license. Find a qualified human first. The loop still helps you ask a better question. It does not make you the expert.
  • The blocker is permission, not knowledge. No loop unlocks a finance system Elena has to approve. Name the blocker early. That is the loop working.
  • You use Adapt as a way to never choose. Endless small actions can be cowardice in a costume.

Limitation: this will not make an unfamiliar company feel like home in a week. It will make you the person who produces a next step instead of a fog.

Council

The Masters' Table

Richard Feynman. Through his lens, not knowing is the honest starting state, not a stain. Locate the exact edge of your ignorance — "I do not know how this company defines vendor" — rather than paper over it with a slide. See and Think are how you find the edge.

Marie Curie. Systematic work in conditions that were never convenient. Measurement, notebook, next experiment. She did not wait for a perfect laboratory before she started isolating. Adapt was the contents of the notebook after the procedure failed.

Benjamin Franklin. Define the aim, try a small experiment, write down what happened. His Junto was a Find step with people. His self-improvement charts were Reflect with a calendar. Through Franklin's lens, resourcefulness is civic and ordinary, not a lone-genius myth.

Herbert Simon. Bounded rationality. You will not get a complete picture of vendor spend, the airline's network, or a new job. Satisficing is not laziness. It is respect for time. Search until the next action is clear, then take it.

Andrew Grove. Output. Through High Output Management, the question is not "did I look busy in an unfamiliar system?" It is "what is the output this meeting needs, and what is in my way?" Elena asked for a picture. The loop should produce a picture or a named blocker, not a memoir of effort.

John Boyd. Speed of cycling matters more than the elegance of any single pass. A crude loop run twice beats a perfect plan run never. Where Boyd can mislead: civilian work is not a dogfight. Reckless cycling without Verify ships confident nonsense.

Synthesis. Uncertainty is normal. Small honest steps beat theatrical plans. Writing things down is part of thinking, not a clerk's afterthought.

Where the council could be wrong. Feynman-style relish for ignorance can look like detachment when a manager needs a number by Friday. Curie's endurance can romanticize conditions you should leave. Simon's satisficing can become an excuse for sloppy. Grove's output focus can crush See — you ship a page that is on time and wrong. The loop includes all seven for a reason.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to Thursday, 9:40 a.m.

Calm. Mara does not reply in the first minute. Shoulders. One breath. The Pause is still the first move of a bad morning.

Observe. New job, day three. Elena wants a one-pager for Friday 10:00. Previous owner gone. Finance login fails. A 2022 deck exists. Jonah is on a call.

Define. A leadership-usable picture of Q3 vendor spend, definition attached, by tomorrow morning. Not "become procurement." Not "protect my image as someone who never asks."

Verify. She does not know fiscal versus calendar Q3, which vendors are in, or whether a previous one-pager exists. She will not invent those.

Prioritize. Access and definition unblock everything. Design of the page is last.

Find resources / people. Help desk for access. Elena for a one-line definition and a previous example. Jonah, later, for where people actually export this. Shared drive for the old owner's files. Official docs after she can log in.

Options. Wander the menus. Send a fog email. Wait for Jonah. Or one tight loop: ticket + a four-line clarification to Elena + a hunt for a previous file.

Risks. Asking Elena might look green. Shipping a wrong picture will look worse in The Room. A long question gets ignored. A precise question might get a two-line answer that saves the page.

Choose. The tight loop.

Communicate. To Elena, at 9:52: "I can do the one-pager for Friday. Two things that will make it accurate: (1) may I get finance-tool access today — my login is denied — and (2) do we have a previous version I should match? I am treating Q3 as fiscal unless you say otherwise. I will send a skeleton by 4:00 with whatever numbers I can verify, and flag anything still empty."

Act. Ticket to help desk with Elena copied. Search the drive for the old owner's name + "vendor." Screenshot the access-denied message.

Document. A note called LOOP: facts, job sentence, open unknowns, next check-in at 2:00.

Adapt. Help desk: access by 1:30 if Elena clicks. Drive search finds a 2024 one-pager in a buried folder. Definition: "external vendors over $10k, fiscal Q, excluding intercompany." She now has a picture to copy and a definition to confirm.

Reflect. The fear was "I don't know the system." The first blocker was access plus a missing example. Next loop starts at 1:30 inside the tool, with a timer.

That is a resourceful human on day three. Not a hero. A person with a sequence.

Practice

Practice

Do this now. Twelve minutes. Use a real unknown from this week — a tool, a form, a trip, a bill, a task you have been circling.

  1. Write the seven words down the page.
  2. Fill See with only pointable facts. Force a second column for stories.
  3. Write the job in one sentence, and one sentence of what is not the job.
  4. Put one item in each Find bucket: person, artifact, system.
  5. Circle one 15-minute action.
  6. If you can do it now, do it. If not, write the first five minutes of it (the email, the ticket, the search query).
  7. Write one Adapt line as a prediction: "If this action fails, I will learn ___ and my next step will be ___."

You are not journaling how you feel about being new. You are installing a circuit.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — unfamiliar system. Situation: a report from a tool you have never used, on a deadline that does not care. What most people do: click around, then send "any guidance?" Why it fails: the owner hears "I have not thought yet." Resourceful response: See the error and the ask. Think the actual output. Find access path + previous file + named human. Choose the blocker first. Words to use: "I can do X by [time] if I get [specific access or definition]. Here is the skeleton either way." Words to avoid: "I'm not sure how things work here," "Would love to pick your brain." Tool: Figure-It-Out Loop. AI assist: the prompt above, then verify every noun. What could go wrong: you over-promise the skeleton. Promise only what a blank page plus a definition can hold.

Travel — missed connection. Situation: foreign terminal, low battery, unreadable board. What most people do: follow a crowd, drain the phone, argue fairness with the first official. Why it fails: fairness is not the job. A later seat or a protected overnight is the job. Resourceful response: photograph the pass and every sign. Identify the right desk. Ask one specific question: "Same ticket, missed connection — rebook today or hotel voucher?" Words to use: ticket number, original flight number, "protected connection" if it was the airline's miss. Words to avoid: a speech about how this always happens to you. Tool: Loop, Fastest version. AI assist: if you have battery, paste the public delay notice and ask for the airline's official rebooking path — then confirm at the desk. Do not take a model's reading of passenger rights as fact. What could go wrong: the wrong queue. Spend thirty seconds confirming the logo and the purpose of the desk.

Digital — a hostile error message. Situation: a tool throws a code you have never seen. Jonah says "just Google it." What most people do: paste the code, click the first forum post, run a command they do not understand. Why it fails: the post is for a different version, or a different product with the same words. Resourceful response: copy the exact string, version, and what you were doing. Think — permissions, missing file, or known outage? Find official docs and the status page first. Choose one change you can reverse. Words to use, in a ticket: exact error, version, timestamp, what you already tried. Words to avoid: "It's broken," "This is urgent" with no payload. Tool: Loop, then Search / Compare / Verify in 3.2. What could go wrong: you "fix" it by disabling a safety control. If the action is irreversible, stop and ask.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Same Thursday. Worse.

Elena's ping includes: "Do not make this a project. The last new person turned a one-pager into a three-day archaeology dig." Jonah, off mute, laughs. You still have no access. Friday's Room includes a finance lead who enjoys catching sloppy numbers.

The loop still starts with See. Then shrink Think until the job is almost insultingly small: a one-page picture, source labeled, unknowns labeled, no theatre.

Your 9:52 note gets tighter: "One-pager by Friday 9:00. I need finance access today and a yes/no: match the 2024 format I found in [folder], fiscal Q3, external vendors. If access slips past 2:00 I will send a page with the last known extract and a red box where the live numbers belong. I will not turn this into a process review."

You have used the loop in public without giving a talk about resourcefulness. You have also pre-committed to a Minimum Useful Version — you will meet that tool in 3.7 — so Elena can trust that "figure it out" does not mean "disappear into the system."

If your hands are hot, that is Level 1, still. Pause. Then the sequence. The Room can survive a new person. It cannot survive a confident blank.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Where do you usually skip: See, Choose, or Reflect?
  • What story do you tell instead of running the loop ("I should already know," "I'll look stupid if I ask")?
  • When did wandering recently create a second problem?
  • What is the actual job in the thing you are avoiding this week — in one sentence?
  • Who already knows a piece of it, and why have you not asked them a specific question?
Remember

Remember This

When you do not know, you still have a sequence. Run it before you perform.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Two cousins, and why this course does not worship either.

Boyd's OODA was built for a fight where the opponent is also cycling. The advantage was getting inside the other person's loop. In an airport or a new job there is often no opponent — only entropy, missing definitions, and your own pride. If you import "speed at all costs" you will skip Verify and ship. Cycle fast after you can point at facts.

Simon's scissors is the other cousin: the mind is one blade, the structure of the environment is the other. You cut with both. That is why Find exists as its own step. A clever person in a barren environment still fails. A merely competent person who finds the previous one-pager and the help-desk path looks like a genius by Friday. Resourcefulness is partly navigation of other people's leftover structure — files, tools, humans, official pages.

A note on "growth mindset," because it will try to sit down at this table. Carol Dweck's research program is real and also more contested in replication and school-implementation studies than posters admit. The operational claim this lesson needs is smaller: treat the current gap as information, not as a verdict on your identity. You do not need a slogan. You need a loop that produces a next step.

Harder variation: run the loop on a problem you have been calling "not my job." See what is actually there. Think whether a Minimum Useful Version would help the Room. You may still choose not to pick it up. Choosing is different from never looking.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24 hours, run one full loop on a live unknown — not a hypothetical.

The unknown must be real (a tool, a form, a trip, a request, a bill). Write the seven steps, even if ugly. Take one Act that the world can answer.

Afterward, four lines:

  1. The job, in one sentence
  2. The first fact you had been skipping
  3. The action you took
  4. What the world said back

If you cannot fill line 4, you did not Act. Run it again. This is Level 3, not a museum of frameworks.
