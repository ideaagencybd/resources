Deep Dive 3: Learning How to Learn

770 words

The meta-skill that makes every other skill cheaper.

Level 3.1 gave you the 90-minute skill sprint. This section covers the full underlying science of learning efficiently — how memory works, how expertise develops, and how to design your own learning to compound over years.

The three principles of durable learning

From decades of research — synthesized in books like Peter Brown's Make It Stick and Barbara Oakley's A Mind for Numbers — three principles keep appearing as more important than others:

  • ACTIVE RECALL is more effective than passive re-reading. Testing yourself on material, even without reviewing it first, produces dramatically better retention.
  • SPACED REPETITION beats massed practice. Learning one hour a day for six days beats six hours in one day, for retention across weeks.
  • INTERLEAVING beats blocked practice. Mixing different but related topics in a study session is harder in the moment and better for long-term learning.

The Feynman Technique, explained

Richard Feynman's method for testing understanding, which has become one of the most useful learning tools ever named:

Note

THE TOOL — Four steps

Note

1. Pick a concept. 2. Explain it as if to a child — or to someone with no background in the topic. 3. Wherever you stumble, get stuck, or reach for jargon, mark that as a gap. 4. Return to the source material, fill the gap, and explain again. Iterate until you can explain it fluently without jargon. Then you understand it.

Deliberate practice vs. casual practice

K. Anders Ericsson's research on expertise established a distinction most people ignore: casual practice (doing the thing) does not build expertise. Deliberate practice (doing the thing at the edge of your current ability, with immediate feedback, focused on specific weaknesses) does.

The implication: 10,000 hours of casual chess playing produces a moderate club player. A few thousand hours of deliberate practice — playing above your level, studying grandmaster games, running specific tactical drills, getting coached — produces a strong player.

Note

THE TOOL — What deliberate practice looks like in professional work

Note

• Not just doing the work. Reviewing the work afterward with a critical eye. • Not just presenting. Presenting to a video camera, watching it, identifying the 30 seconds that were weakest. • Not just writing emails. Rewriting an email five different ways and asking which is clearest. • Not just negotiating. Role-playing with someone who will actually push back. • Not just managing people. Reading feedback on your management style, honestly, and adjusting one specific behavior each month.

The plateau problem

Almost every learner hits plateaus — periods where practice produces no visible improvement. The temptation is to give up. The alternative response — the one that produces breakthroughs — is:

  • Change the practice. Do something at the edge of your current ability, not more of what's now easy.
  • Get feedback from someone above your current level.
  • Break the skill into components and drill the weakest one.
  • Accept that the plateau period may last weeks or months, during which you are actually reorganizing your understanding.

The Josh Waitzkin insight: 'It's not the smartest who succeed. It's the ones who can tolerate the plateau without becoming discouraged.'

Building a learning system for your life

Note

THE TOOL — The annual learning practice

Note

1. Pick 1–3 skills you want to develop meaningfully over the year. 2. For each, define the level you want to reach and what evidence would demonstrate it. 3. Design the practice — deliberate, not casual. How will you get feedback? Who will teach you? 4. Book the time. Weekly, not aspirationally. 5. Track it — not obsessively, but visibly. 6. At year end, honestly assess. Where did you make real progress? Where did you drift? 7. Choose the next year's skills based on what reality taught you.

Note

THE GREAT MINDS COUNCIL — On the craft of learning

Note

Through K. Anders Ericsson's lens: Expertise is built by deliberate practice, not by raw hours. Most of us confuse the two. Through Josh Waitzkin's lens: The Art of Learning: fundamentals should be made unconscious, not skipped. Build the foundation, then techniques compound. Through Barbara Oakley's lens: Focused and diffuse modes both matter. Solving hard problems often requires stepping away and letting the diffuse mode work. Through Peter Brown's lens: Make It Stick: the strategies that FEEL like learning (re-reading, highlighting) are less effective than the ones that feel hard (self-testing, interleaving). Where the Council might be wrong: The rigorous learning frame can become another form of self-optimization. Some learning is meant to be joyful, curiosity-driven, and un-optimized. Both modes are legitimate.

Remember This

“Practice at the edge, with feedback, consistently, on the things that matter. Then be patient.”
