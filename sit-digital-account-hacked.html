Digital, Scams & Reputation

Your account has been taken over

high The Containment Ordersecurityemailaccountsrecovery
1

What is happening

You cannot log in, or you can but there are messages you did not send, a changed recovery email, and a login from a country you have never visited. Your email account is the one that matters most and it may be the one that is gone.

2

What is actually going on

  • Email is the master key. Whoever holds your email can reset almost everything else, so it is the first thing to recover and the first thing to lock down.
  • Attackers move in a predictable order: change the recovery details, set up forwarding rules, then use the account to reach your money and your contacts.
  • Password reuse is how a single old breach becomes six compromised accounts. If one password was shared, treat every account that shared it as compromised.
  • Speed matters more than thoroughness in the first hour. Contain first, tidy later.
3

Do this, in this order

  1. Work from a device you trustIf the compromise might be malware on your laptop, use a different device. Resetting passwords on an infected machine just hands over the new ones.
  2. Recover email first, then moneyEmail, then bank and payment apps, then everything else. Order matters here more than people expect.
  3. Change the password and force sign-out everywhereEvery major service has "sign out of all sessions" or "log out all devices." Changing the password alone often leaves their session alive.
  4. Enable two-factor authentication immediatelyAn authenticator app rather than SMS where possible. This is the step that actually ends the takeover.
  5. Check the quiet settings, not just the passwordForwarding rules, filters that delete alerts, recovery email and phone, connected apps, trusted devices. Attackers hide here and come back later.
  6. Warn your contacts brieflyA one-line message stops your friends and colleagues being defrauded in your name.
4

Words you can borrow

To contacts
"My account was compromised. Ignore anything from me between Tuesday and today, especially anything about money."
To your bank
"My email was taken over. Please put a note on my account and check for any changes to payees or details."
To your employer's IT team
"I think my work account is compromised — here is when I noticed and what I have already changed."
To yourself, as a rule
"Unique password on email. Two-factor on everything that holds money or identity."
5

What makes it worse

  • Clicking links in the "suspicious login" email itself. Go to the site directly.
  • Paying anyone who contacts you offering to recover the account. That is a second scam attached to the first.
  • Reusing a variation of the old password. Attackers try increments first.
  • Deleting the malicious messages before screenshotting them. You may need them.
  • Assuming it is over once you can log in. Unremoved forwarding rules are how people get hit twice.
6

Once the pressure drops

  • Move to a password manager and rotate everything that shared the compromised password.
  • Check your email address against a breach-notification service and change whatever it names.
  • Print or write down recovery codes and store them offline. Losing these is the most common second disaster.
  • Review financial statements for the previous three months, not just this week. Quiet small charges are the usual pattern.
A note on this library

These are general principles written to be useful under pressure, not legal, medical, or financial advice. Laws and emergency procedures differ by country. If life is at risk, contact your emergency services first and read this afterwards.
