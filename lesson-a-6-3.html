The Master Prompt

One framework instead of a prompt museum

Skill

In one line
Brief it like an intern. Not like a vending machine.
The idea

The Hook

There are people who collect prompts the way other people collect recipes they will never cook.

Forty formulas. A thread of "magic words." They type "act as a world-class" and wait to be knighted by a paragraph.

It does not work, except when it accidentally does, which is how superstition survives.

You already know how to brief a human. You would not walk up to a sharp intern, say "make this better," and then be shocked at the mush. You would say what this is, what you need, what is off-limits, what good looks like, and how you will check.

A model is not an intern. It is intern-shaped. That is enough. One framework. Not a museum.

The room

The Scenario

Jonah is sharing his screen. A prompt library with names like "Killer Email v7."

Elena needs a one-page update for a customer already angry about a delay. Jonah pastes the thread into v7. The model returns a warm letter with "I hope this message finds you well" and a promise Jonah does not have authority to make: next Friday, guaranteed.

Jonah grins. "See? That's why you don't cheap out on prompts."

Elena looks at you. "We cannot send that. Also we cannot spend the afternoon prompt-shopping. Get a page we can stand behind."

The customer is waiting. What do you type?

Your move

Your Move

Common move. Steal Jonah's output and delete the illegal promise. You still ship a letter that sounds like a template and hides the facts.

Better move. "Write a professional update about a delay." Better than v7. Still a vending-machine ask. You will get generic professionalism, which is a style, not a brief.

Expert move. Brief the model the way you would brief a person: context, goal, constraints, a scrap of example, the output shape, and how to show its work so you can verify.

Resourceful move. The expert move, plus two extra asks: "Give me two alternatives" and "What do you not know? Challenge my assumptions." Then you pick the page.

Why the common move fails: Jonah's v7 was built to sound warm. Elena's job is to be accurate under heat. Wrong job, fluent output.

What can go wrong with the expert move: a prompt so long the model loses the plot. Six parts, not a novella.

The idea

The Big Idea

One briefing framework beats forty spells. Call it CGCEOV: Context, Goal, Constraints, Examples, Output, Verification. When the product names change, you will still know how to brief.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

A model continues the pattern you set. Vague pattern, average of the internet. Specific pattern — situation, job, off-limits, voice, shape, how we check — and it has something to continue that is yours.

CONTEXT. Who you are, who they are, what happened. Audience lives here. A page for an angry customer is not a page for a board.

GOAL. One sentence. A verb. "Produce a one-page update that states the delay, the cause we actually know, and the next date we can defend."

CONSTRAINTS. Must / must not. No new promises. No blame of a named junior. Length. Tone: direct, not warm-fog. Privacy. Authority: what you cannot promise.

EXAMPLES. "Not: I hope this finds you well. More like: *You were promised March 12. We missed it.*" Direction, not a perfect sample.

OUTPUT. Format. Word limit. Alternatives: "Two versions: one BLUF blunt, one slightly softer, same facts."

VERIFICATION. "List every factual claim and where it came from. Mark anything you inferred. What do you not know? Challenge my assumptions."

That last pair is the difference between a vending machine and a collaborator. Models will not volunteer ignorance unless you make ignorance a required section.

What's changing. Projects, memory, custom GPTs/Gems/Grok-equivalents will offer to save your brief. Use them as storage for CGCEOV, not as a replacement. Saved magic is still magic. A saved brief is a tool.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Master Prompt (CGCEOV)

Fill the brackets. Delete empty lines rather than leaving "N/A" theater.

  1. CONTEXT. Situation, audience, numbered facts.
  2. GOAL. One sentence. Two goals = two prompts.
  3. CONSTRAINTS. Must / must not. Privacy. Tone. Authority.
  4. EXAMPLES. Two to six lines of like-this / not-like-this.
  5. OUTPUT. Shape, length, number of options. Ask for alternatives on purpose.
  6. VERIFICATION. Required sections: UNCERTAIN, ASSUMPTIONS TO CHALLENGE, CLAIMS TO CHECK.

If you remember nothing else: audience, format, alternatives, "what do you not know?", "challenge my assumptions."

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

Ninety seconds:

"Audience: [who]. Goal: [one sentence]. Constraints: [three bullets]. Output: [format + two alternatives]. Then: what you don't know, and one assumption of mine that might be wrong. Don't invent facts."

Skip Examples only when you are late. Skip Verification never.

The hack

The Hack

Keep facts outside the instruction. Raw material in a marked SOURCE block. Instructions above it. When you iterate, change the instructions, not the facts.

If you mix facts and wishes — "we're basically on track except the delay which isn't really our fault" — the model will help you launder the story. Facts vs Stories still applies to the prompt.

AI move

The AI Move

Use AI for: turning a messy ask into a CGCEOV draft you then edit; generating two output shapes so you can pick; "If you were confused by this brief, what would you ask?"

Do not use AI for: inventing the facts in Context; impersonating a licensed professional and treating the result as licensed work.

Prompt (copy, then fill):

CONTEXT
I am: [role]
Audience: [who they are, what they care about]
Situation: [short]
Facts I believe are true:
1.
2.
3.
Source:
"""
[paste only what is allowed]
"""

GOAL
[one sentence, a verb and an object]

CONSTRAINTS
Must: [length, tone, BLUF]
Must not: [promises, admissions, secrets, invented numbers]
Authority limit: I cannot [promise a date / spend money / speak for legal]

EXAMPLES
Like this: "[two sentences]"
Not like this: "[the voice you refuse]"

OUTPUT
Format: [email / 200 words / table]
2 alternatives, same facts, differ in [tone / risk]. Label A and B.

VERIFICATION
UNCERTAIN / INFERRED / CHALLENGE (two assumptions) / CHECK (every number, name, date)
Do not invent citations. If you lack a fact, say so.

What to verify: everything in CHECK. Whether alternative A quietly reintroduced a promise you forbade.

Do not paste: credentials, health, HR, customer personal data, postcard-test failures.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "brief a language model so the first useful draft appears."

Category: any general assistant. Current examples will shuffle: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, Copilot, and the versions nested inside document apps. The framework is the same.

When not to use a long prompt: "Make this list alphabetical" does not need CGCEOV.

What's changing. Vendors will sell prompt marketplaces and "expert" custom bots. Treat them as someone else's CGCEOV, possibly for a different job, possibly stale. Steal the useful constraints. Rewrite the Goal so it is yours.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

People stuff the Goal with adjectives. "You are an award-winning, world-class…" is incense. It does not tell the model who the audience is or what would be a fireable error.

Second mistake: one giant prompt that asks for research, a decision, an email, and a poem. Split jobs. The Improve step from 6.2 is a second brief, not a longer first one.

Recovery: if the output is mush, do not add magic words. Delete half the prompt and specify audience and format. If it is still mush, your Context facts were a story.

Defense

The Defense

CGCEOV fails when you do not have the facts (go get the date); when the model cannot see the file you think you attached (ask it to quote the line it is using); when you need originality that is actually yours — a vow, an apology, a joke that only works because of a private history. Briefing a model to fake intimacy is how you send something you cannot stand behind.

A good prompt will not make a weak model strong, and it will not make a strong model honest. It will make whatever you have usable. Verification is a map. You still walk it. 6.4 is the walk.

Council

The Masters' Table

Grace Hopper. The machine does what you specified, including the gap you did not know was a gap. Precision is kindness to the future you who has to debug the output.

Richard Feynman. If you cannot explain the job simply, you do not understand the job. A prompt full of fog is a confession.

Claude Shannon. Extra words are not extra information. Cut until the remaining bits matter.

George Orwell. Through "Politics and the English Language" (1946): vague language is often a way to hide. If your prompt cannot say "we missed the date," the output will hide it too.

Bill Gates. A reusable brief multiplies. Write CGCEOV once for the three jobs you actually do every week.

Synthesis. Brief, specific, and slightly rude to your own assumptions beats eloquent and wrong.

Where the council could be wrong. Some creative work benefits from a loose ask. If the job is exploration, say so in Goal — "generate options I will not ship" — and keep Verification so exploration does not smuggle in fake facts.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Calm. You do not mock Jonah's library. Clean compose window.

Observe. Angry customer. Real delay. Jonah's draft contains an unauthorized date.

Define. A one-page update that admits the miss, states what we know, and does not mint a promise.

Verify. What date can you defend? If you do not know, the prompt must not invent one.

Prioritize. Facts and authority limits first. Tone second.

Find. The original promise, the actual status, who can approve a date. Not v7.

Options. Send Jonah's with edits. Write it yourself. Or CGCEOV, then edit.

Risks. A new invented date. A grovel. A page that sounds like a model.

Choose. CGCEOV, then your edit, then Elena's eyes.

Communicate. To Elena: "Two versions, same facts, no date we don't have. I'll mark the hole."

Act. Numbered facts only. Constraint: no date unless it is in the facts. Two alternatives. Verification on.

Document. The prompt you used, so the next delay does not require a séance.

Adapt. If both versions still grovel, your Example was too soft. Fix the Example, do not keep regenerating.

Reflect. Did you brief a job, or ask for magic?

Practice

Practice

Twelve minutes.

  1. Find a prompt you have actually used.
  2. Score it: which of the six parts are present?
  3. Rewrite it as CGCEOV. Under 250 words of instruction, plus source material.
  4. Run both if you can.
  5. Compare: which output could you send after a Verify pass?
  6. Add the two lines if missing: "What do you not know?" and "Challenge my assumptions."
Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the angry update. Situation: a miss, a customer, a clock. What most people do: "write a professional email." Why it fails: "professional" is a costume. Resourceful response: CGCEOV with authority limits and two alternatives. Words to use: "You were promised X. We missed it." Words to avoid: "I hope this finds you well," "unforeseen," "rest assured." Tool: Master Prompt + BLUF (4.2). What could go wrong: the model invents a recovery date. You delete it.

Family — the logistics text. Situation: three households, a weekend plan, a model that will "just draft it." What most people do: one vague ask, then a message that misses a dietary constraint or a bedtime. Why it fails: Context was incomplete. Resourceful response: facts numbered, constraints explicit, output as a checklist people can correct. What could go wrong: pasting a medical or school detail into a consumer tool. Strip it.

Digital — the reusable brief. Situation: you do the same job every week — meeting notes to actions, or a weekly status. What most people do: re-explain from scratch, worse each time. Why it fails: you never built the asset. Resourceful response: save a CGCEOV template with empty Context and a stable Goal/Constraints/Output/Verification. What could go wrong: a saved brief that goes stale when the audience changes. Review quarterly.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Jonah's guaranteed-Friday email already went. The customer replied, "Confirmed for Friday." It is now a promise in writing, made by a model, sent by a human.

CGCEOV will not unsend it. The job has changed: repair.

Context: unauthorized date, now echoed. Goal: a correction that does not call the customer a fool and does not pretend Friday is real if it is not. Constraints: no blame-the-tool; no second fake date. Output: a short note. Verification: every sentence checkable.

You may still use a model to draft the correction. You may not use it to hide. If Friday is impossible, the resourceful page says so, offers the next defensible date or a time you will have one, and puts a human on the phone if the relationship needs a voice.

The library of spells is how you got here. The framework is how you leave.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Which of the six parts do you skip because it feels slow?
  • When you type "act as," what are you avoiding specifying?
  • What is one job you do every week that deserves a saved brief?
  • If the model challenged your assumptions, would you want to hear it?
  • Who is the audience you keep forgetting to name?
Remember

Remember This

If you cannot brief a sharp intern in six parts, you are not prompting. You are wishing.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Prompt tricks have a half-life. "Let's think step by step" helped on some older models and some tasks; then it became a meme; then models started doing more of that work internally; then people kept pasting it as a lucky charm. Chain-of-thought, role play, few-shot examples — each is a technique that sometimes serves a part of CGCEOV. Techniques expire. Briefing does not.

Few-shot examples are the technique worth keeping the longest. Models are excellent at continuing a pattern you demonstrate. Two short examples of the output you want will often beat a paragraph of adjectives. That is why Examples is its own letter.

Hopper's compilers translated human-readable instructions into machine action. You are doing a crude version of the same job. The quality of the translation depends on whether the human-readable part was actually a specification.

Harder variation: take a bad public prompt and rewrite it as CGCEOV without adding new facts. Notice how much of the original was costume. This course's later library (9.7) is this framework applied to recurring jobs, not a second religion.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24 hours, write one Master Prompt for a job you will actually run this week.

Fill all six parts. Run it once. Keep the UNCERTAIN section. Check one claim that looks obviously right. Save it in a note called MASTER, not a graveyard of v7.
