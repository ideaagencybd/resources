Emergency & Crisis

You are in or witness a car crash

critical The First Ninety Secondsroadtraumacritical
1

What is happening

The noise stops and there is a strange quiet. Glass, a horn stuck on, an engine still running, someone shouting. Your hands are not fully yours yet.

2

What is actually going on

  • The second collision is the one that kills responders and survivors — traffic that has not yet realised what is ahead. Scene safety comes before care, always, without exception.
  • Adrenaline masks your own injuries completely for twenty minutes or more. Feeling fine is not clinical information about your spine or your organs.
  • What you say in the first minutes gets recorded and used later by insurers and, sometimes, courts. Apologising reflexively can be treated as accepting liability.
3

Do this, in this order

  1. Make the scene safe firstHazard lights, engine off, handbrake. Get people off the carriageway and behind a barrier if there is one.
  2. Call emergency services with a precise locationMotorway marker post, junction number, landmark. Say how many vehicles and roughly how many people.
  3. Do not move injured people unless there is fire or immediate dangerSpinal injury is invisible and moving badly can convert a recoverable injury into a permanent one.
  4. Deal with heavy bleeding immediatelyFirm direct pressure with whatever fabric is available, and keep it there. This is the one intervention that beats waiting.
  5. Talk to anyone conscious and trappedDo not leave them alone. Reassurance measurably reduces shock, and you are doing something real by staying.
  6. Exchange details, factually, without discussing faultName, insurer, registration, photographs of positions and damage before vehicles move.
4

Words you can borrow

To emergency services
"M4 westbound, just past junction 12, marker 118. Two cars, one on its side. Three people, one not moving."
To a trapped person
"I'm staying with you. Help is coming. Don't try to move — just keep looking at me."
On details
"Let's just swap details and let the insurers work out the rest." Never "I'm so sorry, it was my fault."
5

What makes it worse

  • Standing on the carriageway to direct traffic or take photos.
  • Removing a motorcycle helmet.
  • Admitting fault, or arguing it, at the scene.
  • Declining assessment because you feel fine. Internal bleeding and concussion both present late.
  • Driving home in shock. Your reaction time is not what you think it is.
6

Once the pressure drops

  • Get medically assessed the same day, even without visible injury, and get it documented. Late-presenting injuries with no record are a nightmare to claim for.
  • Write your own account within 24 hours while sequence memory is intact.
  • Report to your insurer promptly regardless of fault or intention to claim — late notification voids cover.
  • Expect intrusive replays and jumpiness in traffic for a week or two. If it persists beyond a month, that is worth treating.
A note on this library

These are general principles written to be useful under pressure, not legal, medical, or financial advice. Laws and emergency procedures differ by country. If life is at risk, contact your emergency services first and read this afterwards.
