Mastery · 6.1

Capability Over Brand

Tools change. Jobs-to-be-done do not.

You stop marrying a vendor

The idea

The Hook

The tool you are loyal to will be embarrassing in three years.

Not because you were stupid. Because the market is weather. A name that felt like the future in 2023 is a punchline in a 2026 meeting, and the name that feels like the future this week will be a migration project by 2029. The people who look foolish are not the ones who used the tool. They are the ones who built their identity around it.

You have seen the type. They do not say "I need a first pass from messy notes." They say the product name as if it were a personality. When the vendor raises prices, sunsets a feature, or gets acquired, they experience it as a betrayal instead of as a forecast.

A resourceful human does not marry software. They hire a capability for a job, keep the receipt, and stay able to leave.

This lesson is the floor of the whole level. Models, prompts, sheets, security, automation — all of it is downstream of whether you can tell a job from a logo.

The room

The Scenario

Thursday, 11:06 a.m. Elena's standing meeting. The slide is a vendor comparison with logos large enough to be felt.

Jonah, who has lived in one assistant for a year, does not wait. "We're already on it. Switching is wasted motion. The team knows the interface."

Finance, camera off: "Legal wants a different one. Data residency. They sent a two-page no."

Elena looks at you. "I need a recommendation by Monday I can defend upstairs. Not a fan letter."

Jonah's jaw does the thing. "If we split tools, nobody will know how to work with anyone."

Three constraints, one clock, a room arguing names. What do you actually decide?

Your move

Your Move

Name the job in one sentence as if the logos had been removed.

Common move. Pick a side. You get a decision and a faction. The losing side waits for the tool to fail.

Better move. Ask for a trial of both. Trials are useful. A trial without a job is two more logins and a Monday that still has no recommendation.

Expert move. Strip the logos. Write the job, the inputs, the outputs, the failure mode, the privacy constraint, the lock-in, and the skill that survives a vendor change. Recommend a capability, with a current example, and a way out.

Resourceful move. Do the expert move out loud, in five minutes, so Elena can see the method. The method is the asset. The pick is this quarter's instance of it.

Why the common move fails: brand loyalty photographs as competence. When the contract or the threat model changes, the loyalist has to pretend the world did not move.

What can go wrong with the expert move: a framework longer than the meeting looks like hiding. Pretending all tools are identical is also nonsense. Capabilities are not equal. That is why you test them against a job.

The idea

The Big Idea

Capability over brand. The job is stable. The wrapper is not.

"Draft a first pass from messy notes" is a job. "Summarize a packet without inventing numbers" is a job. Product names that currently perform those jobs are examples. Examples expire. Jobs do not.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Clayton Christensen's "jobs to be done" framing is the adult version. People do not buy a drill because they love drills. They hire a hole. When a better way to make the hole appears, the loyal drill owner suffers.

Software is the same, only faster. A chat model, a sheet, a password manager, an automation platform — each is hired to do a job under constraints. The constraints are the real spec: what you may paste, who may see the output, what happens when it is wrong, how hard it is to leave.

Shannon's lens: information is not the brand. It is a difference that can be moved with an error rate you can live with. Turing's: a machine is what it does. Hopper's: if you cannot describe the procedure without the product name, you have a dependency, not a skill.

What's changing. As of 2026, public examples in the "general assistant" category include ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, Copilot, and a rotating cast of open-weight and search-backed tools. Rankings shuffle. Agents appear, disappoint, appear again. None of that is the point. You can evaluate the next name in five minutes because you already know the job.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Job Test

Five minutes. One page. Seven questions. Use it before you adopt, recommend, or defend any tool.

  1. What job? One sentence. A verb and an object. "Turn interview notes into a one-page brief a VP will actually read." Not "use AI."
  2. What inputs? Text, a table, a recording, a folder of PDFs. If you cannot name them, you are shopping.
  3. What outputs? What does "done" look like? Format and audience belong here.
  4. What failure mode? Silent wrong is worse than a loud crash. Invented citations are worse than a blank.
  5. What privacy? What must never go in — health, money, credentials, other people's unpublished work, anything that would shame you on a postcard. The data path is part of the spec.
  6. What lock-in? If this vendor vanished on Friday, what would you lose? Cheap lock-in is a file you can export. Expensive lock-in is a way of working that only exists inside one product.
  7. What transferable skill? After six months, what can you still do if you switch? Briefing a model, cleaning a table, reading a chart. Clicking one vendor's magic button does not travel.

Then pick a current example that survives the seven. Then write one line called exit: how you leave.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

Ninety seconds, someone asking "which one?":

"What's the job, what goes in, what must come out, how it fails, what we will not paste, how we leave, and what skill we keep. Then we pick a current example. Not a religion."

If they cannot answer the job, you do not have a tools problem. You have a thinking problem. Facts vs Stories. A brand will not save an unstated job.

The hack

The Hack

A one-line capability map: Job. Current example. Exit.

Update it when a tool earns a place or loses one, not because a keynote was exciting. Most people add a tool every time they feel behind. Resourceful people replace. One in, one out.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is a good intern for running the Job Test. It is a bad oracle for which logo to love.

Use AI for: turning a messy "we need a tool" talk into seven answers; listing failure modes; comparing two options against your job.

Do not use AI for: a purchase you will not stand behind; privacy judgments about data you have not looked at; pretending a model knows a vendor's current contract.

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Context: Evaluate a tool for real work, not opinions.
Job: [one sentence]
Inputs: [what actually goes in]
Outputs: [what "done" looks like, audience, format]
Constraints: privacy ([never paste]); budget; hours I will spend learning; who else must use this.
Candidates as examples, not destiny: [two or three, or "propose categories first"]

Goal: Job Test table — fit, inputs, outputs, failure mode, privacy as publicly documented, lock-in, transferable skill, one-line exit.
Do not crown a winner in marketing language. If you do not know a data policy, say so.
Output: table + recommended category + current example + strongest reason to wait.
Verification: what I must check on the vendor's own site or with legal.

What to verify: pricing, data-use policy, export format, whether the mentioned feature still exists. Models lag.

Do not paste: contracts, customer data, API keys, a colleague's private notes.

When not to use a new tool: when the job is already done by something you can export from. A boring sheet that answers the question beats a dazzling assistant that cannot.

What's changing. New names, bundled agents, prices that jump after you depend on them. The Job Test does not expire.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

People confuse familiarity with fit. Jonah is not wrong that switching has a cost. He is wrong that the cost of staying equals the value of staying. Familiarity is how lock-in disguises itself as prudence.

The other version: collecting tools because a feed said the old ones are dead. That is brand-over-capability with worse manners.

Recovery: if you already married a vendor, write the Job Test now, including the exit. The next time the deal changes, you will have a plan instead of a personality crisis.

Defense

The Defense

The Job Test fails when the job is fake ("we need to be an AI company" is a slogan — refuse it); when you are not allowed to leave (then learn the transferable skill inside the mandated tool); when the next hour really does need whatever is open (run the test after); when you use "capability over brand" to sneer at people who have a favorite. Having a favorite is fine. Being unable to work without it is not.

This will not make you a technologist. It will make you a person who can walk into a tool argument and still see the work.

Council

The Masters' Table

Claude Shannon. Ignore the cabinet. Watch what is encoded and what is lost. A tool that cannot preserve true vs plausible is a noisy channel, however pretty.

Alan Turing. Judge the machine by the behavior. Similar inputs, similar outputs, checkable error rate: same category. You may have a favorite. Not a theology.

Grace Hopper. If you cannot describe the procedure without the product, you have not learned it. Write the steps as if the vendor had already disappeared.

Clayton Christensen. Hire the tool for the job. Fire it when the job changes. Loyalty is for people.

Bill Gates. Through the multiplication lens: a tool is worth it if it multiplies a skill you already have. The sheet that makes you faster at checking numbers is a multiplier. The assistant you never verify multiplies your mistakes.

Synthesis. Names are temporary. Jobs are not. A skill that cannot survive a vendor change was never yours.

Where the council could be wrong. Some tools become infrastructure — a language, a file format, a protocol. Switching costs can be honest. The Job Test can be used in bad faith to block any commitment. Commitment is allowed. Theology is not.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 11:06.

Calm. You do not pick a logo to end the tension. Blank page on the shared screen.

Observe. Jonah is protecting switching cost and status. Finance is protecting a constraint that can kill the deal. Elena needs a sentence upstairs.

Define. This meeting is not "crown a vendor." It is "name the job and the constraints so Monday is possible."

Verify. You do not yet know the residency requirement in detail, or what the team pastes. So you will not pretend to.

Prioritize. Legal and privacy first. Job second. Familiarity third — real, not first.

Find. Legal's two-page no. One user who is not Jonah. The export format of what you already have.

Options. Jonah's. Legal's. A split. Or: category plus constraints plus a current example plus an exit.

Risks. A split without rules is chaos. A pick legal will not sign is theater. "More research" is how Monday arrives empty.

Choose. Category, constraints, current example, exit.

Communicate. "The job is first-pass drafts on non-sensitive work, and a second path for customer data. Legal is a gate, not a preference. The transferable skill is the briefing method in 6.3, not the chat theme. One-page Job Test by 4:00."

Act. Seven questions. Read the two-page no. No holy war on the group chat.

Document. Job, inputs, outputs, failure, privacy, lock-in, skill, current example, exit.

Adapt. If legal kills every cloud assistant, the answer may be a sanctioned instance — or "this job does not get a model." Valid output of the test.

Reflect. Did you argue a name, or specify a job?

Practice

Practice

Twelve minutes.

  1. Pick one tool you used in the last 48 hours.
  2. Write the seven answers. One sentence each.
  3. Circle the failure mode. "It might be wrong" is too vague. Wrong how?
  4. Write the exit. If you cannot, you are more locked in than you think.
  5. Name the transferable skill in five words.
  6. Cross out the product name in your first sentence. If the sentence dies, you described a brand, not a job.
Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the mandated stack. Situation: the company picks one assistant. A colleague insists the other is "the best." What most people do: argue benchmarks like sports. Why it fails: benchmarks are not your job, and you do not control procurement. Resourceful response: Job Test inside the mandated tools. Learn the transferable parts. Keep a personal exit for your notes, not for company secrets. Words to use: "What's the job, and what are we not allowed to paste?" Words to avoid: "Everyone serious is on X." Tool: The Job Test. AI assist: the prompt above, mandated tool as a constraint. What could go wrong: using a personal tool for company data because you prefer it. That is a leak.

Family — the subscription pile. Situation: four paid apps, overlapping jobs, a card that auto-renews. What most people do: keep all of them "just in case." Why it fails: unused capability is a standing order, not a skill. Resourceful response: one row per subscription. Job, last used, exit. Cancel the ones whose job is already hired. Words to use: "What job is this still doing?" Words to avoid: "It's only twelve dollars." What could go wrong: canceling the one that holds unique files before you export.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Elena: "Upstairs already told a customer we are standardized on Jonah's tool. Finance, find a yes. You — make it coherent by Monday."

The job has changed. It is no longer "pick." It is "tell the truth about a pick already performed in public."

"Then we use that tool for the jobs it is allowed to do, we write the jobs it is not allowed to do, and we bring legal a mitigation, not a vibe. I will not send a brief that says residency is solved if it is not. I will send: job, risk, workaround, what we tell the customer without lying."

If Jonah turns this into a loyalty test, put the seven questions back on the screen. The room can survive an awkward silence. It cannot survive a contract you pretended not to see.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Which tool would embarrass you to lose — love or lock-in?
  • When you recommend a tool, are you describing a job or signaling a tribe?
  • What skill would survive if your favorite product vanished on Friday?
  • Where have you been arguing a name because the job was hard to state?
  • What are you pasting into a tool whose data path you have never read?
Remember

Remember This

Hire the capability. Date the brand. Keep the skill.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

A short history of dying wrappers.

Office suites, photo sites, chat clients, note apps, "the" search box, "the" assistant — each generation produced people who could not describe their work without the product. The ones who stayed resourceful knew what the file was, where the export lived, and what the job had been all along.

Christensen's jobs-to-be-done work was about milkshakes and hospitals before it was a product slide. Steal the question: "What job did I hire this for?"

Shannon's 1948 paper is not a buying guide. It is a reminder that every tool is another place for noise: a format conversion, a model that invents, a sync that drops a row. The failure-mode question is Shannon for people who will never read the paper.

Hopper's public point: machines do what you tell them, including the stupid thing, and a procedure you cannot inspect is a procedure you do not control. Vendor magic is uninspectable procedure with a subscription.

Harder variation: take a tool you love and write the Job Test as if you were recommending against it. Steelman the case for leaving. If you cannot, you are not loyal. You are uninformed.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24 hours, run the Job Test on one tool you pay for or use at work.

Six lines: Job. Failure mode. Privacy (what never goes in). Lock-in. Transferable skill. Exit.

If you cannot write an exit, export one file today so that an exit exists.
