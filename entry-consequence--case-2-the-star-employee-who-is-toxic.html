Case 2 — The Star Employee Who Is Toxic

555 words

The Situation

One of your team members ships more than anyone else. She's genuinely brilliant. She also demeans junior colleagues in meetings, ignores feedback about her tone, and has had two direct reports leave the company citing her. Her latest project is due in three weeks. You've mentioned the pattern to her. She said she'd try. Nothing has changed.

Your Move — Decide Before Reading On

Option A

Let her finish the current project, then deal with it after.

What likely happens:

The two next junior colleagues leave her orbit visibly upset. HR gets involved with someone else on your team. Your peers start questioning whether you can manage. Your star delivers the project — barely — and the team's output the following quarter collapses because you've lost the junior talent she was supposed to develop.

What you gained:

Short-term ship of a major deliverable.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Long-term team damage. Your reputation as a manager who tolerates bad behavior for output. The specific junior colleagues who leave — many of them were more valuable long-term than the star's quarterly velocity.

What you should have noticed:

Toxic star employees are the second-highest-cost mistake a manager makes. Andy Grove wrote about this: high-performing employees who damage the team must be terminated even if it hurts short-term output.

Option B

Have the direct 'change or leave' conversation now, with a specific 60-day runway.

What likely happens:

One of two things: (1) she genuinely changes — sometimes clear ultimatums do produce change, especially with brilliant people whose blind spot has been that no one has been specific with them; (2) she gets defensive and leaves. Either is better than the current state. Your team's culture visibly improves either way — juniors see you took action.

What you gained:

Clarity in the team. Restored culture. Your credibility as a manager willing to have difficult conversations.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Risk to the project she's on. Possible loss of a genuinely productive individual. A hard few weeks of conversation.

What you should have noticed:

The status of your junior team members as they observe this outcome. What they will conclude about you either way.

Option C

Transfer her off your team to another manager who might handle her better.

What likely happens:

You have solved your problem by making it someone else's. Your peer now inherits the toxic dynamic. If they're less experienced than you, the damage is worse. If they're more experienced, they will notice what you did — and remember.

What you gained:

Immediate relief.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

You have not solved the problem. You have moved it. You have also earned a reputation internally as a manager who dumps problems.

What you should have noticed:

Moving a problem is not solving it. Senior colleagues can smell this from a distance.

The Wrap

The management literature on this — from Andy Grove to Kim Scott — is consistent: brilliant toxic employees must be addressed directly, and if they cannot change, they must leave. Not because they are irredeemable, but because keeping them destroys the team's ability to function and damages your credibility as a leader. Every junior colleague watches how you handle this. Their view of what the company values will be permanently shaped.
