The 90-Minute Skill Sprint

Learn the next useful layer, not the whole mountain

24 min30 / 45 / 154,081 words22 sections
Skill
Acquire a working skill on a clock
Tool
30 / 45 / 15
Result
You can become useful at something new this afternoon
In one line
Ninety minutes. One layer. Then you can do a thing.
The idea

The Hook

You do not need a course. You need the next useful layer.

The mountain is "become good at spreadsheets," or "learn the new CRM," or "finally understand how this deploy works." The mountain is how people stay at the trailhead watching videos. Friday does not need a mountain. It needs you to make a pivot table that does not lie, in a meeting that will not wait.

There is a way to do that in ninety minutes that is not magic and is not a personality. Thirty minutes to understand the smallest slice. Forty-five minutes to practice it with your actual materials. Fifteen minutes to explain it, out loud, as if you were teaching a slightly impatient colleague. Then you stop. You are not an expert. You are useful.

This is the Figure-It-Out Loop with a clock on Learn. Search gave you doors. Verification keeps you honest. The sprint is how you stop living in the doorway.

The room

The Scenario

Wednesday, 1:05 p.m. Elena, in a doorway: "The one-pager needs a table that can slice vendors by category. Pivot it. I thought you had that." She is gone before Mara can say that she has always used other people's pivots.

The extract is a flat list: vendor, amount, category, month. Mara has ninety minutes before a working session with Jonah, who will smell a fake. She can watch a 40-minute "Pivot Tables Masterclass" and still have a flat list. She can tell Elena she is "not a spreadsheet person." She can run a sprint on the one slice Friday actually needs.

The clock is real. The mountain is optional.

What does she do with the next ninety minutes?

Your move

Your Move

Write a plan for the ninety minutes. Then look.

Common move. Open a long tutorial. Watch at 1.5x. Pause to take notes that are a transcript. At 2:30, start clicking. At 2:35, Jonah arrives. You have a notebook of someone else's skill.

Better move. Watch a short video, then try. Still on a clean sample file, not the vendor extract. Transfer fails in front of Jonah. Sample files are polite. Real data is rude.

Expert move. Define the slice: "From this extract, a pivot with category as rows, sum of amount, filter to fiscal Q3." Find one official or high-quality demo. Practice on the real file immediately. Explain the result out loud. Stop.

Resourceful move. 30 / 45 / 15 as a named tool, with a written definition of "useful" before you start. Retrieval and a short teach-back, not highlighting. You are not becoming "a spreadsheet person." You are becoming the person who can slice this extract today.

Why the common move fails: consumption feels like progress because the video is competent. Your hands never meet the ugly file. Dunlosky and colleagues, in a 2013 review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, rated rereading and highlighting as low-utility techniques for learning, and practice testing (retrieval) and distributed practice as high-utility. A masterclass you watch once is rereading with better lighting.

What can go wrong with the expert move: you pick a slice that is still too big ("learn pivots and charts and lookups"). Or you practice a wrong method from a bad video. The 30-minute understand block includes a Verify: official docs or a source you would defend, not the first charismatic clicker.

The idea

The Big Idea

You can become useful at a new slice this afternoon if you stop trying to become impressive at the whole field.

The 90-minute sprint is not a trick for becoming an expert. Expertise is a longer, harder story — 3.6 will not let you dodge that. This lesson is the next layer: the one that unblocks Friday. Understand a little. Practice a lot. Explain enough to find out what you faked.

If you only remember one ratio: less than a third of the time on input. The rest is output.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Three blocks. A clock. A slice.

30 — Understand. Not "learn pivots." A sentence: what you will be able to do at minute 90. Then the smallest model that makes that possible. For Mara: a pivot table is a way to group rows and aggregate a number. Rows = category. Values = sum of amount. Filter = fiscal Q3. She needs to know where those three things live in this tool, and one thing that breaks them (blank categories, numbers stored as text).

Where to get the 30: official help for this product, or one short demo you can pause. Not five tabs. Search / Compare / Verify still applies. If two sources disagree on the click path, believe the one that matches your version.

45 — Practice. On the real thing, or as close as you can get without destroying it. Duplicate the extract. Try. Fail. Name what broke. Try again. This is retrieval: you are pulling the move out of yourself, not recognizing it on a screen. The Dunlosky et al. (2013) review found practice testing had high utility across a range of studies and settings they examined; highlighting and rereading did not. You do not need a neuroscience story about "memory palaces" or "learning styles." Those popular claims are weak or worse. You need your hands on the file.

John Hattie and Gregory Donoghue, in a 2016 synthesis in npj Science of Learning, argued that different strategies help at different phases of learning — surface, deep, transfer — and that a strategy's value depends on the phase. Translation for this lesson, kept modest: watching a demo can help you get the surface of a pivot; it will not give you transfer onto a messy extract. Practice on the messy extract is the phase you are in. Do not overclaim their model. Use the clock.

15 — Explain. Feynman's name gets used as a brand here. The useful piece is older than the brand: if you cannot explain the slice simply, you do not have it yet. Mara, out loud, to an empty chair or a voice note: "I put category in rows, amount in values as a sum, fiscal Q3 on the filter. If a vendor has a blank category it falls into (blank). If amount is text, the sum will be wrong. I checked by sampling three vendors against the flat list."

When the explanation snags, that snag is the next. One more short practice on the snag, not a new video.

What this is not: a claim that ninety minutes equals mastery, or that "the brain encodes" anything in a special sprint window. No brain-derived neurotrophic slogans. No learning-style quiz. Time on a well-defined slice, with retrieval and a teach-back, is the whole trick.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: 30 / 45 / 15

Write this at the top of a note before you open a tutorial.

Slice (one sentence): At minute 90 I will be able to ______ on ______.

Not the slice: I will not try to ______ today.

Then:

  1. Minutes 0–5 — Define and protect. Slice on paper. Duplicate the file. Close the extra tabs. Timer on.
  2. Minutes 5–30 — Understand. One official page or one short demo. Write only: the three controls you need, one failure mode, the version. If you cannot find those in 25 minutes, your slice is still a mountain. Cut it.
  3. Minutes 30–75 — Practice. Real (or duplicated) materials. Goal: produce the artifact, then break it on purpose (blank a category, sort the source, refresh) so you see how it fails. Each time it breaks, write five words: what broke.
  4. Minutes 75–90 — Explain. Voice note or a colleague. No slides. If you cannot explain without opening the tutorial, you consumed. Go back to practice on the snag, even if you overrun by ten minutes. The teach-back is the test.
  5. Minute 90 — Stop. Write: what you can do, what you cannot, what the next slice would be. Then use the skill or ask (3.4) for the part that is still dangerous.

Mara's card:

  • Slice: Pivot this extract by category, sum of amount, fiscal Q3 filter, and spot-check three rows.
  • Not the slice: dashboards, Power Query, being "good at Excel."
  • Understand: official pivot help for her version; failure mode = text-numbers and blanks.
  • Practice: duplicate file; build; break; rebuild.
  • Explain: voice note, then a two-line note to Elena: "Category pivot is live; (blank) is 2% of spend I still need to classify."

That last sentence is output. Grove would recognize it. The mountain would have produced a playlist.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

When you do not have ninety minutes — you have twenty-five, and Jonah is walking over:

Shrink the ratio, keep it.

  • 5 understand: what is a pivot, where are the three fields in this UI.
  • 15 practice: build one ugly table on the real extract.
  • 5 explain: say out loud what you did and what might be wrong.

A holding sentence: "I can slice by category in twenty-five minutes at a 'check me' level. I cannot build a dashboard today." That is Confidence Without Delusion from Level 1, applied to a clock.

The hack

The Hack

Practice on the ugly file first, even for five minutes, before the understand block.

You will not know which three minutes of the manual matter until the extract has hurt you. A five-minute pre-fail — "I will try to pivot this once, now, badly" — makes the 30-minute understand block hungry instead of touristy. Then you watch with a question. Then you practice for real.

If the file can destroy something, duplicate it. The hack is not courage. It is a copy.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is a decent explainer and a reckless doer. It will generate pivot instructions for the wrong product, the wrong year, and a clean sample it invented.

Use AI for: restating the slice in one sentence; listing likely failure modes (blanks, text-numbers, duplicate vendors); a Feynman pass: "Ask me questions until my explanation of this pivot breaks"; generating practice checks ("how would I spot-check this table?").

Do not use AI for: "just build it for me" on data you should not paste; trusting a click path you have not seen in your UI; medical, legal, or safety skills — a 90-minute sprint does not make you a nurse, an electrician, or a lawyer.

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Context: I have 90 minutes to become useful at one slice of a skill, not the whole field.
Skill area: [e.g. pivot tables in this spreadsheet tool]
Slice I need by minute 90:
[one sentence, with my actual materials]
Tool / version:
[what I actually have]
What I already tried:
[bullets]

Goal:
1) Confirm or cut my slice so it fits 30 understand / 45 practice / 15 explain.
2) Give me a 30-minute understand plan: what to read/watch (types of source, not invented URLs), three controls I must be able to name, one likely failure mode.
3) Give me a 45-minute practice sequence on MY materials, including a way to break it on purpose and a spot-check.
4) Ask me three questions I must be able to answer out loud at minute 90.

Constraints: No mountain. No "then learn Power Query." Do not invent menu names if you are unsure — say "check this in your version." Do not ask me to paste confidential data.
Output: A timed plan.
Verification: What I must confirm in the official help for my version.

What to verify: every menu name and every claim about what a number means. The model does not have your extract.

Do not paste: the vendor file, personal data, health records, credentials. Describe the columns; keep the rows.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "timer, duplicate, one source, a way to hear yourself explain."

Current examples of the category: a timer that you will not snooze into a tutorial hole; file duplication or a sample environment; official help for this version; a voice-note app for the 15; a second screen for the demo so you are not alt-tabbing yourself out of practice.

When not to use technology: a new "learn anything" app with streaks. Streaks are consumption with a calendar. The sprint is a kitchen timer and a duplicate file.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Spending the 45 on more understanding.

You hit a snag at minute 40, so you open another video "just to clarify." You are now in a masterclass with worse lighting. The snag is information. Write what broke. Try a smaller move. If you are truly stuck on a fact you cannot see, that is an Expert Request (3.4), not a fourth tutorial.

The sibling mistake: skipping Explain because it feels embarrassing. The embarrassment is the test. If you cannot say it, you cannot use it in front of Jonah at 2:35.

Recovery: if the ninety minutes produced notes and no artifact, you consumed. Start a 25-minute Fastest version immediately on the real file. Do not "finish the video first."

Defense

The Defense

30 / 45 / 15 fails when:

  • The skill is not a ninety-minute slice. Suturing, electrical work, a language, a legal opinion. Do not sprint into harm. Find a qualified human. Use the sprint only for the harmless adjacent slice ("fill this form correctly," "find the official page").
  • You have no real materials. Practicing on a toy file and calling it done is the better move from Your Move. Get a duplicate of the real thing or stop claiming usefulness.
  • You are exhausted. State Before Strategy, Level 1. A depleted 90 minutes is how you learn a wrong click path with confidence.
  • You use the sprint to avoid an expert. If Priya can tell you the definition in forty seconds, do not spend 90 minutes inferring it from a tutorial.

Limitation: you will not remember the slice in a month without practice later. Dunlosky et al. rated distributed practice as high utility. Today's sprint makes you useful today. A 20-minute return this week is what keeps it. 3.6 is that argument, expanded.

Council

The Masters' Table

Richard Feynman. Through the teach-back lens: explanation is a test, not a performance. The 15 minutes are where you discover the hole. If you cannot say it simply, the 45 was not long enough, or the slice was too big.

Benjamin Franklin. He taught himself to write by reconstructing essays from memory and comparing — retrieval, not rereading. Through Franklin's lens, the 45 minutes are the reconstruction. The tutorial is the original you should not be staring at while you "practice."

Marie Curie. Laboratory time is not library time. At some point you have to handle the material. The extract is the material. A sprint that never opens it is a seminar.

K. Anders Ericsson. Deliberate practice is not a 90-minute product. Do not steal his name for a hack. Steal one piece: work on a specific, stretchy slice with feedback, not on "spreadsheets" in general. The rest of Ericsson belongs in 3.6, without the 10,000-hour cartoon.

Herbert Simon. Satisficing applied to learning. You need a pivot that is good enough to check, not a professional data-analyst identity. The slice sentence is a satisficing boundary.

Andrew Grove. Output. At minute 90 there is a table or there is a named blocker. There is not a feeling of having studied.

Synthesis. Define a small output, practice on the real thing, use explanation as a test, stop.

Where the council could be wrong. Feynman-explain can become a party trick that hides a fragile skill. Franklin's self-teaching does not transfer to domains where a wrong move injures someone. Grove's output pressure can cut the Understand block so short that you practice a wrong method fast. The 30 minutes are there to keep you from being confidently wrong.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to Wednesday, 1:05.

Calm. Mara does not say "I'm not a spreadsheet person." She also does not open a two-hour masterclass.

Observe. Flat extract. Need: category slice, sums, fiscal Q3. Jonah at 2:35. She has used other people's pivots, never built one on this file.

Define. Slice: a checked pivot on a duplicate of this extract. Not a dashboard.

Verify. Official help for her version, not a random video's menu names.

Prioritize. Hands on the file over notes.

Find resources / people. Official pivot page. A 6-minute demo matching her product. Jonah at 2:35 as a check, not as a tutor of first resort. Priya only if categories themselves are wrong — that is data, not a pivot skill.

Options. Confess incompetence. Consume. Sprint.

Risks. A wrong aggregation in Friday's Room. A sprint that practices the wrong click path. Asking Jonah to build it for her, which trains her to be stuck.

Choose. 30 / 45 / 15 on a duplicate.

Communicate. To Elena, one line if needed: "I'll have a category slice by 2:30 at check-me quality. Not a dashboard." To Jonah, when he arrives: "Here's the pivot. Spot-check these three vendors with me. If the sums fail, I want to know before Friday."

Act. Duplicate. Official page. Build. Break (blank a category, watch (blank) appear). Rebuild. Voice note. Spot-check three rows against the flat list. One mismatch: a text-number. Fix. Recheck.

Document. Two lines in the LOOP note: how to refresh, the text-number trap.

Adapt. Jonah finds that "category" has two spellings of the same word. The pivot is honest and ugly. Mara adds a five-minute clean-up, not a new mountain ("learn Power Query this afternoon").

Reflect. The useful layer was three controls and one failure mode. The masterclass would have been a tour of a country she did not need to visit today.

That is a resourceful human with a clock. Not a prodigy. A person who practiced.

Practice

Practice

Do this now. You may not have ninety minutes. Run a compressed sprint on a tiny slice.

  1. Write a slice sentence you can finish today: "At the end I will be able to ___ on ___."
  2. Write what you will not attempt.
  3. Set a timer: 10 understand / 15 practice / 5 explain, if you are doing this inside the lesson.
  4. Duplicate or sandbox.
  5. Produce the artifact. Break it on purpose. Write what broke.
  6. Explain out loud. Record if you can stand it.
  7. Write: what you can do, what you cannot, next slice.

If you only read this section, you consumed. The lesson is named after a clock for a reason.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — a tool everyone assumes you know. Situation: a manager asks for a feature of a tool you have faked. What most people do: watch a long video, or hand the file to Jonah. Why it fails: Friday still arrives; you are still fake. Resourceful response: slice, 30/45/15, spot-check, name the leftover risk. Words to use: "I can do X at check-me quality by [time]. I cannot do Y today." Words to avoid: "I'm not a [tool] person," "I'll watch a few tutorials." Tool: 30 / 45 / 15. AI assist: the timed-plan prompt; verify menu names in official help. What could go wrong: you sprint the wrong slice. Confirm the output Elena wants in one sentence before minute 0.

Digital — a new app with a job to do. Situation: you must send an invoice, file a form, or edit a video in a tool you opened for the first time. What most people do: browse every menu, then search "how to use [app]." Why it fails: you learn the app's personality, not the job. Resourceful response: slice = the one job. Official quick start. Practice on a dummy. Explain the steps. Then do the real one. Words to use: the job, not the brand. Words to avoid: downloading three extra apps "in case." Tool: sprint + Capability Over Brand (you will get the full version in Level 6). What could go wrong: the app's official docs are a sales page. Then a 6-minute independent demo, version-matched, then practice.

Family — a skill someone else has always done. Situation: a partner or parent always files the insurance, and today it is you. What most people do: call them, stay on the phone for forty minutes, still not learn it. Why it fails: you borrowed a person instead of taking a layer. Resourceful response: Expert Request for the one thing you cannot see (a password, a policy number), then a sprint on the form itself. Teach-back to them: "I'll do it and tell you what I submitted." Words to use: "I want to be able to do this without you on the line. The one thing I need from you is X." Words to avoid: "Just do it, you know how." Tool: 30 / 45 / 15 plus a tight ask. What could go wrong: a form that is actually a legal or medical decision. Sprint the navigation; a professional owns the content.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Jonah, at 2:35, looks at the pivot: "That's not how anyone here does it. You should have used the template. Also your Q3 filter is calendar, not fiscal." Elena is five minutes behind him.

The sprint was real. The slice was slightly wrong. Adapt, do not defend the ninety minutes as a sunk cost.

Mara: "Show me the template and the fiscal field. I can rebuild in fifteen. I already spot-checked the sums against the extract, so the numbers aren't the risk — the filter is." Then she does a compressed 5 / 15 / 5 on the template, not a speech about learning science.

If Jonah is performing competence at her expense, you still want his information. Thank the information. Rebuild. The Bad Room is not a reason to throw away retrieval. It is a reason to cut the slice again.

If you cannot rebuild in fifteen, say so: "I can ship the flat list with a correct fiscal filter tonight, or a pivot tomorrow morning after I match the template. I will not ship a confident wrong Q3." That is the Enough Test, coming in 3.8, and intellectual honesty from Level 2.

  • Where do you skip the explain step because it would expose a hole?
  • Which skill should you not sprint, because harm is real?

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

What the learning research actually supports, and what posters add.

Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan, and Willingham (2013) reviewed ten techniques students commonly use. High utility: practice testing and distributed practice. Moderate: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice. Low: summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading. Their "utility" was a judgment based on evidence of effectiveness, breadth, and ease of use — not a sacred ranking, and not a study of 90-minute workplace sprints. Steal the direction: retrieve and space; do not worship highlighting.

Hattie and Donoghue (2016) proposed that strategy effectiveness depends on the phase of learning. A demo might help you get the surface of a pivot; it will not transfer that surface onto a messy extract by itself. Their wider Visible Learning program is influential and also debated (effect-size meta-analysis has limits; implementation is messier than league tables). Do not cite a decimal as a talisman. Use the phase idea: you are in practice, not in a documentary about practice.

Feynman: the "Feynman Technique" as a four-step internet product is a later packaging. The underlying demand — can you explain it so the hole shows — is solid and sufficient. You do not need a trademark.

If you want a harder variation, run two sprints on consecutive days on the same slice (distributed practice) instead of one heroic 90 minutes and a month of neglect. Day two will be rude about what you actually kept. That rudeness is the education.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 48 hours, run one 90-minute sprint (or the 25-minute Fastest version) on a slice that would make you useful at work, at home, or in a tool you have been faking.

Deliverable: the slice sentence, the artifact, a five-word note on what broke, and a teach-back (voice note or a human).

If you cannot point at the artifact, you consumed. Run the practice block again. The clock is the teacher.
