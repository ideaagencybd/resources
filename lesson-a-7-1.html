The Decision 7

A pocket protocol for almost any choice

26 minThe Decision 73,515 words22 sections
Skill
Run a complete decision without theater
Tool

Result
You can decide when the spreadsheet is incomplete
In one line
Incomplete information is the normal condition. Decide anyway.
The idea

The Hook

The decision has been sitting on the kitchen table for eleven days.

Not a paper. A feeling with a deadline attached. A job offer that expires Friday. A message you have not answered. A purchase tab you keep reopening at 11 p.m. as if the third look will grow a new fact.

You already know more than you did on day one. You do not know enough to feel safe. Those two sentences can both be true. Most of adult life is lived between them.

People wait for the fog to lift. The fog is not weather. It is the condition. Herbert Simon called this bounded rationality: you will not get a complete map, stable preferences, and unlimited time in the same week. You will get a messy room and a clock.

Waiting is sometimes thorough. More often it is a way to avoid being the person who chose.

Seven questions. In order. On paper. Then a choice you can defend when the outcome is unkind.

The room

The Scenario

Thursday, 7:40 p.m. Mara's kitchen. The offer is printed on the table because she did not trust herself with a tab.

The role is a stretch. Thirty percent more cash. A manager she liked in three interviews and cannot actually know. A commute that would eat her evenings. Her current job is not on fire. Elena has said "soon" about a promotion for a year and a half.

Her partner, Sam, is washing the same plate longer than the plate requires.

"I just don't want us betting the next two years on a person you met four times."

Mara: "The money would let us actually save. I'm not getting younger in a 'soon' job."

Sam: "Then tell Elena you have an offer. Make them compete."

Mara: "And if they don't? I look like a person on the way out, and I still have to stay."

The offer expires tomorrow at 5:00. She has a notes app full of pros and cons that have started to argue with each other. She has not written down what she wants. She has written down what she is afraid of.

What does she do before 5:00?

Your move

Your Move

Name the choice you would actually make, and one reason. Out loud if you can.

Common move. Pros and cons until 1 a.m. Decide from whichever voice is loudest in the last hour — Sam's fear, or the fear of staying.

Better move. Talk to two more people. Useful, and not a procedure. Talking can be research. It can also be borrowed certainty.

Expert move. Write the criteria first — money, learning, hours, downside if it fails in a year — then score the offer against the criteria, not against the fear. Give Elena a time-boxed chance to counter.

Resourceful move. Run the Decision 7 on paper, in order, with a clock. Career is not a special species of choice. It is a choice with a larger bill. The same seven questions work on a job, a purchase, and a person.

Why the common move fails: a list without a "what I want" line is a mood diary. It ratifies whichever column you currently inhabit.

What can go wrong with the expert move: criteria can costume a preference you already have. If you weight "manager quality" at 40% because you liked the interviews, you have decorated, not thought. The Decision 7 puts assumptions in their own chair.

The idea

The Big Idea

A decision is not a feeling that hardens. It is a procedure you can run when the feeling will not settle.

The Decision 7 does not make you certain. It makes you finished enough to act, with a record of why, so that later you can tell a good process from a bad outcome. That distinction is the spine of this level. You get it in full in 7.3. You need the procedure first.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Most people skip to question 7. They pick, then invent reasons. Kahneman and Tversky spent a career showing that the fast system is not stupid — it is cheap, and cheap is usually good enough until it is not. Under uncertainty it grabs a story: I would be a coward if I stayed. I would be reckless if I left.

A procedure interrupts the story.

  1. Want is not option. "I want this job" is an option in a want-costume. "I want two years of harder problems and a raise I can defend" is a want.
  2. Know is not assume. "The manager was sharp in interviews" is a fact about interviews. "The manager will protect me when it gets ugly" is an assumption with a lot of weight on it.
  3. Options are rarely two. Binary traps are how anxiety simplifies. There is usually a third: a counter, a delay, a smaller version, a conversation before a resignation.
  4. Wrong is a list, not a vibe. Bruises vs ruin. Taleb's useful caution, without the cult: do not take a risk that can take you out of the game.
  5. Next is where good decisions die. No first action and no review date, and you have a wish.

Simon: you will satisfice — pick something good enough given the limits — because optimizing a life on incomplete data is a fantasy of people who do not have to choose by Friday. Von Neumann and Morgenstern, lightly: if you could assign probabilities and values, you could compute expected utility. You usually cannot. The Decision 7 is what a bounded human does instead of pretending to be a spreadsheet.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Decision 7

Use it for any choice that will still matter in a month, and for smaller ones when you notice you are spinning. Ten to twenty minutes. Full sentences. Not a novel.

  1. What do I want? Outcome, constraints, what "good" looks like in twelve months. If two wants conflict, write the conflict.
  2. What do I know? Facts only. Dates, numbers, quotes, documents. Two-Column Table from Level 2 if facts turn into memoir.
  3. What am I assuming? Load-bearing beliefs. For each: what would I see if this were false, and can I check it before I choose?
  4. What are my options? At least three. Include "do nothing" with its own cost. Invent one option not yet on the table.
  5. What could go wrong? Invert (Munger, from Jacobi). Mark each item bruise or ruin. Ruin gets a veto or a hedge. "I would feel foolish" is a bruise. "We cannot make rent" is ruin.
  6. What happens next? First action, people who need to hear it, a second-order effect you can already see, a review date.
  7. What should I choose? One paragraph of why, including the assumption you are accepting and the risk you are taking. Then stop.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

Ninety seconds — a call on the other line, a checkout page, a "so are you in?":

Want (one sentence). Know (two facts). Options (three, even ugly). Choose, and say the next action out loud.

Skip assume/wrong/next on paper if you must. Do not skip them in your head: What am I pretending is a fact? Can this take me out of the game? What do I do in the next hour?

Holding sentence: "I can give you a real answer at [time]. I will not give you a fake one now." That is the Pause, applied to choice.

The hack

The Hack

Force a third option before you allow yourself to pick. Anxiety loves a coin flip because a coin flip feels like fate. Fate is a way to avoid authorship. If you cannot name a third option in two minutes, you have not thought yet. You have only picked a team.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is a decent clerk for the Decision 7. It is a dangerous oracle for question 7.

Use AI for: a Want/Know/Assume split; a third option; a "what could go wrong" list you will edit; stress-testing your "why" for hidden assumptions.

Do not use AI for: telling you what you should want; inventing facts about a company or a person; being the adult so you do not have to be.

Prompt (copy, then fill):

What to verify: Names, numbers, timelines. Fluency is not knowledge. Level 6 still applies.

Do not paste: offer letters with ID numbers, salary data you do not own, medical or legal details, anyone's private message.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "make the decision visible and time-boxed." A note titled with the date and the choice. A calendar block earlier than the external deadline. A seven-row table. A reminder on the review date.

A spreadsheet with seventeen weighted criteria for a two-way-door choice (next lesson) is theater. Complexity is not rigor.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Using the seven questions to justify a choice already made in the body. Question 1 comes out as the option. Question 3 is empty. Question 4 has two items, one a cartoon. Question 7 is advocacy.

Recovery: rewrite question 1 without naming any option. If you cannot, you are campaigning.

The other mistake: waiting for question 2 to be complete. Completeness is a feeling. "Enough to act" is a judgment. You practiced it in 3.8 with the Enough Test.

Defense

The Defense

The Decision 7 fails when the choice is a values problem dressed as data — then you still clarify, but you need the Inner Boardroom (1.5) and later Level 8. It fails in a crisis that needs movement, not a worksheet: leave, call, stop the bleeding, then write. It fails when you are doing someone else's thinking so you can feel in control. It fails as group theater where nobody may put a real assumption on the table (7.8).

Limitation: this will not remove regret. It will make regret informative.

Council

The Masters' Table

Herbert Simon. Bounded rationality and satisficing. You are not a failed optimizer. You are a human with a clock.

Daniel Kahneman. The fast system drafts a story and calls it a decision. The seven questions are how the slow system gets a vote.

Charlie Munger. Invert. Question 5 is not pessimism. It is the cheapest way to not die of your favorite idea.

John von Neumann (lightly). If you could know payoffs and probabilities, you could play. You usually cannot. Still: name the payoffs you do know.

Warren Buffett. A choice is also what it does to the next ten years of compounding — skill, savings, reputation.

Jeff Bezos (door lens, not a saint). Some of this you can walk back. The Decision 7 is for both; the time you spend should not be. Next lesson.

Synthesis. Stop asking a mood to do a procedure's job.

Where the council could be wrong. Procedures can avoid courage. Some choices are clear at question 1 and the other six are stalling. Simon can be misread as "good enough is always enough," which is how people stay in the wrong job for a decade.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to Thursday, 7:40 p.m.

Calm. Phone in another room. One pause. The offer is a document with a time on it.

Observe. Expiry tomorrow 5:00; 30% cash; commute; Elena's "soon"; Sam's fear; four months of savings; three good interviews; no independent reference on the manager yet.

Define. Tonight is not for feeling brave. It is for a Decision 7 good enough to act on by 4:00 tomorrow.

Verify. One thing is still checkable: a reference. Send two messages now.

Prioritize. Ruin first: can the household survive a bad 12 months? If no, the stretch is the wrong shape. If yes, fear is a bruise, not a veto.

Find resources / people. Sam is a stakeholder, not an oracle. Elena is a possible option, not a parent.

Options. Take it. Stay and request a dated counter before 5:00. Ask North for five days. Take it with a written downside plan. Decline and keep looking.

Risks. Asking Elena can mark her as leaving. Not asking can waste a reversible internal move. Taking it on chemistry can be a one-way year.

Choose. Write the seven tonight. Do not choose in the kitchen argument. 8:30 Elena. 4:00 choose.

Communicate. To Sam: "I want growth and a raise without betting the rent. I am running the actual decision." To Elena, BLUF: "I have an offer that expires tomorrow. I would rather stay for a real counter by 3:00. If that is not possible, I will decide on the offer as it stands."

Act. Reference messages. Elena ask. 4:00 on the calendar.

Document. The Decision 7 page, dated. You will need it in 7.7.

Adapt. If the reference comes back cautious, assumption 3 changes.

Reflect. Did she answer "want" without naming the job?

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the offer with a Friday fuse. Situation: better-looking role, incomplete information, a partner with a vote, a manager who might counter. What most people do: reread the offer and poll friends until the loudest fear wins. Why it fails: friends are answering a different question (what they would need in order to feel safe). Resourceful response: Decision 7 on paper; one checkable assumption; a time to choose earlier than the panic. Words to use: "What I want in a year is… The assumption I cannot check is… The ruin case is…" Words to avoid: "I just need to sleep on it" with no procedure; "I'll regret it forever." Tool: Decision 7. AI assist: the clerk prompt, then you choose. What could go wrong: you use Elena as a bluff you cannot stand behind.

Money — the purchase with a countdown clock. Situation: a sale ends tonight; the item is fine; you have not compared the next-best. What most people do: buy to stop the timer, or freeze and miss a real need. Why it fails: the seller's clock is not your clock. Resourceful response: Want / Know / Options / Choose in ninety seconds. If the third option is "this is a one-way lemon," walk. Words to use: "My want is X. This sale is not a fact about quality." Words to avoid: "It's a sign." Tool: Decision 7, fast version. AI assist: a third option and a bruise/ruin split, not a blessing. What could go wrong: you delay a two-way purchase for weeks. Speed belongs on reversible doors — 7.2.

People — whether to escalate a concern. Situation: a fact that Elena's plan will slip; Jonah wants a showdown; the room is tomorrow. What most people do: pick a side based on who they like. Why it fails: the room hears alliance, not information. Resourceful response: want = a true date. Options include a private BLUF to Elena before the meeting. Words to use: "The date moves if X is true. I can show you in ten minutes." Words to avoid: "Someone has to say it." Tool: Decision 7, then Room Sheet (4.1). AI assist: draft the ten-minute BLUF from facts only. What could go wrong: a beautiful Decision 7 and still an ambush. The procedure does not replace decency.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Same kitchen. Worse. The new company will not extend. Elena is on a plane. Sam says, "If you take this, I don't know what we're doing." Savings are six weeks, not four months. The only reference said, "It depends who you work for."

The Decision 7 still runs. It gets harsher at questions 1, 5, and 7.

Want: do not blow the household for a maybe. That is now a constraint.

Wrong: ruin is in the room. A stretch role that can strand you in six weeks is not a growth opportunity. It is a bet of the house. You do not take those.

Choose: decline, or take it only with a written floor you do not have — which means you cannot take it. Tell Sam the ruin line, not a speech about destiny. Tell Elena, when she lands, the truth: you had an offer, you did not use her as theater, you are still here, and "soon" needs a date.

Hands can shake. The procedure still works.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Which of the seven questions do you skip because it would change the answer?
  • When you say "I don't know enough," is that a fact or a stall?
  • Who in your life is answering a different decision than the one you asked?
  • What is your current binary trap, and what is the third option you have not allowed?
  • If this choice goes badly, will you be able to tell whether the process was bad — or only that it hurt?
Remember

Remember This

You do not need a complete map. You need seven honest answers and a next step.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Simon was arguing with homo economicus — the agent who knows all options, ranks them, and picks the best. In Administrative Behavior (1947) and later work, he described what people actually do: search until something meets a threshold, then stop. That is not laziness. It is how a nervous system survives a world that will not sit still for the ranking.

Decision analysis, in the von Neumann–Morgenstern line, tried to give the ranking a backbone: utilities, probabilities, expected value. Powerful when the numbers are real — insurance, repeated bets, some engineering. Costume jewelry when you invent probabilities to three decimal places so you can feel like an adult. Expected value lite arrives in 7.3. This course will not teach you to fake a model.

Kahneman and Tversky then showed that even when people could compute, they often do not: they feel losses more than gains, overweight vivid risks, get pulled by framing. The Decision 7 will not delete those features. It will make some of them visible at questions 1, 3, and 5.

Two harder additions: write a minimum acceptable for each want before you look at the option again. And write the opposite choice's best case as strongly as your preferred choice's. If you cannot, you are in love, and love is a terrible clerk.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24 hours, run a complete Decision 7 on one real open choice — career, money, or people. Not a hypothetical. Not a choice you already made.

Write it by hand or in a dated note. Circle the assumption doing the most work. Put a review date on the calendar. If you choose, tell one stakeholder the want and the ruin line, not the campaign.

Bring the page into 7.7. You are going to need a trail.
