Numbers That Lie

Skepticism as a civic skill

24 minBase Rate, Denominator, Causation3,124 words22 sections
Skill

You can challenge a chart without being a statistician

Compared to what, out of what, and did it actually cause that?
The idea

The Hook

A number arrives in a room and the room sits down.

"Up 400 percent." "Nine out of ten." "The average family." "After we launched the tool, revenue rose." The slides are clean. The axis is chopped. The baseline is a quiet month nobody remembers. Nobody wants to be the pedant. The decision moves.

You do not need a statistics degree to refuse that hypnosis. You need three questions that fit in a pocket: Compared to what? Out of what? Did this actually cause that?

Base rate. Denominator. Causation.

People who cannot ask them are not kinder. They are easier to steer. People who ask them badly — sneering, point-scoring — get ignored, and then the bad number still wins. The resourceful human asks them like an adult who wants the decision to survive contact with the world.

The room

The Scenario

The Room, 10:02 a.m. A vendor is on the call. Their slide: a line shooting up. Title: "Customers who adopted our platform saw 400% more engagement." A testimonial. A logo row.

Elena leans forward. Jonah whispers, "That's the one I wanted."

You can see, if you squint, that the y-axis starts at 12, not 0. The time window is six weeks after a holiday. No sample size. No mention of customers who adopted it and left. A footnote in gray: "Engagement among active weekly users, selected accounts."

The vendor smiles. "Any questions before we talk commercial terms?"

Elena looks at you. The pause is two seconds long.

What do you ask?

Your move

Your Move

Common move. Nothing. You do not want to kill the vibe. You will kill it later, in a hallway, when the contract is already a social fact.

Better move. "What's the sample size?" A good question, alone. They will say "dozens of success stories" and keep moving.

Expert move. Three questions, calm, specific: base rate (400% of what, compared to whom), denominator (active weekly users on selected accounts is a filter, not a population), causation (did engagement rise because of the tool, because of the holiday, because only winners stayed in the chart?).

Resourceful move. The expert questions, plus a request that fits in the meeting: "Can you show the same chart including churned accounts, with a zero baseline, and the control — accounts that did not adopt?" You are not trying to win a statistics fight. You are trying to make a number that can travel.

Why the common move fails: silence is treated as consent. The 400% will appear in Elena's Monday email as if you had checked it.

What can go wrong with the expert move: if you perform intelligence, the vendor will drown you in extra slides, and Elena will think you are blocking. Ask for the comparison, not for a seminar. One or two questions. Then a request for a follow-up chart. Then stop.

The idea

The Big Idea

A number is a sentence with the nouns removed.

Your job is to put the nouns back. Compared to what (base rate). Out of what (denominator). And whether the story is a cause or a coincidence.

Until those are named, you do not have evidence. You have a shape.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Base rate. What is typical, what is the starting point, what is the comparison group? "Up 400%" from 1 to 5 is not the same as from 1,000 to 5,000. A disease test that is "99% accurate" is a different object in a rare disease than in a common one — most of the positives may still be false if the base rate is tiny. You do not need Bayes' theorem on a whiteboard. You need to ask "400% of what, and how common was the thing before?"

Denominator. Every percentage is a fraction. Who is in the bottom? "Nine out of ten dentists" — ten of which dentists? "Engagement among active weekly users, selected accounts" — you have dropped the inactive, the churned, the unselected. That is selection bias. The remaining group is a highlight reel.

Causation. Two things moved together, or one thing moved after another. That is not "because." Revenue rose after the tool. Revenue also rose after the holiday, the ad spend, the one giant customer, the weather. Correlation is not causation. To even start on cause you want a comparison group, a time window that was not cherry-picked, and a mechanism that could be real.

How to read a chart in a meeting (ninety seconds, eyes only):

  1. Title: is it a claim or a description?
  2. Axes: zero? units? log?
  3. Time window: why these dates?
  4. Who is in the data? Who is missing?
  5. What would the opposite chart have to look like to kill this claim?

If you cannot answer 4 and 5, you do not understand the chart. You understand the color.

What's changing. Dashboards get prettier. AI will generate the chart and the caption. The caption will sound like a finding. 6.4 still applies: fluency is not accuracy. The three questions still apply: they are older than your software.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: Base Rate, Denominator, Causation

Write BDC on the slide in your notes.

  1. Base rate. What is the starting number, the typical rate, the comparison group? If "up 400%" cannot be restated as "from A to B, n = ?", it is not ready.
  2. Denominator. Say the fraction out loud: "X out of Y, where Y is…" If Y is "selected active users," you have a highlight reel. Ask for Y = all who started.
  3. Causation. List two other things that could have moved the number. Ask what would falsify the causal story. If nobody can name a falsifier, it is a slogan.

Then pick one request: a table of A, B, n, and the excluded. Charts are allowed after the table.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

When the slide is up and you have one sentence of air:

"Can we see that as from-to with a sample size, including the people who didn't make it into the chart?"

If that feels too sharp for the politics of the room:

"I want to make sure I can repeat this upstairs. What's the baseline, who's in the denominator, and what did we compare against?"

Same questions. Less status threat.

The hack

The Hack

Restate the percentage as raw counts.

"400% more engagement" → "from 2 extra clicks a week to 10, on 14 accounts that did not churn." Sometimes the magic dies in that sentence. Sometimes it survives, and then you have a real fact.

If they cannot give counts, they do not have the number. They have a graphic.

AI move

The AI Move

Use AI for:

  • Listing ways a chart could be misleading
  • Turning a percentage back into a hypothetical from-to
  • Playing the hostile board member before you walk into The Room

Do not use AI for:

  • Inventing a statistical test you then cite (6.4)
  • A fake "p-value" or a fake paper
  • Deciding the vendor is lying because you want them to be

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Context: I have to read a number or chart in a meeting. I am not a statistician. I need questions, not a vibe.
Claim: "[paste the title or sentence]"
What I can see: [axis starts at… / window… / footnote… / n unknown]
Decision at stake: [contract / hire / budget / family money]

Goal: Run Base Rate, Denominator, Causation.
1) Restate the claim as from A to B, out of Y, compared to Z. Leave blanks.
2) List the likely tricks (tiny base, selection, cherry-picked window, axis, mean vs median, causal leap).
3) Give me three questions I can actually say out loud, in increasing bluntness.
4) What would falsify the claim?

Constraints: No fake studies. No formulas I will not use. No moral lecture.
Output: blanks, tricks, questions, falsifier.
Verification: What number I should demand in the follow-up email.

What to verify: Any "study" the vendor cites — open it. Date, sample, conflict of interest.

Do not paste: the vendor's confidential pricing sheet, customer names.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "see a number as a sentence."

Category: charts and dashboards. Current examples live inside spreadsheets, presentation tools, BI platforms, and AI assistants that will happily draw a line. The software is not the skill. The skill is BDC.

When not to fight the chart: when the decision is tiny and reversible (7.2's two-way door). Save your political capital for numbers that spend money or set policy.

What's changing. Generated "insights" will keep arriving as captions. Treat a caption as a claim in 6.4's sense. Open the table.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

People confuse being skeptical with being contrary.

If you knock down every number, the room stops bringing you numbers, and then you are the person who is always "difficult" while the contract proceeds in a different meeting. Resourceful skepticism is targeted: load-bearing claims, high stakes, tricks you can actually name.

The other mistake: accepting a relative change without an absolute. "Up 400% from a tiny base" is how a rounding error becomes a strategy.

Recovery: if you already nodded at 400%, you can still send the follow-up: "Before we use this upstairs, I need from-to, n, and the unselected accounts." Nodding is not a signature. Silence in the minutes is. Put the request in writing.

Defense

The Defense

BDC fails when:

  • There is no data, only a vision. Then you are in a different lesson (decisions under uncertainty, Level 7). Do not pretend a vision is a chart.
  • The other person is more expert than you and answering in good faith. Ask for the comparison group anyway. Experts forget that you cannot see their base rate.
  • The trick is moral, not statistical — a true number used to humiliate. Then the number can be right and the use still wrong. Say that separately.
  • You use causation as a way to never act. "We can't be sure it caused it" is true of almost everything. You need a decision standard, not a philosophy seminar. Demand a comparison, then choose.

Limitation: you will not catch sophisticated fraud in ninety seconds. You will catch the usual theater, which is most of what you will be shown.

Council

The Masters' Table

Darrell Huff. Through How to Lie with Statistics (1954): the tricks are old. Cut axes, picked averages, impressive percentages. The book is dated in examples; the moves are not.

Daniel Kahneman. Easy numbers feel true. A sharp up-and-to-the-right line is cognitive ease. Ease is the trick.

Nassim Nicholas Taleb. Through the fragility lens: a story that only works on the survivors (the accounts that stayed) is a story built on graves you were not shown. Do not build a strategy on a highlight reel.

Richard Feynman. If it disagrees with experiment, it is wrong. The "experiment" here is the full dataset, not the slide.

Edward Tufte. A chart should show the data. Chartjunk, 3-D, and decorative baselines are not aesthetics. They are fog.

Synthesis. The council wants the nouns back in the sentence, the dead included in the sample, and the causal story to be able to die.

Where the council could be wrong. Endless suspicion can hide a real effect. Sometimes the tool did help. The point of BDC is to let a true 400% survive as from-to with a denominator, not to make 400% illegal.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 10:02.

Calm. Shoulders. You are not in a duel. You are translating.

Observe. 400%, selected active users, chopped axis, holiday window, no n, Elena leaning in.

Define. Job of your next sentence: keep a bad number off the Monday email without turning the vendor into a martyr.

Verify. You cannot verify their data in the room. You can refuse to treat it as verified.

Prioritize. Denominator and comparison group first. Axis second. Contract last.

Find resources / people. A follow-up with their analyst, your own export if you already have customers on the tool, finance.

Options. Silence. A sneer. Or one adult question plus a written ask.

Risks. Looking obstructionist. Looking naive. Letting 400% become "the" number.

Choose. Adult question.

Communicate. "I want to be able to repeat this upstairs without getting cut in half. Can you give us the from-to in raw counts, including accounts that adopted and left, and the same window for accounts that didn't adopt? The 400% on selected active users isn't a number I can defend yet."

Act. After the call, send the ask in four bullets. Do not accept a new pretty slide as the answer.

Document. What was claimed, what was missing, what you requested.

Adapt. If they produce a real comparison and the effect remains, you update. Intellectual honesty (2.7) is not optional just because you were the skeptic.

Reflect. Did you ask to see, or to win?

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the vendor slide. Situation: 400%, selected users, a contract in the air. What most people do: wait for someone more senior to doubt. Why it fails: seniority is not a check. Resourceful response: BDC out loud, then a written ask. Words to use: "from-to, n, including the ones who left." Words to avoid: "This is statistically illiterate" in a room that still has to work together. Tool: BDC. AI assist: the prompt, before the meeting, not during as a snide fact-check. What could go wrong: you are right and rude, and the number still wins in a second meeting you are not in.

Family — the health headline. Situation: "This food doubles your risk." What most people do: panic or mock. Why it fails: both skip the base rate. Doubling a tiny risk is still tiny; doubling a large one is not. Resourceful response: "Doubles from what to what? How many people? Compared to whom?" Words to use: absolute risk, not just relative. Words to avoid: "That's fake news" as a substitute for a denominator. What could go wrong: delaying real medical advice. Headlines are not clinicians (6.4).

Money — the portfolio screenshot. Situation: a cousin is "up 400%" on a thing that started at $200. What most people do: feel poor, or pile in. Why it fails: tiny base, selection (you did not see the ones that went to zero), causation (luck vs skill). Resourceful response: raw counts, time window, what a 50% drawdown would do. What could go wrong: shaming a cousin at dinner. You can ask the questions about your money without running their life.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Elena already told the board "400% engagement." The board told the customer. You found, that morning, that the vendor's number is active-user highlight reel on twelve accounts, two of which are Elena's friends.

You do not ambush Elena in front of the board. You also do not let the company keep lying.

Private, first: "The 400% cannot be repeated as a general result. Here's the from-to and the n. I need us to correct it before it hits a contract. I'll draft a sentence that keeps your intent — the tool may help — without the fake precision."

If she refuses, you still do not sign your name to the 400%. Facts vs Stories. The story is "we are excited." The fact is "n = 12, selected."

The resourceful sentence upstairs: "We have a small, selected sample that looks promising. We do not have a 400% effect we can promise."

Reflect

Reflection

  • Which trick do you fall for when you want the number to be true?
  • When have you used a percentage to avoid saying a small count?
  • What is your actual sentence in a meeting — the one in your mouth, not on this page?
  • Where do you confuse median-vs-mean in your own life (salary, commute, "typical" day)?
  • If the chart made you feel something first, what was the feeling, and did it do the work of the data?

Remember This

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Huff's 1954 book is a museum of tricks that never closed. Tufte made a design ethic out of "show the data." Kahneman and Tversky mapped why a chopped axis works on a primate: relative difference is louder than absolute level. Taleb's survivor bias is the graveyard you do not see — the funds that died, the customers who churned, the papers that were not published.

A note on log scales: they are not evil. Earthquake magnitudes, some epidemic curves, some price series — log can be the honest view. The lie is an unlabeled log, or a log used to flatten a failure you did not want to show. Ask "is this log, and why?"

A note on base rates and tests: if a condition is rare and a test is imperfect, most positives can be false. This is not a reason to skip medicine. It is a reason not to treat a headline sensitivity number as a personal diagnosis. Human, official source, clinician.

If you want a harder drill, take a public dashboard (city, company, school) and rewrite one headline as A → B, n = Y, compared to Z. Send it to yourself. Notice how often Z is missing.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24 hours, catch one number in the wild — a meeting, an article, a dashboard, an ad.

Write four lines:

  1. The claim as stated
  2. From A to B (or "can't tell")
  3. Denominator
  4. The question you asked, or the question you would have asked

If you are in a meeting, ask one of them. Kind, specific, load-bearing.
