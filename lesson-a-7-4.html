Premortem and Red Team

Invent the failure while you can still prevent it

24 minPremortem3,938 words22 sections
Skill
Stress-test a plan on purpose
Tool
Premortem
Result
You find the hole before the world does
In one line
It is a year later and this failed. Why?
The idea

The Hook

The postmortem is always brilliant.

Six weeks after the launch, everyone can see it. The date was fantasy. The vendor needed a person who was already leaving. The customer had a silent constraint nobody wrote down. Jonah had a doubt on Tuesday and swallowed it because the room was in love. Elena had a doubt on Wednesday and swallowed it because the VP had already told a customer "we're good."

In the postmortem these doubts arrive as wisdom. They get stickers. Someone says "we should have known." They did know. They knew in the form that does not count: a tightness in the chest, a sentence that felt disloyal, a risk that would have slowed the vibe.

A premortem steals that future brilliance and spends it now, while the plan can still change.

Gary Klein's method is almost insultingly simple. You do not ask "what might go wrong?" in the polite voice that lets everyone stay loyal. You set a different scene: it is a year later, and this has failed. Write down why.

The room

The Scenario

Tuesday, 3:15 p.m. Elena's team, a glass room, a date on the whiteboard that the VP already used in a customer call.

"We're locking Friday as go-live. I need a yes around the table."

Jonah has the technical list. He is not looking at anyone. Mara has the customer list. She can name two accounts that will not be ready, and she can feel the cost of naming them in a room that wants a yes.

Elena: "Risks? Anyone?"

A pause that is not a Pause. It is a loyalty test.

Someone says, "We'll have to watch support." Someone else says, "As long as vendor X delivers." Heads nod. These are not risks. These are incense.

Jonah mutters, almost inaudible, "The import still corrupts on names with hyphens." Elena's eyes say not now.

"I'll take that as a yes. Mara, you're quiet."

The date is about to harden. What do you do with the next ninety seconds?

Your move

Your Move

Pick the sentence you would actually say. Then look at the families.

Common move. Nod. Add a small, respectable risk ("support") so you participated. Take your doubt home. Become wise in the postmortem.

Better move. Name the hyphen bug and the two unready accounts. Useful. In this weather it can sound like sabotage, and Elena may have to kill the messenger to keep the VP's story.

Expert move. Change the frame before you add content. "Before we lock, I want ten minutes as if this already failed." Then the hyphen and the accounts arrive as causes of a future failure, not as Mara's personality.

Resourceful move. Run a real premortem: time-boxed, written first so the loud do not eat the shy, then spoken. Separate preventable from weather. Assign owners to the preventable. If the date is still a date, it is a date with eyes open — and a door-type label on what you are locking.

Why the common move fails: "Any risks?" is a question that rewards incense. Klein's insight is that you have to authorize the failure before people will tell you the truth.

What can go wrong with the expert move: a premortem can become theater — a ritual roast, then the same date. Or it can become a veto machine for the most anxious person. The tool is to find holes, not to crown a new mood.

The idea

The Big Idea

Imagine the failure as a fact, then explain it.

That one shift — from "might" to "did" — gives people permission to stop selling the plan and start diagnosing it. You are not being negative. You are borrowing a future autopsy while the patient is still available.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Klein developed the premortem out of research on how people actually decide in the field — firefighters, nurses, military officers — not how they decide on a whiteboard. In a 2007 Harvard Business Review piece, "Performing a Project Premortem," he described the method for project teams: assume the patient died, then list why.

Why it works, when it works:

  • It licenses dissent without requiring a hero. You are not "against the team." You are explaining a future that already happened.
  • It beats generic risk lists. "Support might be busy" is incense. "It failed because we went live on a Friday with two enterprise accounts still on hyphenated names and no on-call" is a cause.
  • It catches the thing everyone half-knows. Kahneman has written that the method is a practical way to counteract the optimism that teams generate when they are invested. That is a use of his work, not a new law of physics.

A red team is the cousin: a person or group whose job is to make the plan fail on purpose, on paper or in a drill. Intelligence, security, and some product orgs use versions of this. Devil's advocate is the cheap home version: one person is assigned to argue the other side.

When it becomes theater:

  • The same two cynics always play red team, so the room discounts them like weather
  • Leadership "wants candor" and then punishes the first real sentence
  • The premortem is scheduled after the date is public, so it can only produce coping, not choice
  • Everyone lists twenty risks and nobody owns one
  • The devil's advocate is a rotating joke, not a serious attempt to break the plan

Janis's groupthink model (you will get the limits in 7.8) is the social weather this tool is trying to cut. The tool is not enough if the room is a loyalty test. You still run it. You also notice whether anyone's career got smaller after they told the truth.

Munger inversion is the same muscle in a different accent: tell me how this dies. Buffett's long game: do not lock a date that can interrupt compounding — reputation with a customer, a team that stops believing dates. Taleb: look for the failure that is not average, the one that ruins you. Simon: you will not find all the holes; you will find enough to change the plan or to stop pretending.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: Premortem

Use it before you lock a date, a hire, a launch, a household bet, a public promise. Ten to thirty minutes. Not a weekend retreat.

  1. Set the scene, out loud. "It is [date in the future]. This failed. Not 'might.' It failed. We are not in trouble for saying why. We are in trouble if we only learn this in the real postmortem."
  2. Write first, silently, two to five minutes. Each person lists causes. Writing first is the difference between a premortem and a performance by Jonah.
  3. Round-robin the causes. No debate yet. No fixing yet. One person captures them on a board in the speakers' words.
  4. Cluster. Sort into: preventable by us / depends on a vendor or weather / ruin vs bruise (7.3). Door type (7.2): which failures are one-way.
  5. Pick three. Not twenty. The three that are both likely enough and bad enough. Assign an owner and a change: kill, shrink, hedge, watch, or accept with eyes open.
  6. Change the plan or the date in the same meeting. A premortem that does not alter a sentence of the plan was theater. Even "we accept this risk; owner is X; trigger to abort is Y" is a change.
  7. Park the rest. A dated list. If a parked item starts coming true, it graduates.

For a solo decision, you can run it in a chair: write the failure letter from twelve months out, then answer it with the Decision 7's question 5, upgraded.

Devil's advocate, when you cannot assemble a group:

  • Assign the role before the discussion, not after everyone has nodded
  • The advocate must argue a specific failure, not a mood
  • Rotate the role so it does not become one person's identity
  • End with "what we are changing," not with applause for how honest you all were

Card:

It Failed. Write Why. Pick Three. Change The Plan.

Worked on Elena's go-live:

Silent list might include: hyphen import corrupts; two accounts unready; Friday go-live means a weekend of support with a short staff; VP already promised a customer; Jonah's doubt was treated as tone; vendor X's person is leaving.

Three that matter: hyphen (preventable, owner Jonah, must-fix or no-go); unready accounts (preventable, owner Mara, delay those two, do not hold the whole train or delay the train); Friday (process, owner Elena, move to Tuesday or staff the weekend for real).

The VP's promise is not a cause you can un-say in this room. It is a constraint. Name it: "We may be locking a date because it was spoken. That is a one-way social door. If we ship broken, we pay twice."

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

Ninety seconds, one person, a plan about to lock:

Say: "Fast premortem. It failed. Three reasons, then we pick one thing to change."

You go first with a real one, not incense, so the room knows the license is real. Then two others. Then one change. Then lock or delay.

Holding sentence when "any risks?" is already in the air:

"That question gets polite answers. Give me four minutes as if it already blew up."

The hack

The Hack

Write the failure from the customer's desk, or from the junior's desk, not from yours.

"It failed because we were wrong" is fog. "It failed because the account with the hyphenated legal name could not invoice for two weeks and they tweeted it" is a cause. "It failed because the on-call was a person who had never seen the import" is a cause.

If you lead, the hack is to go second, not first, after writing. If you go first you set the overton window of allowed failures. If you go last you can still kill candor with a look. Second is the least bad.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is a decent red team for kinds of failure. It does not know your hyphen bug. It will invent plausible failures that sound expensive and are not yours.

Use AI for:

  • A starter list of failure modes you will edit
  • Turning messy notes into preventable / weather / ruin
  • Drafting the "it is a year later" memo so humans can argue with it

Do not use AI for:

  • Replacing the silent write (the point is your people's knowledge)
  • A fake quantitative risk score
  • A villain story about a colleague

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Role: Premortem clerk.
Plan:
"""
[what we are locking, date, who is affected]
"""
Facts we already worry about:
[hyphens, staffing, promises already made — facts]
It is twelve months later. This failed.

Tasks:
1) Write a one-page failure memo in past tense, using only plausible causes. Mark any cause that is not supported by my facts as SPECULATIVE.
2) Sort causes: PREVENTABLE / WEATHER / RUIN.
3) Propose the three highest-leverage changes we could make this week.
4) Give me five questions to ask the room that are specific, not "any risks?"

Constraints: No corporate fog. No "communication issues" unless you can name the sentence that did not get said.
Do not assign blame to a person by name.

What to verify: Every speculative cause. If the model says "vendors like this often slip," that is a prompt to check this vendor, not a fact.

Do not paste: customer names, security details, personnel files, the unreleased plan if it is confidential in a way that would matter.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "capture the holes and attach owners."

Category examples:

  • A shared note with the past-tense prompt at the top
  • A board with three columns: preventable / weather / ruin
  • Tickets that are actual work, not a risk register nobody opens
  • For technical work: a staging drill that is the premortem in motion (break the import on purpose)

When not to use technology: a risk tool with 90 open items and no owners is a museum of incense. Three changes beat a database.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Asking "what might go wrong?" and thinking you ran a premortem.

You didn't. You ran a loyalty-safe weather report. The grammatical shift to past tense is not cute. It is the tool.

The other mistake: collecting twenty brilliant causes and changing nothing because the VP already told a customer. Then the premortem was a feeling. Recovery: if you cannot change the date, change the scope, the staffing, or the abort trigger. Something in the world must be different after the ten minutes, or you are in theater.

A third: using the premortem to kill two-way doors. Not every experiment needs a funeral. Match the depth of the premortem to the door. A flag to 8% gets five minutes. A three-year vendor gets the full ritual.

Defense

The Defense

Premortem fails when:

  • The date is a social one-way door and nobody is allowed to touch it. Then use the tool to change everything except the date, and document that the date was political. That document is for the real postmortem, and for your own Decision Page.
  • The room is not safe. People will write incense. Do not punish the first specific sentence. If you are not the leader, you can still run a solo premortem and bring one cause with a fix, BLUF.
  • You red-team people instead of plans. "It failed because Jonah is careless" is a status move. "It failed because the hyphen case had no test and no owner" is a premortem.
  • The failure is moral, not operational. A premortem will not tell you whether you should do the thing. It will tell you how the thing dies. Level 8 is for the should.

Limitation: imagining failure does not make you a seer. You will still miss. The win is fewer stupid misses, and a team that knows what "we accepted this" meant.

Council

The Masters' Table

Gary Klein. The premortem itself. Naturalistic decision making: people in the field have knowledge that polite meetings suppress. Change the scene, get the knowledge.

Charlie Munger. Invert. The premortem is inversion with a calendar.

Daniel Kahneman. Team optimism and the planning fallacy — we underweight how long and how badly. A premortem is a cheap counterweight, not a cure.

Nassim Taleb. Ask which failure is ruin, not which failure is embarrassing. Staff the ruin.

Irving Janis (with limits). Groupthink as a popular model of how cohesion plus pressure kills dissent. The model is neater than the evidence; treat it as weather you might be in, not as a diagnosis you can stamp on every nodding room. 7.8 returns to this.

Jeff Bezos (door lens). Do not premortem a two-way door into paralysis. Spend the ritual on the one-way.

Synthesis. Permission to be specific about death, then a change in the plan. Everything else is vibes.

Where the council could be wrong. Klein's method can be cargo-culted — past tense, stickers, same date. Kahneman's optimism story can let a cynic dominate. Taleb can turn every bruise into apocalypse. Use three causes and a change. Leave the rest.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 3:15 p.m.

Calm. Pause. You will not win this as a personality.

Observe. Date already spoken to a customer. Incense risks. Jonah's hyphen. Two unready accounts. Elena needs a yes. VP heat.

Define. The next ten minutes must either change the plan or make the accepted risks explicit, with owners. A nod is the failure mode.

Verify. Is the hyphen real? Are the two accounts real? You only need yes/no, not a seminar.

Prioritize. Preventable ruin and preventable customer harm first. Elena's feelings third. VP's already-spoken sentence is a constraint, not a god.

Find resources / people. Jonah (bug), Mara (accounts), whoever is actually on-call Friday, Legal if promises were contractual.

Options. Silent nod. Ambush with doubts. Or premortem frame + three causes + change.

Risks. Elena hears sabotage. The VP's story breaks. Or you ship a known-broken import on Friday and the postmortem is a moral play with your name in it.

Choose. Premortem, short, now.

Communicate. "Before we lock: ten minutes. It is six weeks from now and this go-live failed. Write why, silently, two minutes. Then we pick three and we change something in the plan — scope, date, staffing, or abort trigger. I'll start after we write, not before." Then, when it is time: "It failed because hyphenated names corrupted invoices for two accounts, and because we went live Friday with no real on-call."

Act. Jonah owns a must-fix or a no-go. Mara owns the two accounts (delay them or delay the train). Elena owns the Friday (move or staff). Write the abort trigger: "If hyphen is not green by Thursday noon, we do not go."

Document. The three, the owners, the trigger. One page.

Adapt. If Elena cannot touch the date, shrink the blast radius: flag, subset of customers, war room. Door type, out loud.

Reflect. Did anyone get punished in the next week for a specific sentence? That is data about whether you had a premortem or a play.

Practice

Practice

Twelve minutes. A live plan you are actually in — a launch, a trip, a hire, a household purchase, a conversation.

  1. Write the past-tense sentence: "It is [date]. This failed."
  2. List eight causes, fast, ugly, specific.
  3. Mark each P (preventable), W (weather), R (ruin) or B (bruise).
  4. Circle three.
  5. Write the change in the plan, or the abort trigger, in one sentence each.
  6. Send one of those sentences to the person who can actually change it — or put it on your own Decision 7 if the plan is yours.

If you cannot name a change, you did a mood. Do the last step.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the date the VP already used. Situation: go-live is social-irreversible; the holes are still real. What most people do: incense, then a heroic weekend. Why it fails: the postmortem will be wise and useless. Resourceful response: premortem now; change scope, staffing, or abort trigger if the date is frozen. Words to use: "It failed because…" "The abort trigger is…" Words to avoid: "I'm just not sure we're ready" (mood, no cause). Tool: Premortem. AI assist: failure memo, then you delete the speculative parts. What could go wrong: you become "not a team player." Bring a fix, not only a funeral.

Money — the house, the car, the "can't miss" investment. Situation: a large purchase with a story attached. What most people do: a pro/con list that assumes the story. Why it fails: the failure modes are social (we stretch the budget, we cannot leave) and they do not show up as line items. Resourceful response: "It is two years later and this purchase hurt us. Why?" Write the ruin case. If ruin is possible, it is a one-way door with a veto. Words to use: "If this fails, we still can / cannot…" Words to avoid: "It will be fine if we both hustle." Tool: Premortem + ruin check (7.3) + Door Type (7.2). AI assist: starter failure list; you add the household facts the model does not have. What could go wrong: you premortem yourself into never buying a sofa. Match depth to door.

People — the hire everyone likes. Situation: a candidate who charmed the room; Jonah has a specific doubt about a work sample; Elena wants to close. What most people do: "any concerns?" then a yes. Why it fails: charm is not a work sample. Resourceful response: "It is nine months later and this hire failed. Why?" Force written causes. Jonah's doubt becomes a checkable reference or a paid trial, not a vibe. Words to use: "The failure I'm explaining is…" Words to avoid: "I just don't have a good feeling" as the whole move. Tool: Premortem, then a two-way version (trial) if the door allows. AI assist: none on the person's private data. Process only. What could go wrong: a premortem used to block a candidate for status reasons. Demand specific, checkable causes.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Elena says, "We don't have time for a workshop. Yes or no." The VP is on the video tile. Jonah's hyphen is real. You have forty seconds.

You do not run the full ritual. You run the fast method with your body in the Pause.

"Yes, with an abort. It is next month and this failed because hyphenated names broke invoices and we were on a Friday with no on-call. Two changes: Jonah has a hyphen go/no-go by Thursday noon; if it's red we do not go, and I will call the customer myself. If we cannot have that abort, my vote is no, not because I'm against the team, because I am against the specific failure I just named."

If they override you, you send a three-line note after: the failure you named, the abort you asked for, the decision they made. That is not CYA as a lifestyle. It is a Decision Page for a one-way door you did not get to set.

If your hands shake, allowed. Specific beats brave-looking.

Reflect

Reflection

  • What do you already know about a live plan that you have not said because it would change the weather?
  • When did a postmortem make you look wise for a silence you chose?
  • Who in your rooms is assigned "red team" as a personality, and therefore ignored?
  • Which of your premortems were theater — past tense, then the same date, no owner?
  • What is the abort trigger on the thing you are currently locking?
Remember

Remember This

It is a year later and this failed. Why? Write it now, while you can still change the plan.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Klein's broader project — recognition-primed decision making — is about experts who see a situation and match it to a pattern, then run a mental simulation of the first workable action, not a full option tree. That is Simon's satisficing in a burning building. The premortem is a way to force a mental simulation of failure when the room is simulating only success.

The Heath brothers popularized the premortem further in Decisive (2013). Popularization is a double edge: more people know the name, more rooms run a cargo-cult version. Your test is still: did three causes get owners, and did the plan change?

Red teams in security and intelligence have their own failure mode: they become a guild with incentives to find theatrical holes (to justify their existence) or to go easy (to get invited back). If you ask someone to red-team, watch the incentive. Pay them, in status or in fact, for a hole that changes the plan, not for a performance of dissent.

A harder variation for mastery: the preparade. Klein and others have also discussed imagining spectacular success and asking what actually caused it — to surface the lucky assumptions. Use it after the premortem, not instead. Success-imagining without a funeral first is just the original vibe.

If you facilitate: silent writing is not optional. The first time you skip it, Jonah will eat the session and Mara will take her hyphen home.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 48 hours, run a premortem on one live plan — ten minutes if you are alone, twenty if you can get one other person.

Past tense. Written first. Three causes. One change in the world (scope, date, owner, abort trigger).

Send the change to whoever can enact it, in one BLUF paragraph. If that person is you, enact it before you sleep.
