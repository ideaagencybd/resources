Manage Up

Make your boss easy to help you

24 minThe Upward Brief3,550 words22 sections
Skill
Create clarity above you
Tool
The Upward Brief
Result
You can run a manager who is busy, vague, or both
In one line
Make your boss easy to help you.
The idea

The Hook

Your boss is not a mind-reader. Your boss is a bottleneck with a calendar.

They are in three other machines. They have a manager of their own. They asked you for "visibility" and then disappeared, or they asked you for visibility and then stood behind your chair. You call the first absentee and the second a micromanager. Both are often the same problem in different clothes: they cannot see your world, they are accountable for it, and you have not made it easy to help.

Managing up is not office-politics cosplay. It is not flattery, alliance maps, or "managing your brand." It is a service: you package status, ask, risk, and options so a busy person can do their job in minutes instead of a novel. Grove would call it part of your output. Drucker would call it making knowledge work visible. You can call it the Upward Brief.

If you wait to be managed well, you will wait a long time. If you make your boss easy to help you, even a mediocre manager becomes usable. A good one becomes a force multiplier.

The room

The Scenario

Wednesday, 4:50 p.m. Elena is walking between rooms.

Mara needs three things: a keep/switch decision (still), cover for Friday because Priya is out, and Elena to stop sales from promising 24 hours. She has been "keeping Elena in the loop" with nine chat bubbles over two days. Elena has replied to one of them with "k."

They overlap at the kitchen.

Elena: "Anything I need to know?"

Mara feels the whole machine rise in her throat. Jonah's blunt thread. The customer. The queue. Sam's draft. The 24-hour promise. Her Top 3. The fact that "k" did not mean the decision was made.

She has forty-five seconds before Elena's next door closes.

What comes out of her mouth?

Your move

Your Move

Common move. "It's been a lot, but we're on it." A vibe. Elena hears "no decision required" and leaves. The bottleneck remains Elena, invisibly.

Better move. A dump. Chronology. Names. Heat. Elena's eyes glaze, then she grabs one random detail and issues a tactical order ("just email sales"). The important decision still does not happen.

Expert move. Four beats, in order: status, ask, risk, options. "Queue is visible, oldest item has a date. I need keep/switch by tomorrow 17:00. Risk: sales is still promising 24 hours we cannot do. Options: you tell sales today, or I will, with your name on it, or we accept another complaint. Recommend you tell them."

Resourceful move. The same four beats, plus a default, plus the right channel. Forty-five seconds gets a spoken BLUF. The written brief follows in six lines so Elena can forward it up. Mara does not use the kitchen to process her feelings about Elena's "k." She uses it to make Elena's next action obvious. If Elena is a micromanager, the brief includes how Mara will report, so Elena does not have to hover to feel safe. If Elena is absent, the brief includes the default that will fire without her.

Why the common move fails: bosses cannot help with what they cannot see, and they will not read what you will not structure. "In the loop" is not a brief.

What can go wrong with the expert move: a brief that is actually a prosecution of sales, or a trap that makes Elena look stupid in front of her boss. You are here to make her effective, not to win a sibling fight using her as the parent.

The idea

The Big Idea

Managing up is packaging decisions for a person with less context and more consequences.

You already have BLUF (4.2). This is BLUF with a job: move a decision, a cover, or a risk into the only pair of hands that can hold it. Status without an ask is a newsletter. An ask without options is a dump. Options without a recommendation is fear. Risk unsaid is how eleven-day letters are born.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Two common boss problems, one underlying gap.

The absent manager does not know what you need, overestimates your air cover, and experiences your work as a calm sea because you have been drowning quietly. They need a brief that names the ask and the default if they stay silent.

The micromanager does not trust the picture, so they pull on the nearest thread. Sometimes they were burned. Sometimes they have no other instrument. They need a brief that is frequent enough and clear enough that hovering has nothing to catch. Grove's TRM applies upward too: you are training their confidence in this task.

Neither type is helped by politics-as-identity: "She's a narcissist," "He's checked out." Maybe. You still have a machine to run. Character diagnosis is a story (2.1). The next useful move is a one-page instrument.

Peter Drucker wrote that the effective executive takes responsibility for relationships, including the one with the boss — not as servility, as logistics. Andy Grove treated meetings and updates as the medium of managerial work. If the medium is nine chat bubbles, the work will look like nine chat bubbles.

Bezos's two-way door (light touch; you will do this properly in 7.2): if the decision is reversible, your brief should say so and recommend speed. If it is a one-way door, your brief should say that too, and not bury it under "FYI." Do not make your boss play door-type guessing.

You are not responsible for Elena's whole personality. You are responsible for the quality of the inputs she gets from you. That is a lot, and it is enough.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Upward Brief

Use it whenever someone above you must decide, protect, or know a risk. Spoken in 45 seconds, written in six to ten lines.

  1. Status. One or two facts. Now, not the history of the war. "Queue exists. Oldest item dated. Priya out Friday."
  2. Ask. One sentence. A decision, a door, a name, a no. If you have no ask, you are writing a newsletter. Don't. "Need keep/switch by Thu 17:00."
  3. Risk. What happens if we do nothing, in concrete terms. "If we don't stop the 24-hour promise, we get another complaint in a week. If we don't cover Priya, quality sits until Tuesday."
  4. Options. Two or three, including a default. Label your recommendation. "A) You talk to sales today (recommended). B) I talk to sales with your cover. C) We accept the next miss."
  5. Default if silent. Especially for the absent manager. "If I don't hear you by Thu 17:00, I proceed on split and I email sales with B." Do not default a one-way door.
  6. Cadence (for the hoverer). "I'll send this format Mon/Wed, and immediately if a customer risk appears. If you want more, tell me what would make you feel covered." You are negotiating the instrument, not their anxiety.

Spoken kitchen version (45 seconds):

Status: [one fact].

Ask: [one sentence].

Risk: [one sentence].

Options: [A / B, I recommend A].

I can send this in six lines after this.

HovererCadence + enough detail to put the thread downHiding until they pounce

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

When you have forty-five seconds and a doorway:

Say: Need / By when / If not / Risk.

"I need keep or switch by tomorrow 5. If not, I'll go split. Risk is sales still promising 24 hours — I need you or me to stop that today."

Then close your mouth. The Pause still applies. Do not fill Elena's silence with a dump.

The hack

The Hack

Write the brief as if Elena will forward it without asking you.

That constraint kills slang, score-settling, and buried leads. It also helps her manage her up, which is often the actual bottleneck. If your brief cannot travel, it is a diary.

Second hack: put the ask in the first line of the written version, even before status, when the clock is short. BLUF is not rude. It is kind to a calendar.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is useful for compressing nine bubbles into a brief and for offering options you are too hot to see. It will also sand off a necessary risk if you ask it to "make this more professional."

Use AI for:

  • Drafting status / ask / risk / options from messy notes
  • A version you could speak in 45 seconds and a version you could paste
  • A "what would a busy manager misunderstand?" pass

Do not use AI for:

  • Flattery, "alignment" padding, or political positioning
  • Inventing options you cannot actually execute
  • Processing Elena's personality

Prompt (copy, then fill):

WHEN TO USE: I need an Upward Brief my manager can act on in two minutes.

Audience: [boss role, what they care about, absent or hoverer]
Facts:
- [status]
- [clocks]
Ask: [decision or cover I need]
Risks if we do nothing: [concrete]
Options I can actually live with:
- A
- B
Default if silent: [reversible only]

Goal: Produce
1) A 45-second spoken version.
2) A 6–10 line written version, forwardable.
3) One line on what a busy reader might miss.

Constraints: No flattery. No villain narrative. Ask in the first line of the written version. Label a recommendation.

What to verify: You can execute every option. The default is reversible. Names are fair.

Do not paste: your boss's private messages, HR issues, anything that should be a live conversation about harm.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "a forwardable artifact plus a cadence."

Category: a short written brief in a place they already look. Current examples: a weekly six-liner at the top of a doc; a Monday calendar invite with the brief in the body; a pinned channel post with the same four headings; a voice memo if they commute and you follow with the six lines. Do not build a "leadership dashboard" to avoid a sentence.

When not to use technology: a slide deck for a kitchen conversation. The doorway gets speech. The follow-up gets six lines.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Reporting activity, then calling it managing up.

"This week I met with Jonah, poked sales, and thought about Priya." That is a diary of motion. Elena cannot decide from it.

It happens because activity feels like proof of work, and a clean ask feels exposing — what if she says no?

Recovery: rewrite the last update as Status / Ask / Risk / Options. If you cannot find an ask, you probably needed to leave her alone. Silence is allowed. Newsletters are not required.

Defense

The Defense

The Upward Brief fails when:

  • The boss is not merely absent or hovering. They are unethical, abusive, or running you as cover for a lie. A brief will not fix that. Document, find people, use the real channels. This course will not teach you to manage a predator with formatting.
  • You use "managing up" to hide a miss. Status must be true. Facts vs Stories. If you burned the date, the brief says so, with a path.
  • You flood. A brief every hour is hovering up. Match cadence to risk.
  • Cultural context makes direct asks land as insubordination. Then you keep the four beats and soften the edges — you do not delete the ask.

Limitation: you cannot make Elena a great manager with six lines. You can make her a better-informed one, and you can stop being illegible.

Council

The Masters' Table

Andy Grove. Through Grove's lens, you are part of your manager's production process. A bad update is a defective part. Make the medium efficient: decisions in, decisions out.

Peter Drucker. Take responsibility for the relationship. The boss has strengths and gaps; your job includes working with the actual person, not the one you wish had been hired.

BLUF / military briefing tradition. Status and ask first. Narrative later if asked. Kindness under time pressure (4.2).

Jeff Bezos (light). Label the door type. Do not demand a committee for a two-way door. Do not sneak a one-way door through as an FYI. Full treatment in 7.2.

Chris Voss (light, from Level 4). If there is heat, an accurate label of their problem — "This puts you in a bad spot with your boss if sales is still promising 24 hours" — can make the brief easier to hear. Empathy is not politics. It is accuracy.

Synthesis. Make the next action obvious. Tell the truth about risk. Give options. Do not make your boss excavate you.

Where the council could be wrong. "Managing up" language has been used to justify sucking up, and to put the whole burden of a bad system on the person with the least power. If the machine is the problem, bring the map (5.4), not a more charming brief. If the burden is unfair, the brief can say "we are out of capacity" as a fact. Do not become the translator who makes an impossible job look fine.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to the kitchen, 4:50.

Calm. Pause. Forty-five seconds is enough if you do not spend them on autobiography.

Observe. Elena asked a real question. She is in motion. Nine bubbles failed. Three asks exist; she can hold one or two.

Define. Spoken brief now; written six lines after; pick the decision that unblocks the rest.

Verify. She still has not decided keep/switch. "k" was not a decision. Do not pretend it was.

Prioritize. Decision on vendor path, then sales promise (risk), then Friday cover. Q3 is not this doorway.

Find resources / people. A six-line note Elena can forward to sales or to her boss.

Options. Dump, vibe, or four beats.

Risks. She hears only the sales complaint and misses the decision. Put the ask first.

Choose. Ask first.

Communicate. "I need keep or switch by tomorrow 5, or I go split. Risk: sales is still promising 24 hours we can't do — that will be another complaint. Options: you tell them today, or I do with your name. I recommend you. Priya is out Friday; I'll send six lines on cover. I'll put this in writing in ten minutes."

Act. She sends the written brief. She does not add a tenth bubble of anxiety.

Document. The six lines live in a place Elena already looks.

Adapt. If Elena says "handle it," Mara takes option B and writes "you asked me to handle sales" in the note. Clarity, not a gotcha.

Reflect. Did she try to tell the whole war?

A resourceful human in a doorway. Not a politician. A packager.

Practice

Practice

Do this now. Twelve minutes. Pick a live issue your manager (or a client, a professor, a lead parent) does not fully see.

  1. Write Status / Ask / Risk / Options / Default / Cadence.
  2. Cut it to ten lines.
  3. Write the 45-second spoken version. Say it out loud. Time it.
  4. Mark any line that is a villain story. Rewrite as a system or a request.
  5. If you have no ask, either find one or throw the brief away.
  6. Queue it to send, or put it on tomorrow's Top 3.
Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the busy boss. Situation: you need a decision; they live in "k." What most people do: more pings, then bitterness. Why it fails: pings are not a brief. Resourceful response: Upward Brief with a default. Words to use: "Need X by Y. If I don't hear you, I'll do Z. Risk is R." Words to avoid: "Just circling back," "per my last," a sarcastic "whenever you're ready." Tool: Upward Brief. AI assist: compress the bubbles; you verify tone. What could go wrong: a default that corners them. Warn, don't trap.

Work — the hoverer. Situation: they ask for updates faster than the work moves. What most people do: hide, or produce fake precision. Why it fails: hiding invites more hovering; fake precision invites more questions. Resourceful response: negotiate a cadence in the brief. Deliver it. Invite them to name what would make them feel covered. Words to use: "You'll get this format on X days, and the same day if a customer risk appears. What would make you feel covered?" Words to avoid: "I need more autonomy" with no instrument. Tool: Upward Brief + TRM honesty. What could go wrong: you promise a cadence you cannot keep. Then you become the reason they hover.

Outside work — a landlord, a doctor's office, a school. Situation: an institution that only moves when a decision-maker sees a clean ask. What most people do: a long story in a portal. Why it fails: no one can act. Resourceful response: the same four beats in an email. Words to use: the ask in line one. Words to avoid: your whole week. Tool: Upward Brief, renamed as a request to a busy stranger. What could go wrong: sounding like a lawyer. Stay human. Stay short.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Same kitchen. Worse.

Elena's next meeting is her boss. She says: "If it's on fire, say so. If not, I can't do this right now." Mara is on fire: the client copied Elena's boss; sales promised 24 hours on a call that just ended; Priya is out; Jonah is one blunt message from making it worse.

Do not say "it's a lot." Do not say "we're fine."

"It's on fire in one place. Client copied your boss about the eleven-day request — we now have a date, I'll send it in writing. New risk: sales promised 24 hours on a call just now. Ask: you need to walk into your next room knowing that, and I need you to retract it or authorize me to. I recommend you retract it in one sentence I can draft in five minutes. Everything else can wait until 6:30."

She has made Elena easy to help in the next room. That is the whole skill, under heat. Pause if the body leans into blame. Status, ask, risk, options. Then the six lines, because spoken fire still needs a trail.

If Elena snaps, do not snap. Holding sentence: "I'll put it in six lines. You can use it upstairs." Then go write.

Reflect

Reflection

  • What have you been "looping people in" on that still has no ask?
  • Is your boss absent, hovering, or starved of a usable picture?
  • Which of your updates could they forward? Which would embarrass you if they did?
  • Where do you want them to read your mind because asking feels like weakness?
  • What default would be fair if they stayed silent for 48 hours?
Remember

Remember This

Make your boss easy to help you. Status, ask, risk, options — then stop talking.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Politics versus logistics, and why this course will not teach you the first.

There is a real literature on power, coalitions, and organizational games. You will meet some of it later if you lead. This lesson stays on logistics because the costume of "political savvy" is how a lot of people avoid a clean ask. They network instead of briefing. They hint instead of recommending. They collect allies for a decision that needed six lines and a date.

Drucker's point, used carefully: you are not owed a compatible boss. You are owed, and you owe, a working relationship. That includes studying what they actually read (some will not open a doc; some will not remember a kitchen). Send the brief in the medium they use. That is not groveling. That is user research on one user.

Grove's one-on-ones, if you have them, are the natural home of the brief. Arrive with the four beats written. Do not use the slot to recap your week as a novel. If you do not have a one-on-one, the six lines are the one-on-one.

On micromanagement: some of it is fear, some is lack of TRM in the manager for this kind of work, some is a person who only feels useful when they touch the work. You can only fix the information problem. You cannot therapy them in a brief. If the hovering continues after a reliable cadence, that is data for a difficult conversation (4.4) or a map of whether this machine is livable (Level 8 will ask what is yours).

Harder variation: write the brief your boss needs to send their boss. Then cut yours to match. If you cannot see their up, ask once: "What does your week actually get judged on?" That question, asked without a smirk, is a career skill.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 48 hours, send one real Upward Brief — to a manager, a client, a lead, a committee chair. Use the four beats. Keep it under ten lines.

Afterward:

  1. The ask
  2. What they did with it
  3. What they still missed
  4. The one change to your next brief

If you have no boss, pick the person whose yes you are waiting on. The tool does not require an org chart. It requires a bottleneck above you.
