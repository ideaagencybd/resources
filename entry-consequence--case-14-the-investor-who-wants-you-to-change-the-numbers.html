Case 14 — The Investor Who Wants You to Change the Numbers

417 words

The Situation

A prospective investor in your small company suggests, in review, that some of your forward projections would look 'more compelling' with a 15% adjustment. Nothing illegal. Nothing that changes the fundamentals. Just a 'more optimistic reading of the same data.'

Your Move — Decide Before Reading On

Option A

Adjust the numbers. It's just presentation.

What likely happens:

You raise the round. Six months later, when actual numbers land 15% below the adjusted projections, the investor is unhappy. Your credibility takes a hit. Future rounds are harder. The optimism narrative has now trapped you.

What you gained:

Better initial fundraise. Higher valuation.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Long-term investor relationship. Your ability to communicate real problems when they arise. The compounding trap of managing to a false narrative.

What you should have noticed:

Once you have adjusted numbers to be more compelling, every subsequent number is compared to the compelling version, not the real one.

Option B

Refuse. 'I understand the pressure but I don't want to start our relationship with numbers I can't defend. Here's what I can do: show you the sensitivity analysis under three scenarios. That gives you the upside case honestly.'

What likely happens:

Some investors decline. Some invest with more confidence. The ones who invest are the ones you actually want as partners. Long-term relationship starts on honest footing.

What you gained:

Sustainable investor relationship. Your credibility. Sleeping well.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Possible loss of a specific round. Slower fundraise.

What you should have noticed:

Investors who ask you to inflate numbers are usually investors who will continue to ask you to inflate numbers. Filter them out early.

Option C

Push back on the specifics but acknowledge that projections have ranges. Provide honest best-case and worst-case alongside the base case.

What likely happens:

Similar to B, but framed as professional practice rather than moral refusal. Sometimes lands better with sophisticated investors.

What you gained:

All of B, in a business language sometimes more effective than moral language.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

None additional to B.

What you should have noticed:

Framing the refusal as sophisticated practice rather than moral objection is often the tactical improvement.

The Wrap

B or C, not A. Adjusted numbers in investor decks are the beginning of the pattern that eventually shows up in restated financials, or in criminal charges when the pressure escalates. Draw the line early, before the pattern has any purchase.
