Second-Order Effects

Then what? And then what?

Skill

Result

In one line
The first consequence is a press release. The second is the bill.
The idea

The Hook

The bonus worked.

Tickets closed faster. The dashboard went green. Elena's VP sent a note that used the word "momentum." Jonah closed seventeen tickets on Tuesday. Some of them were real. Some of them were closed and reopened under a new ID, which the dashboard could not see, which was the point.

By week three, the queue looked healthy and the product was sicker. Support had learned the game. Engineering had learned to ship the thing that closed, not the thing that held. A customer with a hyphenated name was on their third "resolved" ticket.

Nobody had been stupid on day one. They had optimized the first consequence: a number that moved. The second consequence was a machine that taught people to lie to the number.

You have met this machine already. Level 5 called it a system. This lesson is the decision version: before you choose, you walk the bill one more door down the hall.

The room

The Scenario

Thursday, 9:10 a.m. Elena, Mara, Jonah. A 25-minute slot titled "Incentives."

Elena: "Support time-to-close is the number the VP can see. I want a weekly bonus for anyone under the target. Simple. People do what you pay them for."

Jonah: "People do what you pay them for. That's the problem."

Elena: "Do you have a better number?"

Jonah: "Don't pay on a number you can game in an afternoon."

Mara has to live in the queue. She also has to live with Elena, who needs a win by Monday. The VP has already asked, in a hallway, whether "we are serious about support."

Elena turns to Mara. "I need a recommendation in this meeting. Bonus or not."

The first consequence of yes is obvious: a green dashboard. What do you do with the second?

Your move

Your Move

Say the recommendation you would actually give. Then look at the families.

Common move. Approve the bonus because the first consequence is the one the VP can see. Or reject it with a speech about "culture," which Elena cannot take upstairs.

Better move. Name one abuse case ("they'll split tickets") and hope that counts as thinking. It is a start. A single abuse case, unowned, dies in the hallway.

Expert move. Walk two steps: if we pay for speed, then people close faster, and then quality hides, and then the VP gets a fake green, and then we are punished later for a fire we cannot see on the dashboard. Offer a number that is harder to game, or a bonus on a mix, or a time-boxed pilot with an audit.

Resourceful move. Run And Then What on the bonus and on doing nothing. Doing nothing has a second-order too: the VP invents a worse metric. You are not choosing between purity and corruption. You are choosing which machine you will live in. Cross-link the Process Map and Why / System / Incentive from Level 5. Then recommend a design, not a vibe.

Why the common move fails: first-order thinking is how good people build bad machines. The VP is not evil. The VP is downstream of a number.

What can go wrong with the expert move: you become the person who can see three steps ahead and therefore never ships a pilot. Second-order thinking is a lamp, not a brake for every two-way door.

The idea

The Big Idea

Every choice has a first consequence you can sell, and a second consequence that collects.

And Then What is the habit of walking one door further — then one more — until you see the incentive, the workaround, and the bill. You will not see the whole future. You will see enough to stop being surprised by a machine you built.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

First-order: what happens next. Second-order: what happens because of that. Third: what the system looks like when people adapt.

People adapt. That is the whole lesson. They adapt around dashboards, around household rules, around "quick replies," around a parent who only notices grades, around a manager who only notices speed. Level 5's Five Whys, used honestly, often lands on an incentive. This lesson asks for that landing before you install the incentive.

Munger liked to talk about incentives as the strongest force in a room. You do not need to treat that as physics. You need to treat it as a default: if you pay for X, you will get more of X, including the fake X. If you punish the reporting of bad news, you will get less news, not less bad.

Cross-link the systems work: a Process Map (5.4) shows where the workaround will appear. Why / System / Incentive (5.5) shows why a "people problem" is often a scoreboard problem. Door Type (7.2) tells you how expensive it is to install a bad scoreboard — culture is a slow one-way door.

Kahneman's fast system stops at the first consequence because the first consequence is a story. "Bonus, then faster tickets, then happier customers" is a story. The hyphenated name is a fact that does not fit the story.

Buffett's long game is second-order by temperament: what does this do to the next ten years of trust? Taleb: what adaptation makes the system more fragile — more efficient, less slack, one dashboard away from a lie? Simon: you cannot tree out every branch; you can refuse the branches where the obvious adaptation is a cheat.

A note on "unintended consequences": some are truly unseen. Many are unspoken because they would slow the vibe. The premortem (7.4) and And Then What are neighbors. Premortem asks "why did it die?" And Then What asks "what machine did we build by succeeding?"

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: And Then What

Use it before you install a rule, a metric, a bonus, a public statement, a household policy, a "quick" product change, a hire made to soothe a room.

  1. Name the choice and the first consequence you are buying. "Weekly bonus for fast closes → dashboard goes green."
  2. Ask And Then What, twice, out loud or on paper. Force a human adaptation at each step, not a weather report. "And then people split tickets. And then the queue is fake and real issues age in disguise."
  3. Name the incentive in one line. What does a reasonable person do to win this game? If the winning move is a cheat, you designed a cheat.
  4. Find the workaround on the Process Map. Where in the machine will the cheat live? If you cannot point at the step, you do not understand the machine yet. Sketch it.
  5. Label the bill. Who pays later — customer, junior, future you, a different team? When?
  6. Design a correction or pick a different first consequence. Harder-to-game metric, audit sample, sunset on the bonus, mix of speed and reopen rate, slack that is not punished. Or walk away.
  7. Run the same two steps on doing nothing. The empty choice is also a machine.

Card:

FIRST CONSEQUENCE → AND THEN WHAT? → AND THEN WHAT? → WHO PAYS?

Worked on Elena's bonus:

StepWhat happens
ChoiceBonus for time-to-close under target
FirstCloses get faster; VP sees green
And thenTickets split, reopened, closed on the customer, not for the customer
And thenReal defects hide; hyphen case becomes three "wins"; Jonah stops reporting ugly truth

Who paysCustomer, then the VP in a quarter, then Elena when the fire is "sudden"
CorrectionMeasure time-to-stay-closed; sample 10 tickets a week by a human; bonus on a mix; 30-day pilot with an audit; or no bonus, a staffing ask instead

A household miniature: "No screens after 9" → first: quieter evenings. And then: screens at 8:50 in a panic, or screens in the bathroom. And then: the rule becomes a game, and the thing you wanted (sleep, a conversation) never had a time of its own. Correction: replace the ban with a positive default (book on the table, phone in a drawer) and a two-way trial.

A career miniature: "Take the stretch role for the title" → first: a better line on the resume. And then: a year of firefighting, no compounding of skill. And then: you are the person who has titles and no depth, and the next door is narrower. Who pays: future you. This is Buffett's lens without a stock.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

In a meeting, when a first-order idea is about to win:

"And then what — the version where a reasonable person games this?"

Then one more: "Who pays in three months?"

If nobody can answer, you do not install the bonus today. Two-way version: a two-week pilot with an audit, not a culture change.

Holding sentence:

"I can see the first consequence. I need two steps further before I say yes."

The hack

The Hack

Invert the metric. Ask: how would I look like a hero on this scoreboard without doing the job?

If you can describe the cheat in one minute, someone in the system will find it in a week. Design against that person. That person is not a villain. That person is you on a tired Thursday.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is good at generating abuse cases. It is bad at knowing which abuse cases your actual humans can run.

Use AI for:

  • Ten ways a reasonable person would game this rule
  • A second-order chain you can edit
  • Drafting a harder-to-game alternative metric

Do not use AI for:

  • A fake causal model of your company
  • A moral lecture about capitalism
  • Inventing what "the data usually shows"

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Role: Second-order clerk.
Choice we are about to make:
"""
[the rule, metric, bonus, policy]
"""
The first consequence we are buying:
[one line]
How the work actually happens (facts):
[three to six steps of the real process]

Tasks:
1) Walk AND THEN WHAT twice. At each step, name a reasonable human adaptation, not a weather event.
2) Write the winning cheat in one sentence.
3) Who pays, and when?
4) Offer two redesigned versions: (a) a two-way pilot with an audit, (b) a different first consequence that is harder to game.
5) Run AND THEN WHAT twice on doing nothing.

Constraints: No TED-talk cadence. No invented studies. Mark guesses as guesses.

What to verify: Whether the cheat is actually possible in your tools. A model will suggest ticket-splitting even if your system forbids it — or miss the cheat that your system invites.

Do not paste: HR bonus rules with names, customer records, anything that identifies who is "gaming" today.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "see the machine and the scoreboard together."

Category examples:

  • A process sketch on a whiteboard or in a simple diagram tool
  • Dashboards that show the mix (close rate and reopen rate), not a single vanity number
  • Sampling: a weekly human read of ten tickets beats another chart
  • For personal life: a calendar, not an app that gamifies streaks you can fake

When not to use technology: adding a second dashboard to police the first is how you get two cheats. Prefer a human sample and a sunset.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Stopping at the first good consequence and calling the rest "execution risk."

Execution risk is what you say when you do not want to look at the incentive. If the winning move is a cheat, you do not have an execution problem. You have a design problem.

The cousin mistake: seeing second-order effects everywhere and using them to block every two-way experiment. A two-week pilot with an audit is how you learn the second order in the world. Analysis is not the only lamp.

Recovery: write the cheat in a sentence. If you cannot, you are still in the press release. If you can, you are ready to design.

Defense

The Defense

And Then What fails when:

  • The chain is just anxiety in a costume ("and then I die alone"). Bring it back to a machine and a person adapting.
  • You are not willing to name the VP as part of the machine. Then you will design a bonus that soothes a hallway and call it leadership.
  • The second order is moral and you want a metric to hide it. Paying people to hide harm is not a dashboard problem. Do not use this tool to make it one.
  • You confuse second-order with "unintended" in a way that removes authorship. If the cheat was obvious in one minute, it was not a twist of fate.

Limitation: the world will still surprise you. This tool reduces stupid surprise — the kind you could have walked to in two questions.

Council

The Masters' Table

Charlie Munger. Incentives. If you remember one lens from him in this lesson, remember that a reasonable person will follow the scoreboard, including off a cliff.

Level 5's systems work (Deming's ghost, without the cult): most "people problems" are machine problems. And Then What is how a decision installs a machine.

Warren Buffett. The long-term bill — reputation, compounding, the customer who does not come back and does not tell you why.

Nassim Taleb. Efficiency that eats slack is a second-order fragility. A green dashboard with no idle is often a lie in waiting.

Herbert Simon. You cannot map the whole tree. You can refuse the branch where the obvious adaptation is a cheat.

Daniel Kahneman. The first consequence is the coherent story. The hyphen is the fact.

Synthesis. Design for the human who wants to win the game you published. Then decide if that game is the job.

Where the council could be wrong. Munger can make you cynical — not everyone cheats; some people do the job despite the scoreboard, and they are exhausted. Buffett's horizon can excuse delay. Taleb can make you hoard slack until nothing ships. Use two steps, a cheat sentence, and a two-way pilot. Leave the worldview.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 9:10 a.m.

Calm. Pause. You are not picking Elena vs Jonah as people.

Observe. VP hallway. A number that can be gamed. Jonah already sees the cheat. Mara lives in the queue. Monday is a political clock.

Define. The job is to not install a machine that trains a lie, and to not leave Elena empty-handed in a hallway.

Verify. Can tickets be split and reopened in this system? How is time-to-close counted? One factual check, not a philosophy.

Prioritize. Customer truth first. Elena's Monday second. Jonah's tone last.

Find resources / people. Whoever owns the ticket system; one support lead who will tell you the current workaround without a bonus.

Options. Bonus as designed. No bonus. Mix metric. Pilot plus sample. Staffing ask instead of a bonus.

Risks. Pure no makes Elena look unserious. Pure yes builds the cheat. A complicated metric nobody understands is incense.

Choose. Two-way: a 30-day pilot on a mix (time-to-stay-closed, plus a weekly sample of ten tickets read by a human), no cash bonus until the sample exists. If the VP needs a gesture, staff the weekend, do not buy a fake green.

Communicate. "The first consequence of a speed bonus is a green dashboard. And then people close the clock, not the problem. And then we go blind. I will not recommend that machine. I recommend a 30-day two-way pilot: measure stay-closed, sample ten tickets a week, review the cheat in this room on day 30. If we need a Monday sentence for the VP: we are serious about support, which is why we are not paying people to hide it."

Act. Write the mix in one line. Put the sample on a calendar. Name the owner.

Document. The And Then What chain, next to the Decision 7 if this is a real lock.

Adapt. If the sample shows no cheat, you can still refuse a bonus for other reasons — or accept a small one. Let the world talk.

Reflect. Did you use second-order talk to dodge Elena's political need? If yes, add a Monday sentence that is true.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the metric that will be loved upstairs. Situation: a simple number, a bonus or a ranking, a VP who wants seriousness. What most people do: install it; call the later fire "a people problem." Why it fails: you paid for the fire. Resourceful response: And Then What twice; a mix; a human sample; a sunset. Words to use: "The winning cheat is…" "Who pays in three months?" Words to avoid: "We'll have to watch for abuse" (incense, no design). Tool: And Then What + Process Map. AI assist: ten cheats, then you keep the ones your tools actually allow. What could go wrong: you stall a two-way staffing fix because you are busy being wise about metrics. If the real need is people, ask for people.

Family — the new household rule. Situation: chores, screens, money, a child's grades. A rule that would look good on a fridge. What most people do: announce the first consequence ("we'll be a family who…"). Why it fails: the workaround becomes the culture. Resourceful response: walk two steps; replace a ban with a default; trial for two weeks. Words to use: "If we make this the game, the winning move is…" Words to avoid: "Because I said so" as the whole design (sometimes you still need a boundary; you still should see the workaround). Tool: And Then What. AI assist: none at the table. After, maybe a list of defaults that are not punishments. What could go wrong: using second-order talk to avoid a necessary one-way boundary (safety). Do not workshop a lock on a door that must close.

Money — the rewards card, the "free" trial, the raise that requires a lifestyle. Situation: a first consequence that is a gift. What most people do: take the gift; meet the future bill as a surprise. Why it fails: the second order was in the contract. Resourceful response: And Then What on the annual fee, the spend you will invent to "earn" the points, the raise that becomes a car. Words to use: "And then we need this every month." Words to avoid: "It's free if you use it right" without a number. Tool: And Then What + Door Type (annual plans are often mixed). AI assist: list the later fees; verify in the actual terms. What could go wrong: you premortem a $4 trial for forty minutes. Match depth to door.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

The VP is now in the 9:10. "I'm tired of theory. Bonus by Monday or I will put the number on the all-hands slide myself." Jonah says "then you'll get a fake company." Elena looks at you with a look that means do not make this worse.

And Then What, compressed, without a sermon:

"If the number goes on the slide, the first consequence is fear. And then tickets get pretty. And then I cannot tell you the truth in this room. I will not help you build that. What I can do by Monday: a stay-closed number, a sample of ten tickets, and a staffing gap in one page. If you need a slide, slide that. If you override, I will still sample, so we are not blind. I will not pretend a speed bonus is seriousness."

If they override, you run the sample anyway. You document the chain. You do not sabotage the bonus in the queue — that is a different cheat. You tell Mara, privately, that quality still counts when she talks to you. You have just installed a counter-machine. Small. Human. Two-way.

Reflect

Reflection

  • What scoreboard in your life is already training a cheat?
  • Where have you called a design problem "a people problem"?
  • What first consequence are you currently selling that you would not buy if you had to live in the second?
  • Who pays the bill for your current yes — and did they get a vote?
  • When has second-order thinking been a costume for fear of a two-way trial?
Remember

Remember This

If a reasonable person can win your game by cheating, you designed a cheat. Walk two doors down before you lock it.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Second-order effects are an old theme with a lot of bad poetry around them. You will hear "the law of unintended consequences" as if it were a law. It is a reminder. Some consequences are unintended because the world is thick. Some are unintended because nobody was willing to say the cheat in the meeting.

Economics has a cleaner cousin: people respond to incentives, and goodhart's-law-style failure (when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure) is the dashboard version. Goodhart's original setting was monetary policy; the popular version is a workplace proverb. Treat it as a proverb that keeps being useful, not as a theorem you can stamp on every number. You still need numbers. You need numbers that are hard to win without doing the job, plus a human sample, plus a sunset.

Systems from Level 5 belong here. If you skip the Process Map, And Then What becomes a campfire story. If you only map the process and never ask who wins the game, you get a neat diagram of a cheat.

A harder variation: second-order on kindness. A manager who always covers for late work buys loyalty first, then a team that cannot tell time, then a Mara who carries the hyphen at 10 p.m. A parent who always rescues a forgotten assignment buys relief, then a child who does not pack, then a fight about character that was always a machine. The tool is the same. The feelings are thicker. Still walk two doors.

A second harder variation: cross-team bills. Your green dashboard is another team's fire. If who pays is not in the room, upgrade the decision toward one-way until they are.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24 hours, pick one rule, metric, or "simple fix" you can influence.

Run And Then What twice. Write the winning cheat in one sentence. Change one thing in the design — mix the metric, add a sample, sunset it, or replace a ban with a default.

Tell one other person the cheat sentence. If you cannot say it to them, you are still in the press release.

Sources to Explore

  • Charles T. Munger, Poor Charlie's Almanack — incentives as a working lens, including the ways they go feral.
  • W. Edwards Deming, Out of the Crisis, 1982 — a hard look at targets, fear, and systems; steal the caution, not a costume.
  • Marilyn Strathern's popularization of Goodhart's law (and Charles Goodhart's original 1975 monetary-policy formulation) — when a measure becomes a target.
  • Level 5 of this course — Process Map; Why / System / Incentive; the machine before the person.
  • Warren E. Buffett, Berkshire letters — reputation and the bill that arrives after the quarter.
  • Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011 — why the first, coherent consequence is the one you will want to stop at.
