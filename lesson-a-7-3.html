Risk, Probability, and Bad Luck

A good decision can still hurt

26 minThe Outcome Audit4,185 words22 sections
Skill

The Outcome Audit
Result
You stop rewriting history after a lucky win
In one line
Judge the bet, not the hand you were dealt after.
The idea

The Hook

Two drivers.

One of them had three drinks, drove home at 1:40 a.m., and parked between the lines. In the kitchen he said he was fine. He was fine. The street was empty. The outcome was clean.

The other had water with dinner, left at 9:10, and was hit by a car that ran a light. Hospital. A month of paperwork. A story that will sound, in the telling, like the universe punishing the wrong person.

If you use outcomes to grade decisions, the first driver is a genius of risk and the second is a cautionary tale about leaving the house.

That grading is how people learn to be lucky and call it skill. It is also how people abandon a good process because the world, once, was rude.

This lesson has one job: make the difference between a bad decision and a good decision with bad luck so clear that you can feel it in your own last year.

The room

The Scenario

Monday, 12:30 p.m. A cheap lunch. Mara and a former colleague, Priya.

Four months ago Mara took a job after a Decision 7 she actually wrote down. The manager checked out. The work was real. She did not bet the rent. In month three the company announced a "restructure." Her team is on the list. She will land. It will still hurt.

Priya took a flashy role the same month with no written process, a founder she found "magnetic," and a salary that required the equity to mean something. The equity, for now, means something. Priya is glowing.

"See, you overthought it. I just jumped. You have to trust the universe a little."

Mara can feel the shame arriving on time. She did the procedure. She got the layoff. Priya did the vibe. She got the party.

Elena, later, in a 1:1: "Maybe you're just unlucky with companies. Maybe take the next thing faster."

What does Mara believe about her last decision — and about Priya's?

Your move

Your Move

Do not skip this. Write one sentence: was Mara's decision bad? Was Priya's good? Then keep going.

Common move. Believe the scoreboard. Mara's process is now "overthinking." Priya's process is now "instinct." Next time Mara will jump. Next time Priya will jump farther.

Better move. Notice luck, vaguely. "Well, anything can happen." Vague luck is how people stay uneducated. It comforts Mara for a day and teaches her nothing.

Expert move. Split process from outcome on both lives. Mara: good process, bad (or mixed) outcome — do not abandon the process. Priya: weak process, good outcome — do not copy the process. Update the assumptions (this sector, this stage of company) without throwing out the Decision 7.

Resourceful move. Run an Outcome Audit on paper, for both, and for one of your own. Then look at the size of the bets. Priya's win is not only luck. It is luck on a bet that may still have a fat tail — a later round that does not come, a founder who stays magnetic until the books open. Taleb's caution, not Taleb's brand: a good-looking path can still be one that blows up later. Never bet the house. Insurance exists because good people still have bad weeks.

Why the common move fails: it trains you to copy winners and abandon thinkers. Markets, careers, and family arguments are full of winners who cannot tell you what they did.

What can go wrong with the expert move: you use "bad luck" as a blanket so you never see a real process error. Mara's Decision 7 might have underweighted sector risk. That is an update, not a confession that procedure is for cowards.

The idea

The Big Idea

A decision is a bet made with the information you had. An outcome is what the world did after.

You can have:

  • a good decision and a good outcome (skill, plus some luck you should not worship)
  • a good decision and a bad outcome (bad luck — keep the process, update the inputs)
  • a bad decision and a good outcome (luck — do not copy the process)
  • a bad decision and a bad outcome (deserved, as far as process goes — change the process)

Annie Duke calls judging a decision by its outcome resulting. The word is useful. The habit is expensive. This level will not work if you keep resulting.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Probability, without the costume.

You almost never know the true odds. You can still think in ranges: "more like 30% than 80%," "this fails often enough that I need a floor," "this almost never happens, but if it happens I am done." That last sentence is the fat tail. A fat tail is not a personality. It is a shape: rare events that are so large they dominate the story. Taleb's useful warning is that you cannot average ruin. If a bet can take you out of the game, the expected-value story is a bedtime story.

Expected value, lite: if you faced the same choice many times, what would the average result tend toward? A 70% chance of a modest gain and a 30% chance of a modest loss can be a good bet. A 95% chance of a bonus and a 5% chance of bankruptcy is not a good bet for a household, even if a spreadsheet calls the average pretty. The spreadsheet does not live in your house.

Insurance is the unfashionable version of this. You will "lose" most years you buy it. That is not a bad decision. You are paying to cut off a tail you cannot survive. Career bets work the same way: a stretch role with six months of savings is a different bet than the same role with six weeks of savings. Same job. Different ruin.

Kahneman and Tversky showed that people do not treat probabilities cleanly. We overweight a vivid story (the colleague who jumped and won) and underweight a base rate (how often this kind of company lays people off in year one). We feel losses more than gains, which is why Mara's shame is louder than Priya's luck is informative.

Von Neumann, lightly: if you could put numbers on every branch, you could pick the branch with the best weighted outcome. You cannot. You can still refuse to treat a single branch that happened as the quality of the tree.

Buffett's long-term lens: do not take bets that interrupt compounding. Munger's inversion: show me how I get ruined, and I will not go there. Simon: you will not compute the full tree; you will satisfice — but satisfice with ruin taken off the table.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Outcome Audit

Use it after any result that is trying to teach you too fast — a win you want to repeat, a loss you want to repent.

  1. Write the decision as it was, then. Date, options you saw, what you knew, what you assumed. If you have a Decision 7 page, use it. If you do not, admit you are reconstructing, which means you are already in danger of myth. (7.7 exists because of this step.)
  2. Grade the process, not the result. Did you name a want? Separate facts from assumptions? List three options? Check ruin? Match speed to door type? Yes/no, not a vibe.
  3. Name the outcome, separately. What happened, in facts. No moral.
  4. Place it in the four-box.
Good outcomeBad outcome
Good processDeserved-ish win. Keep the process. Do not get drunk on it.Bad luck. Keep the process. Update the inputs. Do not take the shame as data about your character.
Bad processLuck. Do not copy. Do not give a talk. Ask what the tail still holds.Deserved miss. Change the process. Do not call it a cursed year.
  1. Ask the luck question out loud: If I ran this bet ten times, would I still like it? If yes, keep it. If you only like the one time it worked, you are resulting.
  2. Check ruin. Could this bet have taken the house — health, rent, reputation you cannot rebuild, a relationship you cannot unsay? If yes, it was a worse process than the outcome suggests, even if you won.
  3. Write one update. A new base rate, a new floor, a new assumption to check. One. Not a new personality.

Mara's lunch, audited:

  • Process: written Decision 7, ruin checked, did not bet the rent, manager diligence. Good enough to keep.
  • Outcome: restructure in month three. Bad.
  • Box: good process, bad outcome. Bad luck, plus a possible underweighted base rate on "companies of this size in this year."
  • Update: next time, add a sector/layoff base rate to question 2. Do not add "trust the universe."
  • Priya: weak process, good outcome so far. Luck. The tail is not finished. Do not copy. Do not preach at her; just do not take the sermon.

Card:

Process First. Outcome Second. Ruin Always. One Update.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

When someone — including you — is about to learn the wrong lesson from a result, four lines:

  1. What did I know then?
  2. Was ruin on the table?
  3. Which box is this?
  4. What is the one update?

Holding sentence, in a room that is worshipping a lucky win:

"That's a good outcome. I want to know if the process is one we'd repeat if it had gone the other way."

Holding sentence after a good process hurt:

"The result is bad. I am not going to pretend the procedure caused the layoff unless we find the actual miss."

The hack

The Hack

When you win, write the failure that almost happened. When you lose, write the process you would repeat.

This is cheap, slightly annoying, and it blocks the two lies: "I am a genius" and "I am cursed."

If you cannot name a failure that almost happened, you are not looking. If you cannot name a process you would repeat, you are drowning in the outcome.

AI move

The AI Move

AI can help you reconstruct a process honestly. It cannot tell you whether you were unlucky. It will, if you let it, write a story that protects your ego or attacks it, depending on the prompt.

Use AI for:

  • Turning a messy recap into a then-vs-now fact list
  • Generating base rates you must then verify (layoff rates, failure rates, return rates)
  • A premortem on a lucky win: "how does this still blow up?"

Do not use AI for:

  • A pep talk after a loss
  • A takedown of a colleague who got lucky
  • Fake statistics about "how often this happens"

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Role: Outcome auditor. No pep talk, no scolding.
The decision I made then:
"""
[what I knew, options, what I chose, date]
"""
The outcome now:
"""
[facts only]
"""
Tasks:
1) Separate PROCESS elements from OUTCOME elements.
2) Place this in the four-box (good/bad process × good/bad outcome). Mark if the process grade is incomplete because I did not write it down then.
3) List luck factors that could explain the outcome without justifying the process.
4) Ruin check: could this bet have taken me out of the game?
5) Offer THREE possible updates to the next Decision 7. Do not offer a new personality.
6) Ask me one question that would change the process grade.

Constraints: No invented percentages. If you mention a base rate, label it a guess and tell me where a human should look.

What to verify: Any number the model offers. Base rates from a model are not base rates. Look them up. Level 6.

Do not paste: other people's performance reviews, medical details, anything that identifies a colleague as the "lucky idiot" in a system you do not control.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "keep score of process, not of mythology."

Category examples:

  • A dated note or Decision Page (7.7) so the audit has a then
  • A simple four-box you reuse, paper or sheet
  • Calendar review dates, so outcomes get audited on purpose instead of in a lunch ambush
  • For money: actual insurance, emergency fund, position sizing — tail-cutters, not apps that gamify bets

When not to use technology: a dashboard of outcomes with no process column will make resulting faster. You will optimize the scoreboard. That is Priya's sermon with charts.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Calling every painful result a bad decision, and every pleasant result a good one.

The first creates shame and process-hopping. The second creates legends and copycats. Both are cheap.

The cousin mistake: calling everything luck, which is how people avoid both credit and responsibility. Luck is a box, not a worldview.

Recovery: force the four-box on paper. If you cannot grade the process, that is a process miss — you did not write it down. Fix that next time. Do not invent a grade that makes the story nicer.

Defense

The Defense

The Outcome Audit fails when:

  • You grade your own process like a lawyer. Get a second reader who was there, or the page you wrote then.
  • The world is not a casino with independent hands. Careers have one path. You cannot actually run the bet ten times. The "ten times" question is a thought tool, not a lab.
  • Fat tails make "average" silly. If the downside is ruin, stop talking like a card counter.
  • Someone uses "bad luck" to avoid a moral miss. Luck is not a defense for a lie, a cruelty, or a bet you had no right to place with other people's money or safety.
  • You are in a domain where skill is real and large (a surgeon's checklist, a pilot's procedure). Do not become so philosophical about luck that you stop practicing. Luck explains the residue, not the whole craft.

Limitation: this will not make you emotionally even. Mara will still feel the lunch. The tool's job is to stop her from torching a good procedure because Priya is glowing.

Council

The Masters' Table

Annie Duke. Resulting — judging the bet by the hand. If you only remember one popular name for this error, remember that.

Daniel Kahneman. The fast system loves a coherent story. Coherence is not odds. A layoff after diligence is a story your brain will try to make into "diligence is for fools."

Nassim Taleb. Fat tails, luck, and ruin. Use him as a caution: do not treat a path that can blow you up as a clever expected value. Do not become a person who says "black swan" instead of doing the audit.

Warren Buffett. Never lose the money you need. The long game is mostly not interrupting compounding with a heroic bet.

Charlie Munger. Invert: how do I get ruined? Take those bets off the table, then play.

Herbert Simon. You will not compute the tree. You can still refuse the branches that end in "and then we cannot pay rent."

John von Neumann (lightly). Weighted outcomes are the ancestor of this lesson. You will not do the math. You will still think in "how often" and "how bad."

Synthesis. Process is what you can repeat. Outcome is what you must survive. Ruin is what you do not get to average.

Where the council could be wrong. Taleb can make you timid in two-way doors. Duke's poker frame can make you glib about losses that are not chips — grief is not a bad beat. Buffett's wealth was not only process; it was also a particular market and a long life. Do not copy anyone's risk budget. Copy the refusal to grade a decision by a lunch.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to the lunch, then the 1:1.

Calm. Mara does not defend her character to Priya. Shame is weather (Level 1). It is not a process grade.

Observe. Two outcomes. Two processes. One glowing sermon. Elena, later, offering "maybe take the next thing faster."

Define. The job is to keep a good procedure and update one input — not to win lunch.

Verify. What did Mara actually write four months ago? If the page exists, read it. If it does not, she is already in 7.7 territory.

Prioritize. Ruin is not on the table (she will land). Shame is. Elena's lesson, if swallowed, would be the expensive one.

Find resources / people. Not Priya, for this. A page, a mentor who grades process, a base rate on this kind of company.

Options. Accept Priya's sermon. Argue. Or audit, privately, then answer Elena with a process sentence.

Risks. Arguing with Priya creates a second story ("Mara is bitter"). Silent agreement trains Elena to value speed over doors.

Choose. Audit. Keep the process. Add a base-rate check next time. Do not copy Priya. Do not punish herself with a faster, dumber next bet.

Communicate. To Priya, light: "I'm glad it worked. I still wouldn't take a bet I couldn't survive if it didn't." To Elena: "The last choice was a good process with a bad quarter. I will not 'take the next thing faster' if faster means skipping ruin. I will add a layoff base rate to the next Decision 7. I want your help on that, not on becoming Priya."

Act. Open the old page. Write the four-box. One update.

Document. The audit next to the original Decision 7.

Adapt. If the original page shows she did skip a checkable assumption, that is a process miss. Name it. That is honesty, not resulting.

Reflect. Did she want Priya to be wrong more than she wanted to learn? That want is human. It is not a method.

Practice

Practice

Do this now. Twelve minutes.

Pick two results from the last two years: one that felt like a win, one that stung.

Run both through the four-box. For each, write:

  1. Process grade (good/bad/unknown) and why, without using the outcome as evidence
  2. Outcome in one factual sentence
  3. Box
  4. Ruin check (yes/no)
  5. One update, or "no update, luck"

If you graded process as "unknown," that is the lesson: you cannot improve what you mythologize. Start a Decision Page this week (7.7).

Then write one sentence you will say the next time a Priya lectures you.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the lucky hero. Situation: a colleague skipped diligence, shipped, and the demo landed. They are now the method. What most people do: copy the skip, or seethe. Why it fails: you will copy a tail you have not seen yet. Resourceful response: congratulate the outcome; ask what they would repeat if it had failed; keep your process; add any real input you missed. Words to use: "Good outcome. What's the repeatable part?" Words to avoid: "You got lucky" in public (true, often, and expensive to say). Tool: Outcome Audit, privately. AI assist: premortem on their win, for your eyes. What could go wrong: you become the team's wet blanket. Audit yourself first so you are not just jealous.

Money — insurance and the "wasted" year. Situation: you paid for health, renter, or disability coverage and "didn't use it." A relative says you wasted the money. What most people do: cancel to feel clever, or keep it without being able to say why. Why it fails: you are resulting on a tail that did not show up. Resourceful response: insurance is a good process whose good outcome is often "nothing happened." That is the product. Words to use: "I am paying to not be ruined. A quiet year is a success." Words to avoid: "I have a good feeling this year." Tool: Outcome Audit + ruin check. AI assist: none for the policy language — read it. A model can list questions, not coverage. What could go wrong: you over-insure trivia and call it Taleb. Cut tails that ruin you, not every bruise.

Family — the sober driver who still got hit. Situation: you did the careful thing; the world did not care; someone uses it as proof that care is pointless. What most people do: a speech about fairness, or a swing toward recklessness. Why it fails: fairness is not a process grade. Resourceful response: name the box. Keep the process. Grieve the outcome without torching the method. Words to use: "That was bad luck. I am not going to learn 'don't bother' from it." Words to avoid: "Everything happens for a reason." Tool: Outcome Audit. Pause if the room is hot. AI assist: none in the grief. Later, if a practical claim is in dispute (insurance, fault), facts only. What could go wrong: using "bad luck" to skip a real miss (a turn you should not have made). If there was a miss, name it. That is still not Priya's sermon.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Priya's sermon is now in The Room. Elena's VP is there. "Priya just jumped. Mara overthought it and still got cut. I want more Priyas." Jonah, who likes a blunt instrument, says, "Process is what people say when they're scared."

You are Mara, or you are the person sitting next to her.

The Outcome Audit has to be said in a sentence that can survive a VP:

"We should not copy a process we would not repeat on a loss. Priya's outcome is good. If we are going to make it the method, I want the ruin case on the table: what happens to the next five people who jump with six weeks of savings and a magnetic founder. Mara's process did not cause a restructure. I will not train this team to treat luck as a strategy. I will train it to take two-way bets fast and one-way bets with a floor."

If they overrule you, you still do not torch Mara's procedure in your own life. You document the room's choice. You do not become Priya to stay loved.

If you are Mara, you do not litigate your character in that meeting. BLUF: "I can show the Decision 7 I wrote. I will use it again. I want help on the next bet, not a personality transplant." Then you leave before you perform shame.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Which of your legends is a lucky outcome with a weak process?
  • Which of your shames is a good process with a rude world?
  • What bet are you currently treating as skill that you could not survive ten times?
  • Where is ruin still on the table in your household, dressed as opportunity?
  • If Priya is glowing in your actual life, what sermon are you tempted to believe?
Remember

Remember This

A good decision can hurt. A bad decision can pay. Judge the bet — and never bet the house.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

"Resulting" is new language for an old error. Philosophers talked about moral luck. Statisticians talked about confusing a draw with a distribution. Poker players had to learn it or go broke. Duke's Thinking in Bets (2018) is the clean popular account; you do not need the rest of the poker.

Tetlock's work (next lesson's neighbor, 7.6) adds a second blade: people who are very sure and very public are often not better at odds. They are better at stories. A story of a win is how Priya convinces a VP. A story of a loss is how Mara convinces herself she is foolish. Calibration is how you stop letting stories set the odds.

On Taleb: read him for the ruin point and for the insult to fake precision. Do not import the whole act. "Black swan" is now a way to sound like you predicted the unpredictable. In this course it means: do not use the average of a process that can kill you. Cut the tail (insurance, savings floor, no career bet that strands the household), then take the remaining risks like an adult.

On expected value: it is a good servant in repeated, bounded domains — pricing, some operations, whether to buy the extended warranty on a $40 cable (usually no). It is a bad master in unique, ruinous, or moral domains. You will not compute the expected value of telling the truth to Elena. You will still not bet the house.

A harder drill: write a luck resume. One page, two columns: outcomes you privately know were luck; processes you would repeat even if they had failed. If the first column is longer, you have been resulting. If the second is empty, you do not yet have a method.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 48 hours, run a full Outcome Audit on one real result — a win or a loss — from the last year.

Place it in the four-box. Write the ruin check. Write one update, or write "luck, no update."

If someone offers you a Priya sermon this week, use one holding sentence and do not argue for more than a minute. Save the argument for the page.

Sources

Sources to Explore

  • Annie Duke, Thinking in Bets, 2018 — resulting, and the habit of judging the quality of a bet apart from the hand.
  • Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, prospect theory and related papers; Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011 — how outcomes and vivid stories distort the odds you think you saw.
  • Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Fooled by Randomness, 2001, and The Black Swan, 2007 — luck, narrative, fat tails; use as caution, not as identity.
  • Philip E. Tetlock, Expert Political Judgment, 2005 — how often confident experts fail as forecasters; a bridge into 7.6.
  • Warren E. Buffett, Berkshire letters — ruin, patience, and not interrupting compounding.
  • Glenn W. Brier, "Verification of forecasts expressed in terms of probability," Monthly Weather Review, 1950 — the ancestor of scoring probabilistic honesty, which you will meet as a concept in 7.6.
