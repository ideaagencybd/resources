Automate the Boring, Keep the Judgment

Workflows without building a fragile Rube Goldberg machine

Automate the boring. Keep a human on the part that can be wrong.
The idea

The Hook

There is a particular kind of tired that is not from hard work.

It is from doing the same copy-paste forty times: the form, the folder, the "just circling back," the spreadsheet cell that could have moved itself. Your brain is a terrible engine for identical steps. It drifts. It invents a better song. It skips row 18.

Automation is how you stop spending a human on a machine's job.

It is also how you build a machine that sends the wrong invoice to forty customers while you sleep, grants a helper-bot the keys to your mail, or loops until the credit card is a smoking crater.

The resourceful rule is small: Trigger, Action, Check. Automate the boring. Keep a human on the judgment step. If you cannot name the check, you are not automating. You are hoping.

The room

The Scenario

Elena, Friday 4:50 p.m. "I don't want anyone on this team spending Monday on status mail. Make it automatic."

Jonah has a tab open: an automation platform, an AI agent demo, and a browser agent that can "just use the site like a person." He is delighted.

"I'll have it read the sheet, write the emails, send them, and file the replies. Fully hands-off. That's the point of agents."

Mara: "The sheet still has two Wests. And last week's numbers were wrong. And legal said we don't auto-send to customers."

Jonah: "That's why the model is in the loop. It can judge."

Elena looks at you. "I need hours back. I do not need a new way to be wrong at 2:00 a.m. What do we actually automate?"

The clock is the weekend. The sheet is dirty. The agent would like permission to send.

What do you build — and what do you refuse?

Your move

Your Move

Common move. Full send. Elena asked for automatic. You give her automatic. Monday's customers get a fluent, wrong West number. You find out from a reply-all.

Better move. Automate the draft, leave send to a human. Better. If the draft is generated from a dirty table, you have only automated the production of plausible nonsense (6.4, 6.5).

Expert move. Name the trigger, the boring action, and the check. Automate only the action that cannot quietly ruin a relationship. Put a human on send, on money, on anything irreversible.

Resourceful move. The expert move, plus a failure-mode pass: silent wrong, permission creep, looping. You write down what the bot is allowed to touch. You use a Clean Table as the trigger source. You keep the agent on a leash that 6.1 would recognize: a job, an exit, a transferable procedure.

Why the common move fails: "hands-off" is not a virtue. It is a bet that the world will stay as clean as the demo.

What can go wrong with the expert move: a check that is theater — a human who clicks Approve on twenty mails without reading. A check you will not actually do is not a check. Make it small enough to do, or do not automate the send.

The idea

The Big Idea

Trigger-Action-Check.

Every automation is three parts. The ones that blow up are missing the third, or they put a model in the third and call it a human.

  • Trigger. What event starts this? A form submit, a new row, 8:00 a.m. Monday, a label in mail.
  • Action. The boring, reversible, identical step. Copy, file, format, draft, move, notify you.
  • Check. A human (or a hard rule that cannot hallucinate) on the judgment: send, pay, delete, publish, grant access.

If the action is the judgment, you do not automate it yet.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

You already know checklists (5.3) and process maps (5.4). Automation is a checklist that runs without you — which is wonderful for steps 1–4 and dangerous for step 5 if step 5 is "is this actually true?"

Categories, then current examples (will change):

  • Native automations. Built into the tools you already have: email rules, calendar booking, spreadsheet scripts, OS shortcuts, Slack/Teams workflows, "if this folder, then that." Start here. Least lock-in, least permission sprawl.
  • iPaaS / glue tools. The category that currently includes Zapier, Make, n8n, Power Automate, and cousins. They connect apps: trigger in A, action in B. Excellent for boring glue. They are also how a token with too much power leaks across a stack.
  • AI agents and browser agents. Models that can use tools, click sites, fill forms, sometimes send. As of 2026 they are real enough to save time and real enough to fail in public. A browser agent that "uses the site like a person" can also click "confirm" like a person who did not read.

Failure modes to name before you turn it on:

Silent wrong. It runs. It looks fine. The number is last week's, the name is the other West, the invoice is the test customer. There is no error. That is the worst failure. Design a check that would catch a quiet lie, not just a crash.

Permission creep. You granted the glue tool or the agent "read mail, send mail, see drive, see the sheet." It needed a label and a draft folder. Extra permission is how a small bot becomes a large incident. Least privilege is a Security Floor idea (6.7) applied to robots.

Looping. A trigger that fires the action that creates the trigger. Mail rules that reply to the auto-reply. A sheet that adds a row when a row is added. Caps, filters, and "only if" conditions are not pedantry. They are brakes.

Taleb's lens (fragility): a system that gains efficiency by removing slack often fails worse when the world moves. Automation removes slack on purpose. So you add a check, a cap, and a way to turn it off.

What's changing. Vendors will keep selling "autonomous." Some workflows will get safer with better confirmations. Some will hide the send button further from your hand. Your rule does not depend on their adjective. If it can spend money, name a person, or leave the building, a human stays on that step until you have evidence the check can be a hard rule.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: Trigger-Action-Check

One page, one workflow.

  1. Job. One sentence. "When a form is submitted, file a row and notify me. I send the reply."
  2. Trigger. The event, plus what must be true (form complete, not a test, not a duplicate).
  3. Action. The boring steps, in order, each reversible if possible. Draft, don't send. Copy, don't delete. Label, don't archive forever.
  4. Check. Who, what they look at, how long it should take, what "approve" means. If it takes more than two minutes, you automated the wrong part or the table is dirty.
  5. Failure modes. Silent wrong: how would we notice? Permission: what is the bot not allowed to access? Loop: what stops a runaway?
  6. Off switch. Where is it, who knows it, what happens to in-flight items.
  7. Log. A row each run: time, input ID, output, who checked.

If you cannot fill 4–6, you are not ready to turn it on.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

When Elena wants "automatic" by Monday:

Pick one native rule. Example: form → new row in a Clean Table → email yourself a summary. You still send the customer mail. Hours come back from the filing, not from the judgment. Say that out loud so "automatic" does not mean "unattended send."

The hack

The Hack

Automate the preparation, never the irreversible — until the preparation has been clean for weeks.

Drafts, folders, tags, reminders, a row appended, a Slack to a human. After you have a log that looks boring, you may move the check later in the chain. You may not skip the log.

Second hack: a kill switch in the first five minutes, not as a sequel. Disconnect, uncheck, revoke token. If you cannot say how to stop it, do not start it.

AI move

The AI Move

Use AI for:

  • Drafting the Trigger-Action-Check page from a messy description
  • Listing silent-wrong and loop risks you missed
  • Writing the user-facing draft after a Clean Table is true, with 6.3's framework, still not sending

Do not use AI for:

  • Being the Check ("the agent will judge")
  • Holding unlimited tokens "so it can be helpful"
  • A browser agent on banking, HR, or production send on day one

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Context: I want to automate a boring workflow without building a fragile machine.
Job: [one sentence]
Current manual steps:
1.
2.
3.
Irreversible steps (send / pay / delete / publish / grant): [list]
Tools I already have: [native rules, glue platform, sheet, mail]
Constraints: least permission; no auto-send to customers; dirty data possible; I will spend 10 minutes on a check, not 0.

Goal: Design Trigger-Action-Check.
- Trigger with filters
- Actions that are reversible
- Check a human can actually do
- Silent-wrong, permission-creep, and looping risks
- Off switch
- A minimum version I can build this weekend

Output: a one-page design. Then a "do not automate yet" list.
Verification: Three ways this fails quietly, and how the log would show it.

What to verify: Permissions on the real tokens. A dry run with a fake customer. The off switch.

Do not paste: live API keys, customer lists, anything the agent would send.

The Technology Move

The job is "remove identical steps without removing judgment."

When not to automate: when the process is still a mess (5.4). Automating a mess scales the mess. Clean Table, then glue. Also when you do the task three times a year. A checklist is enough.

What's changing. Agents will get better at looking like they checked. A fluent "I verified the total" is not a total. Keep a formula or a human in the Check. Brands in this category will churn. The TAC page should still make sense if you switch glue tools (6.1).

Mistake

The Common Mistake

People automate the part they hate because they hate it, not because it is stable.

Hate is a clue, not a spec. If you hate sending status because the numbers are always a fight, the job is the table, not the mailer. An agent on top of a dirty West column is 6.5's problem with a stamp.

The second mistake: a 20-step machine for a 2-step job. Rube Goldberg. Each extra step is a new silent-wrong. Minimum useful version (3.7) applies.

Recovery: if it already sent garbage, turn it off first. Then retract like a human (6.4's recovery). Then fix the table. Then a narrower trigger. Do not "patch the prompt" while it is still live.

Defense

The Defense

TAC fails when:

  • The trigger is not actually well-defined ("when something important happens"). If you cannot detect it with a rule, a human is still the trigger.
  • The check becomes rubber-stamp. Then you have theater. Reduce volume or stop.
  • You are the only person who knows it exists. Then you are a single point of failure. Document the off switch for Elena.
  • Legal or safety is the domain. Auto-sending medical, legal, or financial instructions is how you get 6.4's letterhead problem at machine speed.

Limitation: this will not give you your life back if the job is chaos. It will give you back the identical twenty minutes, which is often enough to be worth it.

Council

The Masters' Table

Nassim Nicholas Taleb. Through fragility: efficiency that deletes slack can make a worse tail. Automation without a check is a tail you signed up for.

Bill Gates. Multiply the boring. Do not multiply the judgment until the boring is clean. A force multiplier on a bad number is a worse number.

Norbert Wiener. Through the cybernetic lens: a loop without a governor hunts. Caps, filters, and off switches are governors.

Grace Hopper. If you cannot explain the procedure, you cannot automate it. Write the steps as if the vendor had disappeared.

Claude Shannon. Every extra hop is a chance to inject noise. Prefer a short chain.

Synthesis. The council would automate filing, not promising; drafting, not sending; alerting, not paying — until the log is boring.

Where the council could be wrong. Some hard rules are better than tired humans (a checksum, a duplicate detector, a "do not send outside the domain" block). A governor can be code. It cannot be a vibe from a model that wants to be helpful.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 4:50 p.m.

Calm. You do not fight Jonah's excitement. You put a TAC page on the screen.

Observe. Dirty sheet. Legal bar on auto-send. Elena wants hours, not theater. Agent wants send permission.

Define. Job: remove Monday's filing and first-draft labor. Not: unattended customer mail.

Verify. Is the table a Clean Table? No. Then it is not a trigger source yet.

Prioritize. Clean the trigger data, or pick a trigger that is already clean (form submit). Human on send.

Find resources / people. Native mail + sheet first. Legal's rule. One dry-run recipient.

Options. Full agent. Nothing. Or draft-and-notify.

Risks. Silent wrong on West. Permission to send. A loop with auto-replies.

Choose. Form or a clean tab → row → draft in a folder → Slack you. You send.

Communicate. "We'll automate filing and a draft. We will not auto-send. Jonah, the model can write the draft after the numbers are in a Clean Table. Legal already answered the send question. I'll have a minimum version Monday morning, with an off switch."

Act. Weekend: native or glue, least tokens, one fake customer, log row.

Document. TAC page in the team folder. Who can turn it off.

Adapt. After two quiet weeks, consider moving the check later — not deleting it.

Reflect. Did you automate hate, or a stable step?

Practice

Practice

Do this now. Twelve minutes.

  1. Pick a chore you did at least three times in the last month.
  2. Write Trigger, Action, Check in six lines.
  3. Circle the irreversible step. If it is inside Action, move it to Check.
  4. Write one silent-wrong, one permission risk, one loop risk.
  5. Write the off switch.
  6. Name the minimum version that uses a tool you already have.

Do not build a 12-step agent on paper. If you cannot fit it on one page, it is not a first automation.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the status mail. Situation: Elena wants Monday back. What most people do: agent with send. Why it fails: dirty numbers, legal, silent wrong. Resourceful response: Clean Table, draft, human send, log. Words to use: "Automatic filing. Human send." Words to avoid: "Fully autonomous," "the agent will judge." Tool: TAC + Clean Table. What could go wrong: a draft folder that still auto-sends because of a default. Check the default.

Family — the calendar. Situation: school mails, practices, three adults. What most people do: a brittle chain across four apps. Why it fails: one renamed calendar and the chain pages the wrong parent. Resourceful response: one native rule (label → calendar hold) plus a Sunday human glance. What could go wrong: duplicate events looping. Filter "already on calendar."

Money — invoices or bills. Situation: "just auto-pay everything." Why it fails: silent wrong on amount; a fraudulent payee; a test invoice. Resourceful response: auto-notify, human pay, or auto-pay only with a hard cap and a known payee list. What could go wrong: a loop of retries that looks like a denial-of-wallet. Caps.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

The agent already sent. Forty customers. The West number was last quarter's. Two customers replied all. Legal is on the thread. Jonah says, "I'll tweak the prompt."

Off switch first. Not a prompt tweak on a live gun.

  1. Disable the workflow. Revoke send permission.
  2. State the facts to Elena: what went out, to whom, which number was wrong.
  3. Human correction: a short, true mail, not a second agent.
  4. Log: trigger, bad action, missing check.
  5. Rebuild as draft-only on a Clean Table, or do not rebuild.

You may feel the urge to "let it send an apology." That is how you get two incidents. Humans apologize. Then you document the Check so this is not a personality flaw, it is a design flaw you closed.

Reflect

Reflection

  • What identical step are you still doing out of virtue?
  • Where would silent wrong show up in a workflow you like?
  • What permissions has a helper tool been given that the job does not need?
  • If you vanished for a week, could someone find the off switch?
  • Are you automating a mess because cleaning it felt slower?
Remember

Remember This

If you cannot name the check, you are not automating. You are hoping at machine speed.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

A short history of governors.

Wiener's Cybernetics (1948) is not an automation-buying guide. It is a reminder that purpose-built loops need feedback. A thermostat that cannot sense heat is a heater with a story. Your agent that cannot sense "this number is last week's" is a mailer with a story.

Taleb's fragility is the cousin: when you remove the messy human who used to notice row 18, you must replace that noticing with something that is not a vibe. A checksum. A row count. A "does this differ from last week by more than 20%?" halt. Those are governors.

Gates's multiplication is the optimistic side, and it is real: a clean trigger and a boring action can give a small team a larger team's filing capacity. The error is multiplying before the table is clean, which 6.5 already taught.

Browser agents deserve a special caution. They fail like tired humans — mis-clicks, wrong tab, confirm on a dialog — at a speed tired humans cannot match. Use them on throwaway tasks and sandboxed accounts first. Never on the skeleton-key email (6.7) as a convenience.

If you want a harder variation, map one existing automation (even a mail filter) for looping and permission. You will usually find at least one surprise.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24–48 hours, ship one minimum automation with a human on the judgment step.

Examples: form → sheet row → notify you; mail label → saved draft; a daily summary to yourself, not to a customer.

Write the TAC page. Run it once on purpose with a fake input. Confirm you know how to turn it off.

If you already have an unattended send, add a check or disable it today.
