Write It Down

A decision that is not recorded cannot be improved

20 minThe Decision Page3,683 words22 sections
Skill
Keep a decision journal
Tool
The Decision Page
Result
You can learn from your own past instead of repeating it
In one line
You cannot improve what you mythologize later.
The idea

The Hook

Ask yourself why you took the last job.

You will get a story. It will have a clean motive, a lesson, a villain or a gift. It will sound like a person who always knew. It will be partly true and partly a novel you wrote after the ending arrived.

Mara, four months after the offer on the kitchen table, can already feel the novel. If the job is good, she "trusted herself." If the layoff comes, she "should have known." Priya's lunch (7.3) only speeds the rewrite. The Decision 7 she ran — if she ran it only in her head — is gone. The assumptions have been laundered into fate.

Memory is not a filing cabinet. It is a press office. Kahneman's work on how we tell the story of a life is the polite version. The impolite version is: you will steal credit from luck and steal blame from process, in whichever direction your current mood needs.

A Decision Page is how you give the future a document the press office is not allowed to edit.

The room

The Scenario

Sunday, 8:05 p.m. Mara at the same kitchen table. Sam is in the other room. The offer is already accepted — or already declined. You pick which; the problem is the same.

She has a notes app full of fragments: a pros/cons list from 1 a.m., a line from a reference, a screenshot of the salary, a voice note she will never replay. She does not have one page that says: on this date, I chose this, because of these reasons, expecting these things, and I will look again on this date.

Elena pings: "Proud of you either way. Let's talk career path in Q3."

Q3 is a fog. Q3 is where good intentions go to become mythology.

Sam, from the doorway: "I just hope we remember why we did this when it gets hard."

Mara: "We'll remember."

They will not, not accurately. What does she write before she sleeps?

Your move

Your Move

Do not keep reading until you have named what you would actually put on a page. Three lines is enough.

Common move. Nothing. Trust memory. Or a long journal about feelings that cannot be scored. Or a graveyard of screenshots with no decision in them.

Better move. A paragraph in a doc: "I chose X because Y." Better than nothing. Easy to write as advocacy, easy to skip the expecteds, easy to have no review date.

Expert move. A structured page: date, choice, options seen, reasons, assumptions, expecteds (what should be true in 3 / 6 / 12 months), ruin check, door type, confidence percent, review date. Short. Boring. Findable.

Resourceful move. The Decision Page as a habit, not a relic of big choices. You write it when you choose, you calendar the review, you run an Outcome Audit (7.3) against the page, not against the novel. You do not perform the page in The Room. You use it so The Room cannot own your memory.

Why the common move fails: the person who does not write it down is forced to learn from stories. Stories are how you become Priya after a win and a coward after a loss.

What can go wrong with the expert move: a template so heavy you never fill it. A page you write as a lawyer for a future HR fight. A page you never review, which is a diary, not a tool. The page is for learning and for honesty. It is not a museum and not a brief unless you are actually in a one-way legal door.

The idea

The Big Idea

If it is not written at the time, you do not have a decision. You have a myth waiting for an ending.

The Decision Page is how a bounded mind (Simon) becomes a mind that can improve. You cannot calibrate (7.6) without a then. You cannot audit luck (7.3) without a then. You cannot tell a one-way door from a mood (7.2) without a then.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Russo and Schoemaker, among others, have argued for decision journals as a way to beat hindsight. The popular version is simple and sturdy: write what you knew, what you expected, and when you will look again. Kahneman's hindsight bias is the enemy — once you know the ending, the beginning looks inevitable. Tetlock's scoring needs a timestamped percent. Klein's premortem is more useful if the causes you feared are still on a page when the real postmortem arrives.

You do not need their careers. You need a page with a date.

What to capture, and what not to:

  • Capture the choice, the options you actually saw, the want, the load-bearing assumptions, what you expect to see if you were right, the ruin you refused or accepted, the door type, a coarse confidence, the next action, the review date.
  • Do not capture a novel. Do not capture other people's private lives in a tool that syncs to a company drive. Do not capture a self-sermon.

Buffett's long game is a written game: capital allocated, thesis, what would change his mind. You are not Buffett. You can still write the thesis and the "what would change my mind." Munger inversion on a page: "I will know this was wrong if…" Taleb: if the page shows you bet the house, the page did its job before the tail.

A Decision Page is also how you are kind to Sam. "We'll remember" is a warm lie. "We will read this on March 1" is a plan.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Decision Page

One page. One choice. Dated. Stored where you will find it (a single folder, a single notebook, not seventeen apps).

Fill this, in this order:

  1. Date and decision. One sentence. "2026-03-12: Accept the stretch role at X, start April 6."
  2. Want. Outcome, not option. Constraints. The conflict if there is one.
  3. Options I actually considered. At least three, including doing nothing. If you only had two, write that — it is a process miss.
  4. What I know / what I assume. Two short lists. Circle the assumption doing the most work.
  5. Door type. Two-way / one-way / mixed, and the undo in a clause.
  6. What could go wrong. Bruise vs ruin. What I am accepting.
  7. Expecteds. Three bullets, dated: "By [date] I should see…" These are how you will score the decision without resulting. Not "I will be happy." Scorable: money, hours, a manager behavior, a product in hand, a conversation that has happened.
  8. Confidence. Coarse percent on the most important expected.
  9. Next action and who I told.
  10. Review date. On a calendar, not only on the page. When it fires, run the Outcome Audit against this page. Do not rewrite the page. Add a dated addendum.

That is the whole tool. If you are exhausted, fill 1, 4, 7, 10. That subset already beats a novel.

Card:

Date · Choice · Assume · Expecteds · Review.

Mara's page, compressed:

  • Date/decision: accept / decline, stated.
  • Want: growth and a raise without betting rent.
  • Options: take, stay+dated counter, delay start, decline+search.
  • Assume (circled): the new manager will protect her when it is ugly.
  • Door: mixed — she can leave in six months (undo exists, costly); household runway is the real door.
  • Wrong: layoff (bruise if runway holds); household cash-out (ruin — refused).
  • Expecteds: by month 3, manager has run interference once; by month 6, work is harder in a way she can name; runway still ≥ 3 months.
  • Confidence: 60% on the manager expected.
  • Next: email Elena; tell Sam the ruin line.
  • Review: first of months 3, 6, 12.

When month 3 is a restructure, she does not get to write "I always knew." She gets to read that the manager assumption was load-bearing, the ruin was refused, the process was good, the outcome was rude. Then one update. That is a mind that can improve.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

After a choice, before you sleep, four lines in a note titled with the date:

  1. I chose ___
  2. Because (facts) ___
  3. Assuming ___
  4. I will look on ___ and I expect to see ___

Set the calendar reminder in the same sitting. If you do not set it, you did not finish.

Holding sentence when someone says "we'll remember why":

"We won't. I'm writing it down. Review date is on the calendar."

The hack

The Hack

Write the expecteds before you write the advocacy paragraph.

If you start with "why this is wise," you will write expecteds that cannot fail ("I will have learned something"). Make them failable. "By September, I have shipped X" can fail. "By September, I am growing as a person" cannot. Unfailable expecteds are how pages become novels.

AI move

The AI Move

AI can format a page and catch missing pieces. It will also help you sound wiser than you were. That is a contamination of the record.

Use AI for:

  • Turning messy notes into the ten fields, without adding reasons you did not say
  • Suggesting scorable expecteds (you then edit)
  • A review-time prompt: compare the page to what happened, four-box audit

Do not use AI for:

  • Writing the "because" you wish you had had
  • A beautiful summary that replaces the dated page
  • Storing the only copy in a chat history you will lose

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Role: Decision-page clerk. Do not improve my reasoning. Do not add motives.
My messy notes:
"""
[paste]
"""
Today's date: [date]
Tasks:
1) Fill a Decision Page with these fields only: Date/Decision, Want, Options, Know vs Assume, Door type, Wrong (bruise/ruin), Expecteds (scorable, dated), Confidence, Next action, Review date.
2) Flag anything you had to invent to fill a field — list as MISSING, do not guess.
3) Rewrite my expecteds if they are unfailable. Offer scorable versions, labeled as offers.
4) Give me a one-line calendar title and a review prompt I can paste on that date.

Constraints: Short. My words where possible. No pep talk. No "journey."

What to verify: That the "because" is still yours. If the model made you more coherent, you may have just written the myth early.

Do not paste: other people's HR information, medical details, children's full names, anything your employer could claim as their record if this is a personal journal. Keep personal pages off company tools.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "one findable record plus a future ping."

Category examples:

  • A paper notebook that lives in one place
  • A notes app with a tag (decision) and a date in the title
  • Calendar holds for review dates, with a link or a page number
  • A simple sheet if you want to sort — overkill for most humans

When not to use technology: five tools, zero reviews. Encryption and local copies matter if the page has household money or career intent you do not want in a company's model. A paper page in a drawer beats a leaked cloud.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Writing the page as a defense attorney.

You can feel it: every assumption is reasonable, every option you rejected is a cartoon, every expected is unfailable, the confidence is 85. That page will not teach you. It will wait in a folder to say "see, I was right" or it will be too embarrassing to open if you were wrong.

Recovery: add one line you do not like. The assumption that is load-bearing and unchecked. The option you did not really consider. A 60 instead of an 85. The page is a gym, not a monument.

The other mistake: reviewing by rereading and nodding, without the four-box. Nodding is mythology with a ritual. Run the Outcome Audit. Write an addendum. Do not edit the original.

Defense

The Defense

The Decision Page fails when:

  • You only write the one-way, dramatic choices. Then you have no calibration on the everyday bets where you actually live. Write some small ones too — a hire of a tool, a hard conversation, a purchase over a threshold you pick.
  • You write and never review. Set the calendar in the same sitting or admit you made a diary.
  • The page becomes surveillance — of a partner, a report, a child. This tool is for your choices. Other people are not your case study without their consent.
  • You are in a legally sensitive situation and you write a novel on a company laptop. Then you have created a record you do not understand. For those doors, be spare, factual, and advised by a human whose job is that door. This course is not that advisor.

Limitation: a page will not make you brave. It will make your bravery, or your cowardice, inspectable. That is more than most people give their future self.

Council

The Masters' Table

Daniel Kahneman. Hindsight will rewrite the beginning. A timestamp is how you refuse the rewrite.

Philip Tetlock. An unscored percent is a personality. A page with a percent and a date can be scored.

Herbert Simon. External memory is how a bounded mind gets a second pass.

Warren Buffett. A thesis, written, and what would change your mind. The long game is documented or it is a slogan.

Gary Klein. The premortem you wrote is only useful if it is still there when the patient actually dies — or lives.

Annie Duke. You cannot tell a good bet from a good outcome if the bet was never recorded.

Synthesis. The future you is a colleague. Leave them a page, not a legend.

Where the council could be wrong. Journals can become rumination. Buffett's memos are not your personality. Some good decisions are made in a body at speed (Klein's firefighters) and written after; do not stop a two-way move to fetch a template. Write after, same day. Do not pretend the after is the before.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to Sunday, 8:05 p.m.

Calm. Phone face down. One page, not a new app account.

Observe. A choice already made, or about to be. Fragments in a notes app. Sam's "we'll remember." Elena's Q3 fog.

Define. The job tonight is a Decision Page and a calendar ping, not a new round of doubt.

Verify. The facts you think you used — salary, runway, the reference line — go on the page as they were, not as they will sound in Q3.

Prioritize. Expecteds and review date over a beautiful "because."

Find resources / people. Sam can hear the want and the ruin line. Sam does not need to co-author the page unless the choice is jointly ruinous.

Options. Nothing; a feeling-journal; a structured page; a page so long it never gets reviewed.

Risks. A lawyerly page. A page on a work laptop. No calendar ping.

Choose. The ten-field page, short. Calendar months 3, 6, 12.

Communicate. To Sam: "I wrote why, and when we'll look. I don't want us to invent this later." To Elena, later: not the page. A BLUF about next steps. The page is yours.

Act. Write. Set reminders. Put the fragments in an archive folder so they do not pretend to be the record.

Document. This is the document.

Adapt. If the choice is not actually made yet, the page is a Decision 7. If it is made, do not reopen it tonight unless ruin appeared. Writing is not a loophole to un-choose a two-way door you already walked.

Reflect. Did you write a 60 or an 85? Did an expected failable?

Practice

Practice

Fifteen minutes. One live choice — open or made in the last two weeks.

Fill a Decision Page with all ten fields. Timebox. If a field is empty, write MISSING, do not invent.

Then set the review date in a calendar with this prompt pasted:

"Read the page. Do not edit it. Run the four-box Outcome Audit. One update. Addendum dated today."

If you have an old choice with no page, reconstruct honestly on a second sheet labeled RECONSTRUCTION, not as if it were a then-document. The label is the honesty.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the choice that will be retold in a performance review. Situation: you took a project, a hire, a no. What most people do: let the ending write the beginning at review time. Why it fails: you will be graded on a novel. Resourceful response: Decision Page the day you choose; review before the review. Words to use: "At the time I knew X, assumed Y, expected Z by this date." Words to avoid: "I always knew" / "Nobody could have seen." Tool: Decision Page + Outcome Audit. AI assist: format only; keep the because yours. What could go wrong: a page that is actually a memo to file on someone else. Keep it about your choice.

Money — the purchase or investment that will grow a legend. Situation: a house, a course, a market bet, a car. What most people do: screenshots of the win; amnesia of the thesis. Why it fails: you will copy a luck. Resourceful response: thesis, expecteds, ruin, review date, before you transfer the money. Words to use: "I will know this was wrong if…" Words to avoid: "It's a no-brainer" with no page. Tool: Decision Page + Confidence Range + Door Type. AI assist: scorable expecteds; you verify numbers in the real documents. What could go wrong: writing a page instead of reading the contract. The page does not replace the one-way read.

People — the conversation you are about to have, or the person you are about to trust. Situation: a hard talk, a partnership, a "let's try again." What most people do: a feeling in the body, then a later story about who they always were. Why it fails: you cannot improve a pattern you mythologize. Resourceful response: a short page — want, assume, expected behavior by a date, review. Words to use: "By [date] I expect to have seen…" Words to avoid: a character diagnosis on the page ("they are a narcissist"). Write behavior. Tool: Decision Page. Pause before you send it to anyone. AI assist: none on another person's psyche. Process fields only. What could go wrong: the page becomes a secret indictment. If it is a joint life, consider a shared expecteds list, consented.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

The choice was made in a hallway, under heat, with Elena and a VP. You said yes to a date. You are now on the train home, ashamed of the 90% you laundered, or proud in a way you do not trust.

You cannot un-say the hallway. You can still write the page as it actually happened:

"Date: today. Decision: I said yes to the 12th in a hallway. Want: not to be the blocker. Know: two vendor slips. Assume: I could not say 60% without a cost to my standing. Door: one-way social once the VP heard it. Wrong: customer harm (bruise to ruin depending on the account). Expecteds: I will feel pressure to defend this date; Wednesday test is the real information. Confidence I should have said: 60%. Confidence I performed: 90%. Review: Wednesday night."

That page is ugly. It is also the only way Wednesday is a lesson instead of a new novel ("the vendor betrayed us").

If you have energy left, send one BLUF to Elena: the hedge, the test, the 60% you did not say. The page stays yours. The hedge goes to the world.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Which of your origin stories would not survive a page written at the time?
  • Where do you keep fragments and call them a record?
  • What expected in your life is currently unfailable, and therefore useless?
  • Who are you writing for — future you, a judge, a parent, a boss?
  • When is the next review date you actually have on a calendar?
Remember

Remember This

You cannot improve what you mythologize later. Date it, expect it, review it.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Hindsight bias is one of the most reliable effects in psychology: once an outcome is known, people overestimate how predictable it was. Fischhoff's early work, and a long line after, is the academic well. Kahneman popularized it. For this course the operational claim is enough: your memory will cheat, in both directions, unless you leave a timestamp.

Decision journals show up in practitioner literature more than in a single famous experiment that "proves they make you rich." Russo and Schoemaker (Decision Traps, 1989; later Winning Decisions) recommended them as a professional habit. Tetlock's forecasters improved by tracking. Pilots and surgeons use written records and checklists (Gawande's The Checklist Manifesto, 2009) for a related reason: memory is a terrible operating system. Do not overclaim. A page is a cheap, high-percentage habit. It is not a talisman.

A harder variation: the change-my-mind line. Buffett and Munger both, in different accents, care about what would falsify a thesis. Add one line to every page: "This decision is a miss if ___ , even if I still feel good." That line is how you stop a sunk story.

A second harder variation: a shared page for a household one-way door (a lease, a child, a move). Two wants, two assumption lists, agreed expecteds, one review date. If you cannot write it together, you may not have a joint decision. You may have one person campaigning.

Keep the archive small. A folder of 30 pages you review is a practice. A Notion cathedral is a hobby.

Mission

Field Mission

Tonight, write one Decision Page for a choice that is open or that you made in the last ten days.

Set the review date in a calendar with the audit prompt.

Put the page where future you will find it — and not where an employer will.

If someone in your life said "we'll remember," show them the review date, not the whole page, unless they are a true co-decider.

Sources

Sources to Explore

  • Baruch Fischhoff, work on hindsight bias (starting in the mid-1970s) — why the ending rewrites the beginning.
  • Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011 — hindsight, the remembering self, and why a story feels like a record.
  • J. Edward Russo and Paul J. H. Schoemaker, Decision Traps, 1989, and Winning Decisions, 2002 — decision journals as a working habit.
  • Philip E. Tetlock and Dan Gardner, Superforecasting, 2015 — tracking and scoring as the difference between a percent and a personality.
  • Atul Gawande, The Checklist Manifesto, 2009 — external memory for processes that matter; cousin to the page, not the same tool.
  • Annie Duke, Thinking in Bets, 2018 — you cannot learn from bets you did not write down.
