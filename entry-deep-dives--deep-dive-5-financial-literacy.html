Deep Dive 5: Financial Literacy

836 words

Enough to be dangerous, not so much you become obsessive.

Playbook 4 gave you the practical basics. This section goes deeper on the concepts every adult should understand before making major financial decisions.

This is not investment advice. It is a compressed orientation to concepts. For consequential decisions, consult a qualified professional in your jurisdiction.

The four financial fundamentals

  • INCOME vs. WEALTH. Income is what arrives in your account. Wealth is what remains after everything else. High income does not automatically produce wealth.
  • ASSETS vs. LIABILITIES. Simplified: assets produce income or appreciate in value. Liabilities cost you money to hold. Your house is an asset that comes with liabilities (mortgage, upkeep). Understanding your actual position requires looking at both sides.
  • COMPOUND vs. LINEAR. Money in productive investments compounds. Money in a savings account accumulates linearly. Over decades, the difference is enormous.
  • REAL vs. NOMINAL. Inflation reduces the buying power of money. A 5% return in a year of 3% inflation is a 2% real return. Do not confuse the two.

The savings hierarchy

Note

THE TOOL — Where the next dollar should probably go

Note

1. EMERGENCY FUND — 3–6 months of essential expenses in a savings account. Not for growth. For emergencies. 2. HIGH-INTEREST DEBT — anything above ~7% is almost certainly worth eliminating before investing. The 'return' on paying off a high-interest debt is the interest rate you no longer pay. 3. EMPLOYER MATCH — if you have retirement accounts with employer matching, contribute at least enough to get the full match. It's the highest immediate return you'll find. 4. TAX-ADVANTAGED RETIREMENT — max out where you can (401(k), IRA, ISA, Superannuation, etc., depending on jurisdiction). 5. GENERAL INVESTING — broadly diversified, low-cost, patient capital. Boring beats exciting. 6. SPECIFIC OPPORTUNITIES — real estate, individual stocks, businesses. Only after the above are handled. Adapt to your specific tax and legal environment. This is a shape, not a prescription.

The four investment principles worth carrying

  • DIVERSIFICATION. Don't put all your eggs in one basket. Across asset classes, geographies, and time horizons.
  • LOW COSTS. Fees compound too — in the wrong direction. A 1% annual fee over 40 years can consume 20-30% of returns.
  • TIME IN THE MARKET beats TIMING THE MARKET. Trying to enter and exit at the right moments is difficult even for professionals. Buying regularly over long periods works reliably.
  • KEEP YOUR HANDS OFF. The single biggest cause of underperformance in individual investors is trading in response to news, fear, or excitement. Set it up. Leave it alone.

The three financial mistakes most people make

  • LIFESTYLE INFLATION — spending more as they earn more, keeping the savings rate constant instead of increasing.
  • UNDER-INSURANCE — not carrying appropriate insurance against catastrophic risks (health, disability, life if others depend on you).
  • OVER-INDEXING ON ONE PERSPECTIVE — believing whatever financial content you consume regularly, without checking against opposing views.

Financial conversations worth having

  • WITH YOUR PARTNER: annual review of finances together. Not just the accountant's version — the story of what you're both trying to build.
  • WITH A PROFESSIONAL: at least once, especially for tax planning, estate planning, and major life transitions. Fee-only fiduciary advisors avoid the conflicts of interest common in commission-based models.
  • WITH YOURSELF: annually on your birthday — where am I financially, where would I like to be in five years, what small change would move the trajectory?
Note

THE GREAT MINDS COUNCIL — On the money question

Note

Through Warren Buffett's lens: 'The stock market is a device for transferring money from the impatient to the patient.' Through Jack Bogle's lens: The founder of Vanguard: low-cost index funds held for decades outperform almost all active management. The evidence for this is now overwhelming. Through Morgan Housel's lens: The Psychology of Money: financial outcomes depend more on behavior than on knowledge. The person who behaves well with modest knowledge outperforms the sophisticated theorist who panics. Through Naval Ravikant's lens: 'Money is not the root of all evil. The lack of money is the root of all evil.' Have a healthy relationship with it; don't worship it; don't disdain it. Where the Council might be wrong: General financial advice becomes wrong when applied to specific situations. Your tax environment, your dependents, your career volatility all matter. Universal principles are a starting point; a professional is often the right destination.

Remember This

“Modest knowledge plus disciplined behavior beats sophisticated knowledge plus emotional trading, forever.”
