Your manager shouts at you

high The State Checkabuseauthorityboundaries
1

What is happening

Your manager's voice goes up. The content stops being about the work and becomes about you — your competence, your attitude, whether you are "actually up to this." Others can hear. You feel yourself shrinking or heating up.

2

What is actually going on

  • Shouting is not feedback. Feedback is specific, about behaviour, and survives being written down. If what is happening could not be repeated in an email without embarrassing the sender, it is not feedback.
  • You cannot resolve the substance while volume is high. Nothing agreed in a shouting match holds, because the state both of you are in is not one that makes durable decisions.
  • Your goal is narrow: end this episode without damage to you, and create a record. Not to win, not to teach them, not to make them see themselves.
3

Do this, in this order

  1. Regulate your body firstFeet flat, one slow exhale, drop your shoulders. You cannot access language while your system is preparing to fight.
  2. Lower your volume instead of matching itTwo people shouting is a scene, and scenes get remembered as mutual. A quiet voice against a loud one changes the picture in the room.
  3. Name the process, not the personDo not diagnose them out loud. Refer to the conversation, not their character.
  4. Offer a specific pauseNot walking out — a named return. "Twenty minutes" is a request for a break. "I'm done" is a resignation risk.
  5. Write it down within the hourDate, time, words used as close to verbatim as you can manage, who else was present. Send yourself an email so it is timestamped.
  6. Decide if this was once or a patternOne bad day under real pressure is human. The third time is the person, and the response has to change accordingly.
4

Words you can borrow

To de-escalate
"I want to fix this. I can't think clearly while we're at this volume. Can we take twenty minutes and come back to it?"
To hold the line without a fight
"I'll take the criticism about the work. I'm not willing to be spoken to like this."
To get the substance
"Tell me the specific thing you need changed and by when, and I'll do it."
The next day
"I want to talk about yesterday. Not the project — the way it happened. That cannot be how we do this."
5

What makes it worse

  • Matching volume. You will be the one written up, whatever the provocation.
  • Apologising for things you did not do just to end the noise. It sets the price of shouting at you at zero.
  • Crying-then-agreeing to an unreasonable deadline. Decisions made under duress become the new baseline.
  • Diagnosing them out loud — "you have anger issues." True or not, it guarantees escalation.
  • Resigning in the same hour. Never make a life decision inside a spike.
6

Once the pressure drops

  • Keep the log going. A pattern with dates is the only thing HR can act on; a general complaint about tone dies in the room.
  • Talk to one person outside the situation. Sustained shouting rewires your sense of what is normal, and outside reference points are how you keep calibration.
  • Read your handbook on conduct and grievance before you need it, not during.
  • Start passive job-searching. Not necessarily to leave — having options changes how you sit in the next conversation, and it shows.
Where this stops being yours to handle

If it involves slurs, physical intimidation, throwing objects, or it targets a protected characteristic, it stops being a management-style problem and becomes a formal matter. Report it in writing, with your log attached, and keep a copy outside company systems.

A note on this library

These are general principles written to be useful under pressure, not legal, medical, or financial advice. Laws and emergency procedures differ by country. If life is at risk, contact your emergency services first and read this afterwards.
