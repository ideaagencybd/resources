Five Whys — and When They Fail

Root cause without the cartoon version

Five whys can find a story. Check the system and the incentive.
The idea

The Hook

The meeting ends with a single sentence that feels like wisdom.

"The root cause is communication." "The root cause is that we didn't have a process." "The root cause is Priya was out." "The root cause is human error."

Someone wrote "5 Whys" on the board. They asked why five times, in a straight line, and arrived at a cause that is either unfixable ("humans") or suspiciously convenient ("we need another tool," "we need another meeting," "we need Priya to not have a life"). The room is relieved. Relief is not the same as a cause.

Five Whys is a real tool from a real factory tradition. It is also one of the most abused rituals in knowledge work. Used well, it walks a simple causal chain until you find a change you can make. Used badly, it manufactures a single fake root and trains everyone to stop thinking.

This lesson teaches the tool and the trap.

The room

The Scenario

Monday, 9:40 a.m. The eleven-day request from 5.4 has become a customer complaint.

Elena: "I want a root cause before noon. Use Five Whys. I don't want a novel."

Jonah, already at the whiteboard:

  1. Why was the customer angry? Because they waited eleven days.
  2. Why eleven days? Because the request sat in Slack.
  3. Why Slack? Because sales DMed Mara.
  4. Why did Mara not move it? Because she was in the 10:00s all week.
  5. Why was she in meetings? Because we don't protect maker time.

Root cause: meetings. Fix: no-meeting Wednesday.

Mara's face is hot. Priya, back today, says nothing. Sales is not in the room. The "root cause" has a villain-shaped hole in it, and a fix that will not touch intake.

Elena looks at you. "That looks clean. Are we done?"

Are you?

Your move

Your Move

Common move. Accept the clean line. It was five. It ended. People love a number that feels like a method.

Better move. Add two more whys until you reach "culture" or "leadership." That feels deeper. It is often just a longer story with a bigger noun. You cannot ship a culture by noon.

Expert move. Keep the chain, then break it. For each "why," ask: is this the only because? What else had to be true? Where is the system (the three front doors)? Where is the incentive (sales is rewarded for promising speed, not for using the form)?

Resourceful move. Use the tool named Why / System / Incentive. One simple chain, plus a system look, plus an incentive look. You will probably find two or three causes you can actually change: one front door, a visible queue, a rule that a promise sales cannot keep cannot be made. "Meetings" may be real and still not be the cause of this miss. Mara is not the root.

Why the common move fails: a linear why-chain follows the first plausible story. Humans are story animals (Level 2). Five repetitions will not save you from confirmation bias. They can launder it.

What can go wrong with the expert move: analysis until the customer is angrier. You still need a noon artifact: causes you can change, and a next step. Depth without a ship is another stall.

The idea

The Big Idea

A root cause is not a single noun. It is a changeable structure that would produce this miss again with different people in the chairs.

Five Whys can get you moving down a chain. It cannot, by itself, tell you when the chain is a cartoon. You need two extra lenses: the machine (5.4) and the scoreboard people are actually playing (incentives). If those are missing, you will "five why" your way to a scapegoat or a slogan.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Sakichi Toyoda is often credited with the practice of asking "why" repeatedly in the Toyota lineage. Taiichi Ohno, architect of the Toyota Production System, described Five Whys as a practical way to see the nature of a problem by asking why several times — five is a habit, not a law of nature. In a factory with a relatively stable machine, a linear chain sometimes is the story: the machine stopped; the fuse blew; the fuse was the wrong rating; the spec was wrong; the spec process missed a change. You can walk that. You can fix the spec process.

Knowledge work is rarely that linear. Several things had to be true at once: multiple front doors, an invisible pile, a part-time check, a sales promise, a week of 10:00s, a missing field on a form. If you force that into one chain, the chain will pick a person, because people are easy to point at.

Known failure modes of Five Whys, which quality people have been writing about for years:

  • Stopping at a convenient cause. "Human error." "Priya was out." Those are events. The question is what the machine does when a human errs or a key person is out. (You will need that in 5.B.)
  • One chain, one root. Complex misses have causes that combine. A single line hides the combination.
  • No facts. Why-chains built from memory are just Facts vs Stories without the table. "Mara was in meetings" might be true and still not explain why the item was invisible to Jonah.
  • The investigator's pet fix. Jonah likes no-meeting Wednesday. The chain walked there like a trained dog.
  • Unfixable roots. "Human nature." "The industry." If you cannot change it this month, it is not an operational root. It is a shrug.

Deming again, in different clothes: most trouble is in the system. Incentives are part of the system. If you pay sales to promise 24 hours and pay operations to be careful, you have designed a complaint. Five Whys that never touch the scoreboard are a ritual.

When is Five Whys useful? Short, physical, or simple procedural chains: the file went out wrong; the wrong template; the template is the default; the default was never changed after the rebrand. Or: the bill was late; it sat in a bag; there is no place bills live. Linear, checkable, changeable. Use it. Then still ask system and incentive, once, so you do not miss the second cause.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: Why / System / Incentive

Use it after a miss, before the room settles on a villain.

  1. Fix the event in facts. What happened, when, what was supposed to happen, who was harmed. Two columns if you need them (2.1). No why yet.
  2. One why-chain, written, with evidence. Ask why. Write the because. Note the evidence for that because, or mark GUESSED. Stop at a cause you could change in thirty days, or at five, whichever comes first. Five is a ceiling, not a quota.
  3. Break the chain. At each step: "What else had to be true?" Add a second because if it exists. If you cannot find a second, fine — some chains are simple. Many are not.
  4. System lens (from 5.4). What machine would produce this even with decent people? Front doors, piles, loops, bottleneck, a missing checklist (5.3). Write one sentence.
  5. Incentive lens. What is rewarded, punished, easy, or awkward? Who looks good if this promise is made? Who looks good if this form is skipped? Include social incentives (not looking pedantic in front of sales).
  6. Pick causes you can change. One to three. Each must have an owner, a next action, and a way you would notice if it worked. If your only cause is "be more careful," you failed the tool. That is a slogan. Put the care into a checklist, a queue, or a scoreboard.
  7. Say what you are not claiming. "We are not saying Mara is the problem. We are not saying this is only meetings." Intellectual honesty (2.7) belongs in incident notes.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

Twelve minutes, Elena wants a root by noon:

Write three bullets only:

  1. Event: customer waited eleven days; request lived in a DM.
  2. System: three front doors, no visible queue, quality in a two-day cul-de-sac.
  3. Incentive: sales can promise times the machine cannot do, and looks good for being "responsive."

Then one change you can make today (one queue) and one you can make this week (promise rule). That is a better noon artifact than a five-line cartoon.

The hack

The Hack

When someone says "root cause," hear "cause we can change," and ask for two.

"Give me two causes that would still be true if we swapped the people." If they cannot, you are in a story about characters. If they can, you are in a machine. You can still coach a person. You start with the machine.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is fast at generating a tidy five-why chain. That is exactly the risk. It will complete the story you started.

Use AI for:

  • Turning a fact list into candidate chains, labeled as hypotheses
  • Asking "what else had to be true?"
  • Drafting the system sentence and incentive sentence
  • Meeting notes that separate facts, guesses, and actions

Do not use AI for:

  • The official root cause of a safety, legal, or HR incident
  • A chain built from your rant
  • Naming a person as the cause in a document that will travel

Prompt (copy, then fill):

WHEN TO USE: After a miss, I want Why / System / Incentive without a fake single root.

Event (facts only):
"""
[what, when, expected, harm]
"""
What we already know about the machine: [front doors, piles, people out]
What people are rewarded for: [sales, ops, managers]

Goal:
1) One why-chain, each step tagged EVIDENCE or GUESSED.
2) At each step, one "what else could be true."
3) System sentence (no names).
4) Incentive sentence.
5) Three changeable causes with a next action each.
6) One paragraph: how Five Whys could fool us on this case.

Constraints: Do not invent facts. Do not end on "human error" or "culture."

What to verify: Every GUESSED step. Walk it with someone who disagrees. Steelman (2.7).

Do not paste: HR allegations, medical details, customer secrets, anything that should be in a proper incident process instead of a chatbot.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "keep facts, chains, and actions in different buckets."

Category: a simple incident note, not a detective platform. Current examples: a one-page template in the wiki (Event / Chain / System / Incentive / Actions); a shared doc with those headings; a ticketing postmortem field that forbids a single "root cause" dropdown. Fancy "root cause analysis" software will not save a cartoon if the culture wants a cartoon.

When not to use technology: a 40-field postmortem that takes longer than the fix. The template should be shorter than the argument.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Ending on a person, then dressing it as a system.

"The root cause is that Mara did not follow the process" is a character sentence. Adding "because we need to train Mara" does not make it Deming. Training may still be right. It is not the first lens.

It happens because accountability feels like rigor. Sometimes it is. More often it is relief.

Recovery: rewrite the sentence with the person removed. If it still explains the miss, you have a system cause. If it collapses, you may have a special cause — skill, load, or a true performance issue. Then you have a different conversation (4.4, 4.5), not a fake why-chain.

Defense

The Defense

Why / System / Incentive fails when:

  • You are in a genuine simple chain and you overcomplicate it. If the fuse is the fuse, change the fuse. Do not hold a seminar on incentives.
  • The real cause is political and unnamed. Then the tool becomes cover. Name the constraint you are not allowed to touch, or you will "fix" Mara forever.
  • Safety-critical investigation. Aviation and medicine have deeper methods (and legal processes). Do not pretend five whys plus a prompt is an NTSB report.
  • You use "systems" to dodge your own part. You are in the machine too.

Limitation: this will not give you certainty. It will give you causes you can test. If the change does not move the result, you had a story. Update in public.

Council

The Masters' Table

Taiichi Ohno. Through Ohno's lens, asking why repeatedly is how you refuse the first explanation. The spirit is go-and-see, not sit-and-narrate. If you Five Why from a conference room without the inbox in front of you, you have left Toyota and entered theater.

W. Edwards Deming. Do not treat common-cause machine noise as a person's sin. Do not treat a true special cause as "the process." The map and the chain serve that distinction.

Daniel Kahneman. The fast system will hand you a coherent story. A tidy five-line why is catnip. Slow down at "because." Demand the second because.

Donella Meadows. Incentives and information are leverage. A queue everyone can see and a promise sales cannot make without a date from operations will beat a poster about ownership.

Peter Drucker. If you cannot convert a cause into a decision, you have a comment, not an analysis. Noon needs a decision.

Synthesis. Dig, but do not worship a single root. Pair the chain with the machine and the scoreboard. Change something that would survive a recast of the play.

Where the council could be wrong. Toyota-style tools can be cargo-culted into offices they do not fit. A factory has parts. Your office has status. If you ignore status, your "incentive" paragraph will be naive. If you become obsessed with status, you will never fix the inbox. Hold both.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 9:40.

Calm. You do not humiliate Jonah's chain. You also do not let it close the case.

Observe. Facts: eleven days, Slack DM, customer complaint, sales not present, Priya was out last week, three front doors already mapped.

Define. Noon artifact: Why / System / Incentive, plus one today-change.

Verify. Was Mara actually in 10:00s every day, or is that a story? Check the calendar. Facts.

Prioritize. Changeable causes over a clean narrative. Customer repair in parallel (a date, an owner) — analysis is not a substitute for the apology and the path.

Find resources / people. Get one sales voice in the note, even a five-minute call. Without them the incentive lens is a guess.

Options. Accept "meetings." Or break the chain.

Risks. Elena wanted clean. Clean may cost you the real miss next month. Offer her a short document, not a novel — she asked for that.

Choose. Three causes: (1) multiple front doors, no queue; (2) promises uncoupled from capacity; (3) quality concentrated in one part-time role with no backup (Priya out = silent stall). Meetings as a possible fourth, not the root.

Communicate. "Jonah's chain is a good start and it stops too early. If we swap Mara out, this still happens. Here's the system sentence, the incentive sentence, and three changes. Today's: one queue. This week: no date to a customer unless the queue owner stamps it. Backup for Priya's check is a separate action — that's our 5.B risk."

Act. Queue. Customer date. Invite sales to the promise rule.

Document. The note with GUESSED tags. No "root cause: Mara."

Adapt. If next month's miss is different, you learned. If it is the same, you did not change the cause.

Reflect. Where did you want a villain?

Practice

Practice

Do this now. Twelve minutes. Use a real miss from the last month.

  1. Write the event in four factual lines.
  2. Build one why-chain. Tag EVIDENCE or GUESSED.
  3. At two steps, add "what else had to be true."
  4. Write one system sentence and one incentive sentence.
  5. Circle one to three changeable causes. For each: owner, next action, how you would know.
  6. Cross out any cause that is a person's name or the words "communication," "culture," or "human error" unless you have translated them into a structure.
Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the incident note. Situation: a miss; a manager wants a root cause by noon. What most people do: a five-line chain that ends on a person or a slogan. Why it fails: the miss repeats with a new name in the box. Resourceful response: Why / System / Incentive; one today-change. Words to use: "Changeable causes, not a single root." "What else had to be true?" Words to avoid: "Human error," "that's just Jonah," "lessons learned" with no owner. Tool: Why / System / Incentive. AI assist: the prompt, then a skeptic reads it. What could go wrong: three causes that are actually one slogan in a trench coat. Make them independently testable.

Family — the repeated explosion. Situation: the same fight (bills, dishes, lateness) with a new costume. What most people do: a why-chain that ends on "you don't care." Why it fails: caring is not a process. Resourceful response: event, system (no home for the bill), incentive (the person who nags becomes the owner by default). Change the machine: a bowl, a date, a checklist. Words to use: "If we both went on a trip, how would this fail?" Words to avoid: "The root cause is you." Tool: Why / System / Incentive, gently. This is not a factory stand-up. What could go wrong: using "systems" to dodge an apology. Own your part, then change the bowl.

Money — the late fee. Situation: a fee, again. What most people do: "I need to be more on it." Why it fails: a slogan. Resourceful response: why-chain (didn't see it / mail is a pile / no calendar) + system (no autopay, no one inbox for bills) + incentive (avoiding looking at money feels better short-term). Change: autopay or a 20-minute money date on the Week Top 3. Tool: Why / System / Incentive + Top 3. What could go wrong: automating a wrong amount. Verify once.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Same Monday. Worse.

Elena's boss wants a name for a report. Sales is on the call and angry. Jonah's chain is already in a screenshot. Mara looks like she might cry or explode. You have eight minutes.

Pause. Holding sentence: "We can give you causes by noon. We should not give you a person on this call."

Then, fast:

  • Event in facts.
  • System sentence: three doors, no queue, key check concentrated.
  • Incentive sentence: promises uncoupled from capacity.
  • Offer: "If you need a name for 'who owns the fix,' it's me until we have a queue owner. If you need a name for 'who was lazy,' I won't invent one."

You have used Manage Up a week early. You have also refused to launder a sacrifice through Five Whys. That is the defense of the tool.

If they demand a villain anyway, you still do not supply Mara. You can supply a role: "The queue had no owner. That role is now X." Roles are changeable. Character assassination is not a process.

Reflect

Reflection

  • When did a tidy "root cause" let your team off the hook?
  • Which of your pet fixes do your why-chains keep walking toward?
  • What is rewarded in your shop that produces the miss you are currently blaming on an individual?
  • Can you state a recent failure with the people removed and still be true?
  • Where would Five Whys actually be enough — a simple chain you have been overthinking?
Remember

Remember This

Five whys can find a story. Check the system and the incentive, then change something that would survive a recast.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

A method that traveled, and what it lost in the airport.

Toyota's use of why-chains sat inside a thicker practice: go to the place, look at the thing, talk to the people who do the work, improve the standard, do not stop at the worker. Western offices often imported the number five and left the rest. The result is a meeting ritual that feels operational and is mostly narrative.

There is no law that the fifth why is the true one. Ohno's "five" is pedagogy: you will stop too early, so push. In practice you might need three, or seven. The stopping rule for this course is: a cause you can change, with evidence, that would still be true with different people, plus a look at combining causes.

Other tools exist when the chain is the wrong shape: fishbone (Ishikawa) diagrams for categories of cause; fault trees for safety; statistical process control for variation. You do not need to become a quality engineer. You need to know when a straight line is a lie. If three independent things had to fail together, write all three. "Root" in the singular is often the cartoon.

On incentives: you do not need a conspiracy. People follow what is easy and what is praised. Sales is not evil for promising. Operations is not evil for buffering. The design set them into a game. Change the game or keep writing novels about "alignment."

Harder variation: take three old postmortems. Highlight every singular "root cause." Rewrite each with Why / System / Incentive. If two of the three still end on a person, your culture is using RCA as HR. That is useful to know before 5.B, when the key person is out and the cartoon will say "the root cause is that Priya was out." Priya will be out again. The machine has to survive it.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 48 hours, run Why / System / Incentive on one real miss — work, home, or money. Write it on one page.

Four lines at the bottom:

  1. The cartoon root you almost accepted
  2. The system sentence
  3. The incentive sentence
  4. The change you will actually make

Make the change small enough to happen this week. Analysis that does not touch the machine is a hobby.

Sources to Explore

  • Taiichi Ohno, Toyota Production System: Beyond Large-Scale Production, 1988 (English) — Five Whys in its home context; "five" as practice, not magic.
  • W. Edwards Deming, Out of the Crisis, 1986 — common cause, system, the futility of hunting workers first.
  • Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011 — coherent stories versus causes.
  • Donella H. Meadows, Thinking in Systems, 2008 — leverage in information and incentives.
  • For critiques of Five Whys as a solo method (single root, confirmation bias, stopping early), see quality-engineering discussions in the years after it was popularized in Western lean programs; treat Five Whys as one lens among others, not as a complete investigation.
