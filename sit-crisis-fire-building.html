Emergency & Crisis

Fire in your building

critical The Exit Mapfireevacuationcritical
1

What is happening

The alarm is going, or you smell smoke, or someone is banging on doors. There is a strong pull to work out whether this is real before doing anything.

2

What is actually going on

  • Smoke kills far more people than flame, and it is fast — a corridor can go from hazy to unsurvivable in a couple of minutes. Time spent verifying is taken directly from your escape window.
  • The dangerous instinct is normalcy bias: gathering belongings, getting dressed, finishing the call. It is well documented in fire deaths and it feels entirely reasonable at the time.
  • Every second of hesitation costs more than any object you own.
3

Do this, in this order

  1. Get out now; do not investigateTake keys and a phone if they are within reach. Nothing else. Not the laptop, not the documents.
  2. Test doors with the back of your hand before openingHot door means fire on the other side. Do not open it. Find another route.
  3. Stay low under smokeThe survivable air is near the floor. Crawl if you must, and keep one hand on a wall so you can navigate in zero visibility.
  4. Never use a liftThey fail, they open onto the fire floor, and they fill with smoke.
  5. Close doors behind youEvery closed door buys minutes for whoever is still inside.
  6. If you cannot get out, make a safe room and signalSeal the gaps with wet fabric, go to a window, call and give your exact floor and room. Being locatable is what gets you rescued.
4

Words you can borrow

To emergency services
"Fire at 14 Oakfield Road. I am on the fourth floor, front of the building, one adult and a child. We cannot reach the stairs."
Warning neighbours in passing
"Fire — get out now." Two words while you move. Do not stop to explain.
To someone frozen
A direct instruction with their name and a physical action: "Sam. Take my hand. Walk."
5

What makes it worse

  • Going back in for anything at all, including pets, once you are out. This is a leading cause of death in building fires.
  • Opening a hot door to see how bad it is.
  • Assuming it is a drill because it usually is.
  • Getting dressed properly or gathering documents.
  • Standing in the stairwell filming.
6

Once the pressure drops

  • Go to the assembly point and be counted. Rescuers make life-risking decisions based on who is unaccounted for.
  • Get checked for smoke inhalation even if you feel fine. Damage presents hours later.
  • Photograph everything for insurance once permitted back, and get the incident report number.
  • Locate your two nearest exits wherever you sleep tonight and every night after. This is the lesson that actually saves you next time.
A note on this library

These are general principles written to be useful under pressure, not legal, medical, or financial advice. Laws and emergency procedures differ by country. If life is at risk, contact your emergency services first and read this afterwards.
