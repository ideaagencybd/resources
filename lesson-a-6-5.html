Spreadsheets That Make You Useful

The quiet superpower of every competent workplace

30 minThe Clean Table2,731 words23 sections
Skill
Tables, lookups, pivots, charts
Tool
The Clean Table
Result
You can answer a real question with a sheet
In one line
If it is not a clean table, it is not a spreadsheet. It is a poster.
The idea

The Hook

There is a moment in every office when a person who cannot use a spreadsheet becomes decorative.

Someone asks "which region is actually down?" Three people open slide decks. One person opens a sheet, sorts a column, and answers in twelve seconds. That person is not a genius. They have a table.

You do not need to become an analyst. You need to stop pasting numbers into a document and hoping. A spreadsheet is a place where a question can be asked twice and get the same answer. That is a rare property in adult life.

The tool is not a brand. It is a clean table, a few honest formulas, and the nerve to check them.

The room

The Scenario

Wednesday, 3:40 p.m. Elena: "Before 5:00, is the West actually behind, or did two loud deals fall through? Last quarter's export. No novel."

Jonah forwards a CSV. Merged title row. "West " with a trailing space in some cells, "west" in others. Dollar amounts typed as text. A total at the bottom Jonah typed by hand last month. Two blank rows "for air."

Finance: "Don't use Jonah's total. We have never been able to reproduce it."

Eighty minutes, a dirty file, a manager who wants a true sentence. What do you build?

Your move

Your Move

Common move. Make it pretty. Colors, a title, a chart that starts at 40. Elena will still ask "what's the number," and you will not be able to point at a cell.

Better move. Delete the blank rows and SUM the amount column. If West is spelled three ways, your SUM is a story.

Expert move. Clean Table first — one header row, one thing per column, no merges, no handwritten totals in the data, consistent names. Then filter, summarize with a formula you can point at, then a chart that does not lie. Check the total against a count of rows.

Resourceful move. The expert move, plus an assumptions box, plus a second method on the load-bearing number. If a model writes the formula, you still walk a sample row by hand.

Why the common move fails: decoration is how dirty data hides. A chart of a dirty table is a confident lie with a legend.

The idea

The Big Idea

Clean Table first. Almost every spreadsheet disaster is a structure disaster: mixed units, extra header rows, numbers stored as text, totals mixed into data. Formulas, pivots, and charts are easy once the table is a table.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

A spreadsheet holds data, relations, and displays. Do not make one tab do all three without naming them.

Data tab — a Clean Table: one header row; one record per row; one variable per column; no merged cells; no blank rows for air; no totals inside the data; consistent categories (West, not "West "/"west"/"W"); numbers as numbers, dates as dates.

Work tab. Formulas that point at the data.

Display. A small summary and a chart. Pretty is allowed. Pretty is not allowed to be the only place the number exists.

Category, not brand: Excel, Google Sheets, and cousins (LibreOffice Calc, Apple Numbers). Buttons move. The ideas do not.

Sort and filter. If you cannot see only West, you do not have a table yet.

IF. A decision in a cell. Prefer a column of TRUE/FALSE over a nest of clever IFs.

SUMIFS. Add numbers that meet conditions. "West, last quarter, product A" without a pivot if the question is simple.

XLOOKUP (or INDEX/MATCH as the same idea). A key — SKU, email, region — and a value from another table. VLOOKUP is the ancestor; it breaks when you insert a column. XLOOKUP exists in both Excel and Google Sheets as of the mid-2020s. INDEX/MATCH is the same idea in two functions: key, return column, exact match unless you have a very good reason.

PivotTables. Rows = Region, values = Sum of Amount. If the pivot looks insane, the table is dirty.

Charts. After the summary is true. Label axes, start at zero unless you have a reason, no 3-D. 6.6 is how charts lie.

Percentages, averages, outliers. A percentage needs a denominator you can name. An average is pulled by outliers; look at median or a sorted list when the question is "typical." Scan min and max before you present a mean.

AI can write formulas. You still check them. Walk three rows.

What's changing. Copilot-in-the-sheet, Gemini-in-the-sheet, and whatever is next will draft formulas and "insights." Treat them as Jonah: fast, not the owner. Function names migrate. Clean tables do not.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Clean Table

  1. Copy the raw file to a tab called RAW. Do not type in it again.
  2. Make DATA as a true table. Headers in row 1. No merges.
  3. Fix types. Amounts to numbers. Dates to dates. Trim spaces. Normalize Region.
  4. Kill the handwritten total. Totals live in formulas.
  5. Write the question in a cell: "Is West behind on revenue vs other regions, last quarter?"
  6. Answer with SUMIFS or a pivot on a tab called ANSWER.
  7. Check. Count of rows. Sum vs a filter. One lookup walked by hand.
  8. Assumptions box. What you excluded, what you could not clean, date of the export.
  9. One chart, if it helps Elena see — not if it helps you feel done.
Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

Fifteen minutes, dirty export: paste into a new sheet. Delete extra headers and footer totals. Freeze the header. Filter. =SUBTOTAL(9, range) or =SUMIFS with the one condition you care about. Write the number and the row count. Do not chart yet. A true number beats a false picture.

The hack

The Hack

Units in a column, not in the cell. 100 in Amount and USD in Currency, not "$100 (est.)". Text that looks like a number will sit out of your SUM.

Second: a mapping table for dirty categories (west / West / W → West), then =XLOOKUP(A2, Map[Dirty], Map[Clean], "UNMAPPED"). UNMAPPED is work you still owe. Do not hide it in a chart.

AI move

The AI Move

Use AI for: drafting a formula from a plain-language job; explaining a formula you inherited; suggesting how to split a column that contains two facts.

Do not use AI for: believing a formula without walking rows; "insights" that ignore dirty categories; sending a number you have not reproduced.

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Context: Clean Table (or making one). Category: spreadsheet (Excel or Google Sheets).
Headers: [list them exactly]
Question: [one sentence]
Sample rows (redacted):
[3–5 rows, no real names]

1) Is this a Clean Table, and what to fix?
2) One formula or pivot layout that answers the question.
3) A check: verify with a filter or a hand count.
Prefer SUMIFS, XLOOKUP, or a pivot over nested IFs. Exact match on lookups.
Do not invent functions that do not exist in [Excel / Sheets].
Output: steps, formula, worked example on my sample rows.
Verification: compute the sample by hand in words so I can tick it.

What to verify: recalculate the sample. If the model's arithmetic on five rows is wrong, the formula is not a gift.

Do not paste: real customer lists, salaries with names, health.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "answer a question with a reproducible number."

Category: spreadsheets. Current examples: Excel, Google Sheets, desktop cousins. Databases and BI tools (the category that currently includes names like Power BI, Looker, Tableau) are what you graduate to when the table no longer fits a sheet. Do not graduate as a way of avoiding a Clean Table.

When not to use a sheet: when the object is a document. Prose in grid cells is how people build unmaintainable monsters.

What's changing. AI will write more formulas. Collaboration (who can edit) matters more than which logo is in the corner. Export still matters — 6.1's lock-in question.

Examples

Worked mini-examples

Do these once with your hands.

Example 1 — SUMIFS and a percentage.

SaleIDRegionAmount
101West400
102East500
103West100
104North500
105West200

West: =SUMIFS(C2:C6, B2:B6, "West") → 700. All: =SUM(C2:C6) → 1700. West share: 700/1700 ≈ 41%.

Is West "behind"? Not from this toy set — West is 700, others 500 each. If Elena's fear came from two lost deals, those deals are not in the table, so they must not sneak into the sentence. The table answers what is in the table.

Example 2 — XLOOKUP.

Prices: A-1 = 10, B-2 = 25, C-3 = 40.

Order 1, SKU B-2, Qty 3: =XLOOKUP(B2, Prices[SKU], Prices[Price], "MISSING") → 25. Line = 75. Walk it. If you see MISSING, your key is dirty. Do not paper over MISSING with a zero. Zero looks like a free item.

INDEX/MATCH of the same idea: =INDEX(PriceColumn, MATCH(SKU, SKUColumn, 0)) — the 0 is exact match. That concept travels even if function names shift.

Example 3 — average, outlier, pivot-shaped summary.

RepDealsRevenue
Ana1050
Bo945
Cam840
Dee1200

Mean revenue: 83.75. Median: 47.5. Dee is the outlier — one large deal. If Elena asks "how are reps doing typically?", the mean is a liar. If she asks "where did the money come from?", Dee is the story. A pivot (Rep as rows, Sum of Revenue, Count of Deals) makes that visible. Then a chart of the summary, not of the raw mess.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

People calculate in the same grid they present, then overwrite a formula with a typed "fix." Three months later the input changes and the total does not. A number that does not come from a formula is a claim. Treat it like 6.4.

Recovery: do not "clean in place" on the only copy. RAW tab. New DATA tab. Rebuild the answer. Leave the monster for autopsy.

Defense

The Defense

Fails when the export is not a table (scans, screenshots — say so; do not pretend OCR is a source until you have sampled it); when you are not the owner of the definition ("revenue" means four things — write yours in the assumptions box); when two people sort at once (table object, pivot on a separate tab, never sort RAW).

This will not make you a financial modeler. It will make you the person who can answer Elena at 5:00 without inventing Jonah's total.

Council

The Masters' Table

Grace Hopper. Debug the data before you debug the universe. The bug is often a space in "West ".

Bill Gates. A reusable table multiplies. A one-off pretty grid does not.

John Tukey. Look — min, max, a sort — before you trust a summary. Outliers are either errors or the story.

Florence Nightingale. A display is for a decision. If the chart does not change what Elena can do at 5:00, skip it.

Claude Shannon. A merged cell is noise in the encoding.

Synthesis. Structure first, summary second, picture last, and a human still walks three rows.

Where the council could be wrong. Some questions are too small for the ritual. Adding two receipts in a cell is allowed. The ritual is for the moment when a number will travel.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Calm. You do not argue with Jonah's total in chat. You copy RAW.

Observe. Dirty regions, text amounts, a handwritten total, a question about West vs "loud deals."

Define. A defensible sentence about West, last quarter, with a method Elena can see.

Verify. You do not trust the footer. You will rebuild.

Prioritize. Clean Region and Amount. Time-box. A true West sum beats a full data dictionary.

Find. Export date. Definition of "behind." Finance's known issues.

Options. Pretty slide. Jonah's total. Or Clean Table + check.

Risks. Eighty minutes of cleaning. A wrong mapping. A chart that makes West look catastrophic by chopping the axis.

Choose. Clean Table, one summary, one check, assumptions box.

Communicate. "West is X% of last-quarter revenue in this export, Y vs East. Two lost deals are not in the file, so I will not mix them in. Here's the pivot and the row count. Jonah's footer is a typed number; I'm not using it."

Act. DATA, ANSWER, assumptions. Walk three West rows.

Document. Question, definition, formula, check, timestamp.

Adapt. UNMAPPED regions: fix them or exclude them in the open.

Reflect. Did you answer the question, or decorate the fear?

Practice

Practice

Twenty minutes.

  1. Type Example 1 into a real sheet. Get 700 and 41%.
  2. Add a dirty row: Region west , Amount "200". Watch SUMIFS miss. Fix types and spaces. Recalc.
  3. Build Example 2 with XLOOKUP. Break it with a bad SKU. Confirm MISSING, not 0.
  4. In Example 3, mean and median. One sentence: which number you would say if asked "typical."
  5. Write an assumptions box for Elena in four lines.
Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the 5:00 question. Situation: dirty export, regional fear, a clock. What most people do: a chart in a slide. Why it fails: you cannot reproduce it. Resourceful response: Clean Table, SUMIFS or pivot, check, assumptions. Words to use: "In this export, defined as…" Words to avoid: "Basically," "the AI said West is struggling." What could go wrong: different definitions of West. Write yours.

Money — personal spending. Situation: a bank export, a feeling that "food is out of control." What most people do: glance at the app's category pie. Why it fails: categories are often wrong; the pie hides the outlier (one wedding dinner). Resourceful response: Clean Table, sort by amount, median vs mean, then a category you actually trust. What could go wrong: a shame spiral. The job is a number, not a verdict on your soul.

Family — the shared trip sheet. Situation: four people, expenses, a tab that looks like a poster. What most people do: more colors. Why it fails: nobody can say who is owed what. Resourceful response: one row per expense: Who paid, Amount, Split, For whom. SUMIFS per person. No merged title. What could go wrong: a formula overwritten by a typed "even enough."

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Elena is already on the customer call: "West is down 40 percent — we'll make it up." You can see from your Clean Table that Jonah compared a partial month to a full month.

You do not humiliate her on a live call. You also do not let a false 40 percent become a commitment.

Private message or a calm unmute: "I don't want us to lock a 40 percent figure. The export doesn't support it — different-length periods. I can send a three-line correction in ten minutes with the comparable window."

Then send the two numbers, the formula, and what you are not claiming. 6.6 will deepen the trick. Today you just refuse to ship it.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Where is your handwritten total hiding?
  • What column in your life is two facts taped together?
  • When did a chart last make you feel finished before you were accurate?
  • If finance asked you to reproduce a number from last month, could you?
  • Which formula have you never walked row-by-row?
Remember

Remember This

Pretty is not clean. If you cannot filter it, you cannot trust it.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Spreadsheets became the world's default database because they sit in the gap between a scrap of paper and a real system. That gap is where most of work lives. It is also where most silent errors live: a shifted range, a lookup that approximately matches, a pivot cached on old data, a hidden filter.

Tukey's exploratory data analysis: look before you model. Nightingale's charts were arguments with a body count; she would not have started with a 3-D pie. Hopper's practice: isolate, simplify, reproduce.

Harder variation: take a sheet you did not build. Rebuild the load-bearing number on a new tab without looking at the original formulas. Then compare. The difference is the lesson.

On AI-written formulas: treat them like code from a stranger. Many are fine. The ones that are not will fail on the empty cell, the extra space, the approximate match, the range that stops at row 99 because the sample had 99 rows. Walk the ugly rows, not the first three.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24–48 hours, take one real list — expenses, a work export, a roster — and turn it into a Clean Table.

Answer one question with a formula or a pivot. Write the question in a cell. Write the check. Send the answer to someone who needed it, with the definition.
