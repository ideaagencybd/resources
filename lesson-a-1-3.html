Attention Is a Budget

Protect the only scarce resource you actually have

24 minOne Screen, One Aim4,039 words22 sections
Skill
Spend attention on purpose
Tool
One Screen, One Aim
Result
You stop leaking focus into every ping
In one line
Attention is spent, not found.
The idea

The Hook

You do not have an attention problem. You have a spending problem.

The morning is intact for about eleven minutes. Then a badge lights up. Then a second tab, because the first one is loading. Then a message that is not urgent and feels like it is. By 10:40 you have touched twelve objects and finished none of them. You feel busy. You are not. You are leaking.

People talk about focus as if it were a virtue you either have or lack, like perfect pitch. It is not. It is a budget. You wake up with a limited amount of clean attention. Every switch makes a withdrawal. Some withdrawals are worth it. Most of the ones you make before lunch are not.

This lesson is not a sermon about monkhood. You live in a world that sells your attention back to you as a service. The resourceful move is not to pretend the world is a cabin. It is to spend on purpose, on one screen, toward one aim, for a stretch of time you can actually defend.

If you cannot protect attention, the Pause from 1.1 has nothing to work with. The gap fills with other people's priorities.

The room

The Scenario

Thursday, 9:22 a.m. Jonah has a ninety-minute block to finish a technical note Elena needs before a 1:00 call.

He opens the document. He also opens email "just to check." Fourteen unread. One is from Elena: "Can you glance at this when you have a second?" The second is a calendar nibble. The third is a thread he is copied on for politics, not work.

His phone buzzes on the desk, face up. A group chat is having a joke. He thumbs a reply. When he looks back at the document, the sentence he was about to write is gone. He rereads the last two paragraphs to find it. While he rereads, Slack lights up. Mara asks a reasonable question that is not his job this morning.

It is 9:41. He has not written a useful line. He feels behind, so he works faster, which means he switches more.

The 1:00 call will still happen.

What does he do with the next forty minutes?

Your move

Your Move

Do not keep reading yet. Name the next thing you would actually click. Be honest.

Then look at the four families of move.

Common move. Try to do all of it in parallel. Glance at Elena, answer Mara, keep the document "open," phone face up. You will feel responsive. You will hand Elena a thin note and call it a morning.

Better move. Close the phone, keep Slack visible "in case." Half a door. The half is how residue gets in.

Expert move. One screen, one aim, a visible wall. Write the aim in one line at the top of the document: Finish the technical note through section 3. Close everything else. Put a time on it. Tell Elena, in one sentence, when the glance will happen — not "soon."

Resourceful move. Pause long enough to feel the lean toward the badge. Name the weather if the lean is anxiety (behind, guilty, scatter). Then One Screen, One Aim. The pause is how you stop treating every ping as a fire. The tool is how you spend the hour.

Why the common move fails: switching is not free. You pay twice — once to leave, once to return — and you pay in a currency you cannot see, so you keep spending.

What can go wrong with the expert move: if Elena's "glance" is actually a landmine, a delayed reply can cost you. The resourceful version includes a ten-second sort: is this a real interrupt or a polite one? You will get this wrong sometimes. You will still be richer than the person who never sorts.

The idea

The Big Idea

Attention residue is the tax you pay for living in pieces.

You do not rise to the quality of your intentions. You fall to the number of open loops in the last hour. Single-tasking is not a personality. It is the default that makes every other skill in this course cheaper.

The useful move is to treat attention like money you can see. One screen. One aim. A start and a stop. Everything else is a request, not a right.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Sophie Leroy, in a 2009 paper in Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, gave this tax a name: attention residue. When you switch from Task A to Task B, part of your attention stays with A. You are not fully on B. The leftover is not a mood. It is a performance cost. Residue is worse when A is unfinished, which is the normal condition of modern work.

That is the mechanism behind the morning that feels full and produces nothing. You did not "lack discipline." You ran a design that guarantees residue.

Herbert Simon said it in 1971, in a line that has been quoted until it is furniture: a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. The sentence is still the right diagnosis. Your tools got better at delivering information. They did not get better at protecting the budget.

A few clarifications, because this topic attracts prophets.

Cal Newport's Deep Work (2016) is a popular and useful argument for long, protected stretches of concentration. Use the argument. Do not join a church. Most jobs cannot offer four silent hours, and pretending they can is how people feel morally behind. The operational claim of this lesson is smaller: default to one aim at a time, for a stretch you can actually defend — twenty-five minutes counts — and treat notifications as requests that must earn entry.

"Multitasking" as people mean it is usually rapid switching. The brain can automate a walk while talking. It cannot write a careful paragraph while also performing inbox. The first is parallel. The second is residue with extra steps.

You do not need a neuroscience lecture. You need a closed door, even if the door is a setting.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: One Screen, One Aim

Use it for any stretch of work that actually needs a mind.

  1. Write the aim in one line, before you touch the work. Not "get through email." A finish line: Send Elena the note through section 3. If you cannot write the aim, you are not ready to open a screen. You are grazing.
  2. Choose one screen. One document, one deck, one inbox if inbox is the aim. Not one screen plus a "monitor for Slack." The second surface is a second aim pretending to be a safety policy.
  3. Close the rest on purpose. Phone face down, in another room if the room exists. Notifications off for the block. Browser tabs that are not the aim, gone. You are not a monk. You are a person who has met residue.
  4. Put a start and a stop on a clock you can see. Twenty-five minutes is enough to count. Ninety is luxury. The stop matters as much as the start: an endless block becomes a fog, and fog invites checking.
  5. Park the interrupts on a paper list, not in your body. When a thought arrives — reply to Mara, Elena's glance, the joke in the chat — write one line and return. The list is how you tell your nervous system the loop is not lost.
  6. At the stop, do a thirty-second close. What got done. What is the next aim. What waits. Then, and only then, open the other screens.

Write this on a card:

Aim → One Screen → Clock → Park The Rest → Close.

That is the whole tool. Do not add a ritual playlist and a candle until you can do these five when Elena is waiting.

A worked pass on Jonah's morning:

  • Aim: Finish the note through section 3 by 10:30.
  • Screen: the document only.
  • Elena's glance: parked, with a time — "I'll look at 10:35 and reply by 10:50."
  • Mara: parked, or a fifteen-word holding reply if she is blocked: "In a focus block until 10:30. If this is a blocker, call."
  • Phone: in a bag.
Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

When you do not have a beautiful morning — open office, on-call, a child in the next room:

Take ninety seconds.

Write the aim on a sticky note. Mute the noisiest channel. Set a timer for twelve minutes. Work only the aim until the timer. Then look.

Twelve minutes is not deep work. It is a proof that you can spend on purpose. Stack two if the room allows. If a real interrupt arrives — a true blocker, a safety issue, a boss at your shoulder — you take it. You do not take the joke in the group chat and call it being a team player.

A holding sentence for humans who expect instant access:

  • "I'm heads-down until :30. If it's a blocker, call."
  • "Got it — I'll hit this at 11:00."
  • "One thing at a time this morning. Yours is second. I'll start it at 10:40."

People who never say these sentences are not more helpful. They are more interruptible. Helpfulness that never finishes is a costume.

The hack

The Hack

Make the first click expensive and the aim cheap.

The cheap version of the aim sits already open: the document, the paragraph, the problem. The expensive version of distraction sits behind a wall: phone in another room, Slack set to "pause notifications," email closed, not merely unclicked.

Most people do the reverse. They make Slack one keystroke and the real work three clicks deep. Of course the budget goes to Slack. You designed it that way.

If you work in text, the hack is the same one as the Pause: draft in a window that cannot send. Inbox is a slot machine. A notes file is a desk.

Do not wait to "feel focused." Focus is often the result of the block, not the entry ticket.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is excellent at helping you name the aim and park the rest. It is terrible at doing your focusing for you. A model that summarizes your unread mail while you "stay in flow" can become a second residue engine if you let it keep tapping you.

Use AI for:

  • Turning a messy morning into one written aim and a parked list
  • Drafting the holding sentences you will send before a block
  • A pre-mortem: "How will I break this block in the first ten minutes?"

Do not use AI for:

  • An endless research prelude that delays the actual screen
  • Monitoring every channel "so you don't have to"
  • Feeding it other people's private threads so it can tell you what to care about

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Context: I have a limited attention budget this morning.
Here is the work that actually matters by [time]:
[one or two outcomes]
Here is everything currently pulling on me:
[list, messy is fine]
Here is how long I truly have: [minutes]
Here is my usual failure mode: [e.g. "I open email to 'just check'"]

Goal:
1) One written AIM for the next block (one sentence, finish-line language).
2) A PARK list: what waits, and when I will touch it.
3) What I will close or mute.
4) Two holding sentences I can send in under 20 words.
5) The first two minutes of the work, so I do not graze.

Constraints: No productivity theater. No 4-hour fantasy. Fit the minutes I actually have.
Output: Labeled lists. One line on the main risk.
Verification: What am I calling urgent that is only loud?

What to verify: The model does not know Elena's politics. If you labeled everything "urgent," it will help you stay scattered with better formatting.

Do not paste: confidential mail, customer data, personnel issues, anything you would not put on a postcard.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "insert friction before a switch, remove friction from the aim."

Current examples of the category, not a shopping list you must buy:

  • Notification pause / focus modes on the phone and the computer
  • Full-screen the aim; hide the dock; one monitor if two monitors have become two lives
  • Site and app blockers during a named block
  • A physical timer you can see without unlocking a phone
  • Inbox delay-send and scheduled look-times (two or three a day beats a day of grazing)

When not to use technology: if the setup takes longer than the block. A forty-minute ritual for a twelve-minute focus is another form of residue. Also: if you are on-call for real safety, do not mute the channel that carries the fire. Mute the joke channel. Discernment is the skill. Tools are furniture.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

People try to protect attention by becoming rude, or they try to stay kind by becoming available to everyone.

The first produces a reputation that costs more than the residue. The second produces a morning with no output and a self-story about being helpful.

Why it happens: availability is visible. Deep work is invisible until it ships. Organizations reward the visible.

Recovery: if you already spent the morning in pieces, do not punish yourself with a heroic afternoon. Run one honest block. Send two holding sentences. Close the loops you opened. Shame is just more residue.

A second mistake: confusing "I work well with background noise" with "I can write a careful argument while answering Slack." Maybe you can code with music. You still cannot hold two aims. Be precise about what you are actually doing.

Defense

The Defense

One Screen, One Aim fails when:

  • The job is genuinely interrupt-driven — support, incident response, a floor of a hospital, a classroom. Then the tool shrinks: one aim between interrupts, and a real triage rule for what breaks the block.
  • You use the block to hide. A person who is always "heads-down" during the hard conversation is not focused. They are avoiding. The Pause still applies.
  • Your aim is fake. "Clear the inbox" is not an aim; it is a lifestyle. Name an outcome.
  • You are depleted. A starved, sleepless body will not hold a block. That is the next lesson — State Before Strategy. Do not moralize a biology problem.

Limitation: this will not give you a monastery. It will give you a few protected spends per day. That is enough to change the quality of a week.

Council

The Masters' Table

Sophie Leroy. Through the residue lens: switching is not a clean cut. Part of you stays with the last unfinished thing. Design for completion, or at least for a deliberate park, before you open the next object.

Herbert Simon. Through the scarcity lens: information is abundant; attention is not. Every tool that "keeps you informed" is making a claim on the budget. Make it apply.

William James. Through the attention-as-selection lens: what you attend to is your experience of the day. A day of pings is a day made of pings, even if you also "meant" to write the note.

Cal Newport. Through the deep-work lens, as a popularizer not a prophet: long, protected concentration produces work that shallow switching cannot. Steal the protection. Ignore any implication that your job is invalid if it is not a four-hour cave.

Simone Weil. Through the moral lens: attention is a form of care. To attend to one thing is to refuse, for a time, to attend to another. That refusal can be love (the child, the page, the patient). It can also be neglect. Choose the refusal on purpose.

Synthesis. The council agrees that attention is not a mood you wait for. It is a spend you design. Residue is the cost of pretending otherwise.

Where the council could be wrong. A cult of uninterrupted focus can become contempt for the people whose job is to interrupt you — the junior with a blocker, the partner with a real day, the client on fire. Weil's care cuts both ways. If One Screen, One Aim makes you unreachable to the actual humans you are responsible for, you have built a nicer form of selfishness. The budget includes other people. It does not include every badge.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 9:22 a.m.

Calm. Jonah feels the lean toward email. He does not click yet. One breath. Shoulders.

Observe. Facts: Elena needs a note before 1:00. She also asked for a glance. Mara asked a question. The group chat is noise. He has ninety minutes of claimed time and a brain that is already splitting.

Define. The job of 9:22 to 10:30 is the note through section 3. Everything else is a request.

Verify. Is Elena's glance a landmine? He opens only that one line of her message — not the inbox. It is a non-blocking "when you have a second." Parked.

Prioritize. The note. Then Elena's glance at 10:35. Then Mara. Chat last, or never.

Find resources / people. He does not need a meeting. He needs a closed Slack and the spec.

Options. Graze. Half-close. Or One Screen, One Aim plus two holding sentences.

Risks. Elena may want the glance sooner. Mara may be blocked. The note may be worse if he writes it in pieces.

Choose. One screen. One aim. Two short messages.

Communicate. To Elena: "Heads-down on the 1:00 note until 10:30. I'll glance at yours at 10:35." To Mara: "In a block until 10:30. Call if I'm the blocker."

Act. Phone in a bag. Document full-screen. Timer 9:25–10:25. Park list at the elbow.

Document. At 10:25: what section 3 still needs, what the glance requires, what Mara asked.

Adapt. If Mara calls, he takes it. If the chat pings, he does not.

Reflect. Where did the lean happen? Which interrupt was a real one? Did the holding sentences cost him anything that mattered?

That is a resourceful human with a budget. Not a hermit. A spender.

Practice

Practice

Do this now. Fifteen minutes.

  1. List everything that pulled your attention yesterday before lunch. Be crude. Aim for twelve items.
  2. Circle the two that actually moved a result.
  3. Write today's one aim for the next block you can defend — one sentence, finish-line language.
  4. Write the park list: what waits, and when you will touch it.
  5. Draft two holding sentences in your actual voice.
  6. Set a timer for twelve minutes and start the aim ugly. No extra tabs.

Then, for the rest of the day, put the phone face down during any conversation with a human in the room. You are training the body that a badge is not a name being called.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the "quick glance." Situation: a manager asks you to look at something while you are in the only block that will save the afternoon. What most people do: look now, to seem loyal. Why it fails: loyalty that never finishes the main thing is not loyalty. It is anxiety with good manners. Resourceful response: ten-second sort (blocker or not), then a time, then the block. Words to use: "I'll hit this at 10:35." Words to avoid: "Sorry, I'm slammed," which invites negotiation, and "Sure, one sec," which is a lie. Tool: One Screen, One Aim. AI assist: use the prompt to turn a messy list into one aim and a park list. What could go wrong: you mis-sort a real landmine. If the person is above you and the culture punishes delay, shorten the block, do not abolish it.

Family — the half-present evening. Situation: a partner or child is talking; you are "listening" with a phone in your hand. What most people do: glance, nod, miss the actual sentence, then feel confused when the room goes cold. Why it fails: they can see the budget. You spent it elsewhere. Resourceful response: one screen — the human. Phone in another room for twenty minutes. If you must be on-call, say so: "I have to watch for a work call until 8. After that I'm here." Words to use: the honest constraint. Words to avoid: "I'm listening" while scrolling. Tool: One Screen, One Aim, applied to a person. What could go wrong: you announce presence and then sneak the phone. Do not make a promise the badge will break.

Travel — the airport hour. Situation: a delay, a dying battery, a hundred other people's calls. What most people do: graze news and mail until boarding, then work in a panic on the jet bridge. Resourceful response: pick one aim for the hour (the note, the rest, the call you have been avoiding). Headphones as a door. The rest of the airport is not your inbox.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Same morning. Worse.

Elena's "glance" is actually: "The client moved the call to 11:00. I need you on it." Mara's question is a production blocker. Jonah slept badly. The group chat is still noise, and now it includes a political argument.

One Screen, One Aim does not mean he ignores Elena. It means he rewrites the aim in thirty seconds instead of pretending he can do three aims.

New aim: Be useful on the 11:00 call. Next actions, in order: confirm with Elena (one minute), unblock Mara (five minutes, or hand it off), then one screen on the two facts the client will ask for. Phone still down for the chat. The political argument is not a work object.

The tool survived because the aim was allowed to change on evidence, not on a badge.

If his hands want to open four things at once, that is the lean. Pause. Name the weather (behind, heat, scatter). Then one screen. You already know this sequence. Use it.

Reflect

Reflection

  • What is the first click you make in the morning, and who does it serve?
  • Which channel have you allowed to call your name without earning it?
  • When has being reachable cost more than it bought?
  • What holding sentence will you actually say, in your mouth?
  • After you protect a block, do you use it — or do you decorate it with setup?
Remember

Remember This

Every ping is a request for your life. Most of them can wait.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

A short history of the budget.

William James treated attention as selection: a world of too much, a mind that must choose. Simon, working on organizations and computers, saw that information would not be the scarce thing. Newport, writing for knowledge workers drowning in mail, popularized a counter-practice: long, unbroken work on cognitively hard things. Leroy measured what the others described — the leftover attention that makes "I'll just check" expensive.

None of this requires you to believe that the industrial-era office was a golden age, or that Slack is evil, or that the only serious people are novelists in cabins. The empirical core is modest. Switching with unfinished work leaves residue. Notifications harvest the budget because they are designed to. Single-tasking, for a named stretch, is the cheapest reliable countermeasure.

On "flow": Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi described a state of absorption that people find in well-matched challenge. It is real enough as an experience. It is a poor daily plan. You cannot schedule flow. You can schedule a block in which flow is allowed to happen. Do not wait for the feeling. Build the conditions.

If you want a practice beyond this lesson, run five One Screen, One Aim blocks in five days. Track only two numbers: minutes of intended block, minutes until first unjustified switch. The second number going up is not the win. The first number being real, and the switch being chosen, is the win.

You will need this budget again in Level 5 (execution) and Level 6 (digital tools). A person who cannot spend attention on purpose will use AI to become a faster grazer.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24 hours, run one One Screen, One Aim block of at least twenty-five minutes on work that actually matters.

Afterward, write four lines:

  1. The written aim
  2. What you closed or muted
  3. The first interrupt that arrived, and whether it earned entry
  4. What shipped

If you never started the clock, that is data. Run it tomorrow before you open the inbox.
