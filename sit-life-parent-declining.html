A parent is declining and refusing help

high The Smallest Acceptable Stepageingfamilycapacitycaring
1

What is happening

There are unopened bills, food going off, a bruise they will not explain, a car they should not be driving. When you raise it, you are told firmly that they are perfectly fine.

2

What is actually going on

  • The resistance is almost never about the help. It is about the meaning of accepting it: that independence is ending and this is the direction of travel.
  • An adult with capacity has the right to make bad decisions. That is genuinely the law and it is genuinely maddening.
  • Sudden confusion, falls, or personality change are frequently medical and reversible — infection, dehydration, medication interaction. Do not accept "just getting old" for a sudden change.
  • The sibling who lives nearest does most and resents it; the one furthest away has the strongest opinions. This is nearly universal and worth naming early.
3

Do this, in this order

  1. Get a medical review for anything suddenRapid changes in cognition or balance need a doctor within days. Rule out the reversible causes first.
  2. Start with the smallest, least symbolic helpA cleaner, a shopping delivery, a pill organiser. Framed as convenience, not as care.
  3. Offer choice inside a fixed frameNot "do you want help" but "would you rather I came Tuesdays or we got someone in?" Autonomy preserved, direction maintained.
  4. Fix the safety items before the lifestyle onesStairs, bathroom, heating, smoke alarms, the hob, the driving. Prioritise what can kill them.
  5. Sort the paperwork while they can still choosePower of attorney, will, wishes about care. Doing it later is vastly harder and sometimes impossible.
  6. Get the siblings onto one channelA shared note of what is happening, who does what, and what is decided. Most family conflict here is information asymmetry.
4

Words you can borrow

Framing help as convenience
"I am not suggesting you cannot manage. I am saying I would worry less, and I am asking you to do this one for me."
The bounded choice
"The bathroom needs a rail. Would you rather choose it yourself or should I just get one that matches?"
On driving
"I am not going to argue about your driving. I am asking you to have the assessment, and I will drive you to it."
To siblings
"Here is what is actually happening each week. I need one of you to take the finances and one to take appointments."
5

What makes it worse

  • Removing autonomy by stealth — cancelling things, taking keys, deciding for them. It produces entrenchment and often lasting resentment.
  • The big confrontational family meeting as an opener. It reads as an ambush and hardens the no.
  • Threatening a care home as leverage.
  • Accepting "I am fine" for a bruise, a burn, or missing money without gently establishing what happened.
  • Doing everything yourself until you break. That is the most common outcome and it helps nobody.
6

Once the pressure drops

  • Keep a simple dated log of incidents. It is what gets services engaged and it prevents the "you are exaggerating" argument.
  • Arrange your own respite deliberately. Carer collapse is a predictable event, not a personal failing.
  • Find out what is available locally — carer assessments, day services, adaptations, allowances. Most goes unclaimed.
  • Have the harder conversation once, calmly: what they would want if they could not decide. It is a gift to everyone later.
Where this stops being yours to handle

If there are signs of financial exploitation, physical harm, neglect, or a new carer controlling access to them, contact adult safeguarding. If confusion has come on over hours or days, treat it as a medical emergency.

A note on this library

These are general principles written to be useful under pressure, not legal, medical, or financial advice. Laws and emergency procedures differ by country. If life is at risk, contact your emergency services first and read this afterwards.
