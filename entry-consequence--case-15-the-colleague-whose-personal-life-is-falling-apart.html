Case 15 — The Colleague Whose Personal Life Is Falling Apart

606 words

The Situation

A colleague you know socially is going through a clearly difficult personal situation — divorce, health, family — and their work is visibly suffering. Their manager (also your peer) is losing patience.

Your Move — Decide Before Reading On

Option A

Stay out of it. Not your team, not your business.

What likely happens:

Your colleague loses their job in three months. In retrospect, a well-timed conversation from you might have changed the trajectory. You will remember this.

What you gained:

Boundaries preserved. No political exposure.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

The loss of a colleague who might have made it through with modest support. Your own sense of what kind of colleague you want to be.

What you should have noticed:

The workplace is not just an instrumental relationship. Colleagues are also human. Sometimes the professional and human dimensions call for the same response.

Option B

Reach out to the colleague personally. Not as a manager, not as a fixer. As a colleague. 'I've noticed you're going through a hard time. I don't need to know the details. I just want to say I'm here if you need someone to think through work — or just to have coffee.'

What likely happens:

One of two things: (1) they accept the offer and use you as a sounding board, and the modest support helps them stabilize; (2) they decline politely, but remember the gesture. Either way, you have done the human thing.

What you gained:

The relationship. Your integrity. Modest but real help.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Some time. Emotional intensity. Occasionally, being drawn into a situation beyond what you signed up for.

What you should have noticed:

The offer is different from the obligation. Making the offer is generous; the response is theirs.

Option C

Talk to their manager. Advocate for compassionate handling.

What likely happens:

The manager either was already compassionate and is grateful for the confirmation, or was not and is now aware that colleagues are watching. Depending on the manager, this may or may not help. In either case, you have added information to the system without violating any confidence.

What you gained:

Systemic possibility of a better outcome for the colleague.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Political exposure. The manager may read your intervention as intrusive.

What you should have noticed:

There is a difference between supporting a colleague directly and advocating for them upward. Both are legitimate; both carry different risks.

The Wrap

B, primarily. Sometimes B combined with a light-touch version of C (a comment to the manager only if the manager is receptive and if the situation genuinely warrants it). The workplace is one of the few places adults spend enough time to notice when a colleague is in serious trouble. The offer of quiet human support costs almost nothing and matters more than we think in the years afterward.

The pattern beneath the pattern

Fifteen scenarios. If you look across them, a small set of moves keeps producing the best outcomes:

  • Slow down before consequential decisions.
  • Address the actual issue, not the surface one.
  • Refuse to trade long-term reputation for short-term convenience.
  • When in doubt, escalate through institutional process rather than acting unilaterally.
  • Give credit generously; take blame cleanly.
  • Say the honest thing, kindly.
  • Preserve optionality wherever possible.
  • Treat colleagues as human beings — because they are — while maintaining professional boundaries.

None of these are dramatic. Together, they compound into a career that ages well and a reputation that outlasts any specific role.

Remember This

“Every choice has consequences. Make them visible. Choose accordingly.”
