Deep Dive 2: The Craft of Communication

890 words

From competent to excellent.

Level 4 covered the operational essentials. This section is for readers who want to communicate at a level that gets remembered. It is the difference between technique and craft.

The four modes of professional writing

Most professional writing falls into one of four modes. The same person may need to shift between them multiple times per day. Confusing the modes is the source of much bad workplace writing.

MODEPURPOSEKEY MOVE
INFORMDeliver facts, updates, decisions.Lead with the news. Be specific. Cut hedging.
PERSUADEChange what someone believes or does.Anticipate their frame. Show respect for the counter-argument. Give them a specific action to take.
EXPLAINMake an unfamiliar thing understandable.One central analogy. Concrete examples. Predict confusion and address it preemptively.
INSPIREMove people emotionally toward action.Story over abstraction. Specific human detail. Restraint (the emotion is the reader's, not yours).

A weekly status update is INFORM. A pitch deck is PERSUADE. A technical doc is EXPLAIN. A team all-hands email after a hard week is INSPIRE. Using explain when you needed to persuade, or inspire when you needed to inform, produces documents nobody remembers.

THE TOOL — Universally useful professional writing patterns

Note

1. The one-line summary 'The core of this is [X].' Every consequential document should be reducible to one sentence. If yours isn't, you don't know what you're writing. 2. The specific ask 'Specifically, I need you to [X] by [Y].' Not 'let me know your thoughts.' Not 'happy to discuss.' A specific request with a specific deadline. 3. The steel-man 'The strongest argument against my position is [X]. Here's how I'd address it: [Y].' Preemptive steel-manning is more persuasive than defending against the actual counterattack later. 4. The graceful decline 'I can't say yes to this without saying no to something else I've already committed to. Here's what I could shift: [X]. Otherwise, I have to pass on this.' Reasoned refusal. 5. The escalated clarification 'I want to make sure I understood correctly. My interpretation is [X]. If that's off, please tell me — I'd rather catch it now than build on the wrong assumption.' Fixes miscommunication before it compounds.

The rhythm of good sentences

Good writing has rhythm. Short sentences deliver impact. Long, developed sentences with subordinate clauses give the reader room to breathe and to notice the argument developing across multiple related clauses. Then short again.

Notice the paragraph above. Short. Long. Short.

You don't have to be a stylist. You do have to vary your sentence length. Uniform sentence length — even if each sentence is technically well-formed — reads as robotic.

Storytelling for professionals

Every good professional story has three moves:

  • A specific setting. Not 'in a meeting.' 'In Priya's office, Tuesday at 4:30, coffee going cold.'
  • A specific problem. Not 'things were tense.' 'The client had just used the word 'unacceptable' three times in two minutes.'
  • A specific resolution. Not 'we worked it out.' 'I asked the one question that reframed the room, and by 5:15 we had a written next-steps document.'

Concrete detail is what makes stories memorable. Abstract summaries are what people scroll past.

The five errors of professional writing

  • HEDGING. 'Just wanted to check…' 'Sorry to bother…' 'Hopefully this makes sense…' Every hedge is a small deposit into the reader's impression that you are unsure of your own point.
  • JARGON. Every profession has its terms of art. Using them WITH people who know them = signal. Using them AT people who don't = wall. Match the audience.
  • PASSIVE VOICE FOR RESPONSIBILITY-AVOIDANCE. 'Mistakes were made' is the classic. 'I made a mistake' or 'Priya's team made an error we did not catch' — active voice is honest voice.
  • LONG PARAGRAPHS. Readers on screens skim by paragraph. A 300-word paragraph is a paragraph nobody reads. Break it up.
  • BURYING THE POINT. The main claim should be in the first sentence, or in a boldable position later. If your reader stops at any point, they should have gotten the point.

THE GREAT MINDS COUNCIL — On writing that lasts

Note

Through William Zinsser's lens: 'Writing is thinking on paper.' If your writing is muddled, your thinking is muddled. Fix the thinking, then the words follow. Through George Orwell's lens: 'Politics and the English Language' — six rules for clear writing: never use a metaphor you're used to seeing in print; never use a long word where a short one will do; if it's possible to cut a word out, always cut it out; never use passive where active is possible; never use a foreign, scientific, or jargon word where a plain English one will do; break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous. Through Ann Handley's lens: Everybody Writes — writing in the professional age is now a survival skill, not a specialty. You will be judged on it more than you realize. Through Paul Graham's lens: 'Write like you talk' — the essay style that reads like actual thought, not performed expertise, is the modern standard. Where the Council might be wrong: 'Write like you talk' works for essays and informal work. Certain formal contexts (legal, medical, regulatory) require precision that colloquial style cannot deliver. Match register to context.

Remember This

“Write like you think. Then edit like you're someone else reading it for the first time.”
