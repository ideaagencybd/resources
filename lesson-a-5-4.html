Map the Machine

Most personal failures are system failures

26 minThe Process Map2,958 words22 sections
Skill

Result

Blame the machine before the person.
The idea

The Hook

Someone dropped the ball. That is the sentence the room likes. It is almost never the whole sentence.

A form sat for six days. A handoff had no owner. Two teams each thought the other was waiting. A hero saved Friday, which trained the machine to need a hero on Friday. Then the hero took a day off.

You can yell at the person holding the ball when the music stopped. You will feel briefly effective. The next ball will drop in the same place, because the place is the problem.

This lesson is boxes and arrows. A sketch of how work really moves — not how the slide says it moves — so you can see the bottleneck, the pile, and the loop that is feeding itself. Deming's blunt teaching: the system produces the result. If you do not like the result, look at the system before you look for a villain.

The room

The Scenario

Friday, 11:05 a.m. Elena's team does "intake."

A customer request arrives in a shared inbox. Sometimes sales forwards a side email. Sometimes a form. Sometimes a Slack DM to whoever seems awake. Mara triages when she has a gap. Jonah builds the thing. A contractor named Priya does quality on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Nobody can say how many requests are waiting. The oldest one is eleven days old and the customer has started CC'ing Elena's boss.

Elena, in The Room: "We need people to be more on it. This is a responsiveness problem."

Jonah, socially blunt: "It's not a people problem. I don't even see half of these until they explode."

Three piles. No picture. Elena turns to you. "Map this. I want to know why we are slow."

Twenty-five minutes until the next meeting. What do you draw?

Your move

Your Move

Common move. A blame map. Names in boxes. "Mara is the bottleneck." Sometimes a person is overloaded. A name is not a map of the work.

Better move. The official flowchart from last year's deck. Tidy. Fiction.

Expert move. A crude current-state sketch: how a request actually arrives, waits, is seen, is built, is checked, is sent. Count the piles. Mark the queue that never goes empty. Mark the loop (rework because the form lacks a field). Whiteboard. No software.

Resourceful move. Walk one real request from birth to now, with the person who touched it. Then three labels only: bottleneck, inventory, loop. Offer Elena a system sentence, not a character sentence: "Three front doors, one person who can say yes, quality in a part-time cul-de-sac. Jonah is not lazy. The machine is built to surprise him."

Why the common move fails: people are visible; queues are not. You will punish the visible.

What can go wrong: a beautiful map of the wrong process, or a map so detailed it cannot be used. Crude and true beats precise and theatrical. You cannot un-accuse a person in The Room once Elena has a name.

The idea

The Big Idea

If you cannot draw the machine, you are condemned to heroics.

A process map is not bureaucracy. It is how you stop arguing about characters and start arguing about paths, piles, and pace. Most "performance problems" are unlabeled queues. Most "we need better people" speeches are a manager standing inside a machine they have never seen.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Work is a flow, even when it looks like a pile of emails.

A process turns an input into an output. Boxes are steps. Arrows are handoffs. Anything that sits is inventory — work-in-progress. It looks like progress because it exists. It is often delay wearing a folder.

A bottleneck is the step with the least capacity. Eliyahu Goldratt's The Goal (1984) made this vivid in a factory novel: the pace of the system is the pace of the constraint. Sprint upstream of a bottleneck all day and you only grow the pile in front of it.

A feedback loop is an arrow that comes back. Some loops are healthy: a check that teaches the form to include the field. Some are parasites: rework, extra approvals, a meeting that exists to compensate for a handoff with no owner. If you do not draw the loop, you will treat the rework as a personality.

W. Edwards Deming taught managers to stop hunting workers as the default cause. In Out of the Crisis he estimated that the large majority of troubles — he used figures like 94%, later even higher — belong to the system, as common causes, not to special villains. Those numbers are teaching estimates, not a lab measurement of your office. The operational claim: do not blame people first. Ask what machine would produce this result even with decent humans in the boxes.

A household is a machine. A morning is a machine. Level 3's resourcefulness finds a path. This lesson makes the path visible so you can change it.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Process Map

Use it when a result keeps disappointing you and the room wants a name.

  1. Name the output. One sentence. "A customer request becomes a delivered, checked thing they know about."
  2. Pick one real case. Last week's painful one. Walk it. Where did it sit? Who could not see it?
  3. Draw boxes and arrows. Current state, not should-state. Every front door gets a box. Every wait gets a pile. Ten boxes is plenty.
  4. Label three things only, first pass. Bottleneck (always busy while others wait, or only one person can do it). Inventory (work sitting). Loop (work that comes back — write why in three words).
  5. Find the invisible work. Status pings, re-keying, hero saves. If the machine requires a hero, the hero is a hidden step. Draw them.
  6. Write one system sentence. "This result is what you would expect from [structure]." No names unless a role is the constraint ("only Priya can check, two days a week").
  7. Only then, one change. Usually: one front door, make the bottleneck visible, move a check earlier, or give the pile an owner. Not seven initiatives.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

A napkin, one case, five minutes: Arrive → Seen by X → Wait → Done → Checked/Sent. Put a number on the wait. Circle the wait if it is the story. Enough to stop a character argument in The Room.

The hack

The Hack

Map the wait, not the work. People love drawing the clever steps. The delay is in the piles and the loops. Ask: "How old is the oldest thing in this queue?" That question is a flashlight. The person holding the oldest item might have received it this morning.

AI move

The AI Move

AI can turn a messy description into a first-draft map and a first-draft SOP. It cannot see your actual inbox. It will tidy fiction if you feed it fiction.

Use AI for: turning a walkthrough into labeled boxes; drafting a system sentence you then edit; a first-pass SOP after the map is true; meeting notes that capture "how we think it works."

Do not use AI for: a future-state fantasy before current state; naming a colleague as the problem; inventing timings you did not measure.

Prompt (copy, then fill):

WHEN TO USE: Messy process. Need a current-state map, not a redesign speech.

Output we want: [one sentence]
One real case, including waits and "then I pinged X":
"""
[walkthrough]
"""
Front doors: [inbox, form, Slack]
Who can do each step: [roles, not a roast]

Goal:
1) Boxes and arrows in text.
2) Mark bottleneck, inventory, loops.
3) One system sentence with no personal blame.
4) Three possible single changes, with the risk of each.
Constraints: Do not invent steps. Unknown → UNKNOWN.

What to verify: Walk it with someone who does the work. If they laugh, the map is wrong. Their laugh is data.

Do not paste: customer contents, personnel files, anything that identifies a person being blamed.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "make flow and piles visible."

Category: a single queue people can see, plus a sketch of the path. Examples: one intake board with age of ticket; a whiteboard photo in the channel; a simple spreadsheet of arrived / waiting on / age. Fancy workflow tools are optional. Three front doors in three tools is the disease; a fourth tool is not the cure. A photo of a whiteboard beats an unused platform.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Redesigning before seeing. Drawing the current mess is uncomfortable. Drawing a future castle is status. You produce a "new process" that adds two approvals. Inventory grows. Jonah still cannot see the work.

Recovery: throw the castle out. Walk one case. Draw what is. One change. Measure the oldest item's age next Friday. If it did not move, your change was theater.

Defense

The Defense

The Process Map fails when:

  • The work is truly one-off. Do not map a snowflake. Use the Figure-It-Out Loop. If it happens twice, map it.
  • You map at the wrong altitude. Company-wide "value streams" on day one are a stall. One output, one team, one painful case.
  • Power wants a villain. A perfect map will not save you if The Room needs a sacrifice. Tell the system sentence. Do not offer a name to feed it. Manage Up (5.7) is how you brief that.
  • You confuse a bottleneck with a person you dislike. Check: if a competent stranger sat in that box, would the pile still grow? If yes, it is the box.

Limitation: seeing the machine does not fix it. It makes a fix possible. 5.5 tests causes. 5.6 moves work through people. 5.B is the machine already on fire.

Council

The Masters' Table

W. Edwards Deming. A bad system beats a good person. Start with the machine. Training and fear will not compensate for three front doors and an invisible pile.

Eliyahu Goldratt. Protect the constraint. Do not flood it. An idle bottleneck is lost output for the whole system; idle non-bottlenecks are often healthy.

Andy Grove. Managerial work is a production process: indicators, batching, leverage. A meeting can be a step or a pile. Decide which.

Donella Meadows. Leverage often sits in information flows and feedback, not in exhortation. Making the oldest ticket visible can outperform a pep talk.

Peter Drucker. Knowledge work still has an output. If you cannot name it, you cannot map it, and you cannot be effective — only busy.

Synthesis. Draw what is. Find the constraint. Change one information flow or one front door. Leave character out of the first sentence.

Where the council could be wrong. Systems talk can avoid a real performance problem. Sometimes a person is the special cause. Deming's order is first, not never. After the machine is visible, you may still need a hard conversation (4.4).

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 11:05.

Calm. You do not accept "responsiveness problem" as a diagnosis. You also do not throw Elena in front of her team.

Observe. Three front doors. Mara triages in gaps. Jonah cannot see the work. Priya is a two-day cul-de-sac. Oldest item: eleven days.

Define. Twenty-five minutes: a current-state sketch and a system sentence. Not a new religion.

Verify. Ask Mara to walk the eleven-day item. Facts vs Stories.

Prioritize. Visibility of the pile over a new tool. One front door over a campaign about "ownership."

Find / Options / Risks. Mara, Jonah, Priya — the people in the boxes. Elena wanted a name. A system sentence may sound like excuse-making. Make it concrete: numbers, doors, days.

Choose / Communicate. "Three intake doors, no single queue. Jonah sees work when it explodes. Quality sits until Priya's days. Eleven days is what this design produces. One change this afternoon: one visible queue, oldest on top, side DMs dropped into it. I am not asking for a hero weekend."

Act / Document / Adapt / Reflect. Create the queue. Move the eleven-day item to the top. Tell the customer the truth and a date. Photo the map. If sales keeps side-DMing, that is a loop — draw it, then manage the interface (5.7). Did you almost offer Mara as a sacrifice because she was convenient?

Practice

Practice

Fifteen minutes. Real process.

  1. Name an output that keeps slipping.
  2. Walk one real case. Boxes, arrows, waits.
  3. Mark bottleneck, inventory, one loop.
  4. Write a system sentence with no personal blame.
  5. Write one change that does not require a new hire or a new platform.
  6. Note what you still do not know. UNKNOWN is a legal map label.

A house is a machine. You may map "weekday morning" or "how a bill gets paid."

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the invisible pile. Situation: work arrives many ways; someone is always "dropping the ball." What most people do: a speech about accountability. Why it fails: accountability without a queue is theater. Resourceful response: one map, one queue, oldest item named. Words to use: "Show me how a request actually travels." Words to avoid: "Who messed this up?" as the first question. Tool: Process Map. AI assist: turn the walkthrough into a sketch; verify with the people in the boxes. What could go wrong: a tool rollout instead of one front door. Doors first.

Family — the household handoff. Situation: school forms, meals, a bill — each assumed to be "obviously yours." What most people do: a fight about character. Why it fails: there was no map, only resentment. Resourceful response: draw the path of one recurring failure. Put an owner on the wait, not on who cares more. Words to use: "Let's draw how this actually moves." Words to avoid: "You never," "I always." Tool: Process Map, then Owner, Outcome, Deadline (5.6). What could go wrong: using a map to win. The map is a truce document.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Same Friday. Worse.

Elena's boss is in The Room. The customer is on speaker. Priya is out. Jonah says, "This process is stupid," which is true and unhelpful. Sales says they promised 24-hour turnaround. The official flowchart is on the screen, lying.

You do not litigate the flowchart. You do not fight Jonah. You do not confess a villain to the customer.

Napkin map, spoken: "Two facts: multiple intake paths, and the request you care about sat without an owner. I will not guess whose character that is on this call. Every open request goes on one list today, oldest first, and I will send a date we can actually hit. If 24 hours was promised, that promise did not match the machine. I will not repeat it."

Then you map for real with Mara after the call. The Bad Room gets a holding system sentence. The work gets a drawing. 5.B is this situation on fire.

If your hands want to offer a name to make the heat stop, that is the lean from 1.1. Shoulders. Second sentence.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Where is work sitting right now that nobody can see?
  • Which person are you tempted to blame, and what machine are they standing in?
  • How many front doors does your real process have?
  • If you added energy upstream of your bottleneck, would you help — or grow a pile?
  • What loop keeps teaching the team the wrong lesson (hero Friday, extra approval, a meeting because a handoff is broken)?
Remember

Remember This

The system produces the result. Draw the machine before you draw a villain.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Deming, Goldratt, and the temptation of the org chart.

An org chart is a map of reporting. A process map is a map of work. They are not the same picture. Many companies manage the first and hope the second will behave. Work travels sideways, through inboxes, through the person who happens to know the password.

Deming's "94%" is best used as a brake on your moral machinery, not as a statistic for a board pack. He wanted leaders to study variation: some noise is the machine humming (common cause); some is a real exception (special cause). Treating common cause as a villain — a warning letter to Mara because the pile is the pile — makes the next month worse. Treating a true special cause as "the system" is how you keep a wrecked performer in the box. The map helps you tell which.

Goldratt's constraint is a focusing device: exploit the bottleneck (do not waste its time), subordinate everything else (do not flood it), then elevate it if you still need to. Knowledge-work bottlenecks are often "the one person who can approve" or "the one person who understands the customer." Heroes are bottlenecks. Checklists and maps are how you stop worshipping them.

Bezos's two-way doors (full treatment in 7.2) apply: collapsing three inboxes into one is usually reversible. Reorging the team because you will not draw a queue is a one-way door with a costume.

Harder variation: sit with someone who does the work and draw silently while they talk. Then show them the page. The corrections they make are the actual map. Your first drawing is a hypothesis.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 48 hours, map one real process that has recently failed or irritated you. Walk one case. Photograph the drawing.

Four lines: the output; the bottleneck; the oldest pile (age); one change that does not require a new tool.

Share the map with one person who lives in it. If they say "that's not how it works," you succeeded: you found the next drawing.
