Say the Point First

Clarity is kindness under time pressure

22 minBLUF4,014 words22 sections
Skill
Lead with the news
Tool
BLUF
Result
Your emails and updates get shorter and sharper
In one line
Lead with the news. Context can wait.
The idea

The Hook

You have ninety seconds with a person who can say yes.

You spend seventy of them on background. You are being thorough. You are being respectful. You are building up to the ask so it will not sound abrupt. They glance at a second screen. They nod at the wrong part. When you finally say the thing, they have already decided you do not know what the thing is.

This is not a personality flaw in them. It is a design feature of attention. People with full calendars listen for the headline the way drivers listen for a siren. If you hide the siren in the third paragraph, you will be experienced as fog.

Clarity is not bluntness. Bluntness is heat without a point. Clarity is the point, early, in a size the other person can use. The military named this BLUF: Bottom Line Up Front. You do not need a uniform to steal it.

There are rooms where BLUF is wrong. Grief. The slow courtship of an idea. Some cultures and some relationships that treat a headline as a shove. This lesson will say when not to use it. First you have to be able to use it. Most capable people are still burying the lead and calling it professionalism.

The room

The Scenario

Friday, 3:40 p.m. Elena's boss, a VP, has a hard stop at 3:50. Elena asked you to "give her a sense of whether you're ready for the lead role" — the promotion you have wanted for a year. You have eight minutes.

You start with the year. The projects. The person you backfilled. The reason the last lead left. You are trying to be complete. At 3:47 the VP says, "Sorry — I have to jump. Send me a note."

You send a note. Four paragraphs. The ask is in paragraph three. The evidence is scattered. The VP skims it on a phone in a car. On Monday Elena says, "She didn't get that you were asking. She thought it was a status update."

You have another chance on Tuesday: twelve minutes, same VP, after a staff meeting. She will be tired. She will be fair. She will not hunt for your point.

What do you say first?

Your move

Your Move

Do not keep reading yet. Write the first two sentences you would actually send or say.

Common move. Chronology. "I wanted to follow up on Friday and also give you more context on the last two quarters…" You are trying to earn the ask. You are hiding it.

Better move. A polite wind-up, then the ask. "Thanks for the time. I've been thinking about the lead role, and if it's useful I'd love to share why I might be a fit." Softer. Still a hallway, not a door.

Expert move. BLUF, then evidence, then the ask in a form she can answer. "I want to be considered for the team-lead role. I can take it in September. Two facts: I ran the migration when the last lead left, and the November date is currently on my name. What would you need to see in the next six weeks to make that a yes or a no?"

Resourceful move. Pause — because asking for a promotion makes the body lean — then BLUF matched to this room. A tired VP at 3:50 needs the news, the proof, and a question she can answer in two minutes. Not your autobiography. Not a joke to lower the stakes. The point first.

Why the common move fails: under time pressure, people do not award points for suspense. They award points for a usable headline. If they have to search for your ask, they will search badly.

What can go wrong with the expert move: BLUF can sound like a demand in a culture that expects a longer approach. It can sound cold in a relationship that runs on warmth. It can skip a fact she needs. The fix is not to bury the lead again. The fix is a better first sentence, then the right amount of proof.

The idea

The Big Idea

Lead with the news. Then the reason. Then the example. Then stop.

BLUF is the name. The cousins are 5W1H (who, what, when, where, why, how — Level 2) and Point-Reason-Example. All three are the same kindness: do not make a busy person mine your meaning.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Attention is expensive. You already know this from Level 1. Other people's attention is not a moral right you earn with a preamble. It is a budget they are already overspending.

BLUF comes from military and intelligence briefing culture: put the conclusion first so a reader who is interrupted still has the thing that matters. Barbara Minto's The Pyramid Principle (1978, and later editions) taught a related discipline to consultants: start with the answer, group the supporting arguments, do not make the reader wait for your thinking to finish in public. You do not need to join a firm to steal the shape.

A simple stack:

  1. Point. What is true, what you recommend, or what you need. One sentence.
  2. Reason. Why that point, in one or two lines.
  3. Example / evidence. The fact that would survive a skeptic.
  4. Ask. What you need from them, in a form they can answer.

5W1H is how you check that the point is actually a point and not a mood. Who is asking. What is the news. When does it matter. Where does it apply. Why now. How you propose to proceed. If you cannot fill those, you are not ready to BLUF. You are ready to think.

When not to use BLUF.

Grief. A person whose parent died does not need your headline efficiency in the first breath of condolence. They may need the news clearly if you are the one who has to tell them — medical and emergency settings often do lead with the fact, then the care. The distinction is not "never be clear when people are sad." It is: do not use briefing manners as a substitute for being human. "I have bad news. Sam died this morning. I'm here." is BLUF with a pulse. "Per my last update, mortality occurred at 0600" is a crime against the room.

Courtship of an idea. Some ideas cannot survive a headline because the listener does not yet have the frame. You are not hiding the point. You are building the question they can hear the point as an answer to. Teachers do this. Good founders do this with a skeptical board sometimes. The test: are you sequencing so they can receive it, or so you can avoid saying it?

Culture and context. In some high-context workplaces and families, a bald first sentence reads as a shove or a status grab. In some rooms the most senior person is expected to speak first. BLUF still helps you know your point. You may put a greeting, a face-saving clause, or a question in front of it. That is not burying. That is matching weather, which you learned to see in 4.1.

If you are not sure, the useful default in modern professional writing — email, chat, exec updates — is still: news first.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: BLUF

Use it for emails, stand-ups, exec time, status pages, and any ask that can be answered with yes, no, or a date.

  1. Write the last sentence first. The one you were going to hide. Put it at the top.
  2. Make it a sentence, not a topic. "Update on hiring" is a label. "I need approval to open one senior role by Friday or we miss November" is news.
  3. Add the reason in one breath. Why this, why now, why them.
  4. Add one piece of evidence. A number, a date, a name, a constraint. Not five.
  5. End with a closed-open ask. Closed enough to answer; open enough to be real. "Can you approve by Thursday?" or "What would you need to see to say yes?"
  6. Cut the autobiography. If a sentence exists to protect your feelings, it goes in a note to yourself, not in the message.

The pocket version:

News → Why → One Fact → Ask.

Point-Reason-Example is the spoken cousin:

  • Point: "I want the lead role in September."
  • Reason: "The work is already sitting on my name, and the team needs a single owner before the freeze."
  • Example: "I ran the migration after the last lead left; the vendor SLA is on my calendar."
  • Then the ask: "What would you need in six weeks to make it a yes or a no?"

Write a card:

If They Only Read One Sentence, Is It The News?

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

When you have ninety seconds, or a phone, or a VP standing up:

One sentence of news. One sentence of ask. Stop.

  • "I want to be considered for the lead role. What would you need to see in six weeks to decide?"
  • "The date moved by two weeks. I need a yes or no on overtime by 4:00."
  • "I can't take the extra scope. I can take X, or we slip Y. Which do you want?"

If your mouth wants to add a third sentence of history, that is the lean. Pause. The history can live in a follow-up note they did not ask for yet.

The hack

The Hack

Write the subject line as the BLUF.

If the subject is "Quick question" or "Following up," you have already lost the people who live in their inbox. "Need a yes/no on the lead role — 6-week evidence plan" is a subject that can survive a car.

In speech, the hack is to say "The headline is…" and then actually give a headline. It feels clumsy the first ten times. It feels like relief to the other person on the first time.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is excellent at turning a buried-lead draft into BLUF. It is also excellent at making you sound like a press release. You must keep your voice and your facts.

Use AI for:

  • "Find the news in this draft and put it in sentence one"
  • Three subject lines of decreasing length
  • A 90-second spoken version and a 150-word email version of the same point

Do not use AI for:

  • Inventing evidence you did not provide
  • A fake-warm opener that you cannot say out loud
  • Pasting other people's performance information into a model

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Context: I need to communicate under time pressure.
Audience: [role, how much time they have, what they care about]
Draft (buried lead is likely):
"""
[paste]
"""
Facts that must remain true:
[numbered]

Task:
1) Identify the actual news in one sentence.
2) Rewrite as BLUF: news, why, one fact, ask.
3) Give a 90-second spoken version.
4) Give a subject line that is the news.
5) Flag any sentence that is autobiography, not evidence.

Constraints: No corporate fog. No fake warmth. No new facts.
Output: Labeled versions, each short enough to use.
Verification: List anything I still need to check before I send.

What to verify: Dates, numbers, whether you are actually asking. Models will invent a crisp number if you were vague. 5W1H the output.

Do not paste: salary bands you were told in confidence, medical details, customer data, anyone's review.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "make the news impossible to miss."

Current examples of the category:

  • Subject line = headline; first line of the email = the same headline in slightly more English
  • A one-line pinned update at the top of a doc: "Decision needed by Thursday: A or B"
  • In chat, the first message is the point; the thread is the evidence, not the other way around
  • Delay-send if you wrote the angry version first — BLUF is not a reason to ship heat faster

When not to use technology: a condolence, a courtship of a fragile idea, a family conversation where a headline will land as a verdict. Tools that optimize for skimmability will push you to BLUF even when the room needs a slower animal. You have to choose.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

People confuse BLUF with being harsh.

They write: "You failed to deliver. Fix it." That is not bottom line up front. That is a slap with a period. BLUF can be kind: "I can't come this Sunday. I can do next Saturday 2–5. I want to see you; I don't want to pretend I can do both weekends." The news is first. The relationship is not discarded.

The other mistake is fake BLUF: a first sentence that sounds like news and is actually a topic. "I wanted to reach out about next steps on the role" is not a point. It is a throat-clearing.

Recovery: if you already sent the fog, do not send a second fog to explain it. One short follow-up: "The ask, clearly: I want to be considered for the lead role. I can talk Tuesday."

Defense

The Defense

BLUF fails when:

  • The listener needed a frame you did not build. Then you sequence, on purpose, and still know your point in your pocket.
  • The culture reads directness as a status attack. Then you keep the news in sentence two, after a face-saving line — not in sentence fourteen.
  • You BLUF a guess as a fact. "The vendor will fail" is not news if you mean "the SLA is tight." Accuracy first. Level 2 still applies.
  • You use speed to avoid the feeling of asking. A crisp promotion ask can still be a cowardly one if you never name the number, the date, or the fear. 4.7 will pick this up.

Limitation: this will not make you interesting. It will make you usable. Some rooms also need you to be interesting. Do not become a telegram as a personality.

Council

The Masters' Table

Aristotle. Through Rhetoric: logos is not a pile of clauses. It is a claim a mind can hold. If the claim arrives after the patience has run out, you did not make an argument. You made a noise. Application: put the claim where attention still exists.

Barbara Minto. Through the pyramid: answer first, then grouped reasons. Application: your promotion note is an answer ("consider me"), not a memoir.

The military briefing tradition (BLUF). Through interruption as the default. The reader may not finish. Application: the first sentence must be able to travel alone.

Dale Carnegie. Through the other person's interest — with the usual caution. BLUF that only serves you ("I want a title") is weaker than BLUF that names a problem they already have ("the team needs an owner before the freeze, and I can be that owner"). Do not twist this into flattery. Twist it into relevance.

Lincoln. Through public speech that often did build slowly — Gettysburg is not an email. Application: match form to room. A tired VP gets BLUF. A nation in mourning gets a slower instrument. Knowing the difference is 4.1.

Synthesis. The council agrees that attention is scarce and claims should be findable. They disagree about speed. That disagreement is the point of "when not to."

Where the council could be wrong. Western business brevity is not a human universal. Some of the best thinking is oral, relational, and circular on the way to the point. If you BLUF your way out of listening, you have used a writing trick as a character. Lesson 4.3 exists because of that failure.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to Tuesday, twelve minutes.

Calm. You want this. The lean is hope, not only anger. Pause anyway.

Observe. She is tired. She has a hard stop. She already missed the ask once. Elena is in the room or will hear about it.

Define. The job of twelve minutes is a clear ask plus a way she can decide, not a greatest-hits reel.

Verify. You know the migration fact. You do not know her private criteria. So you will ask for them.

Prioritize. News, proof, ask. If time remains, one question about her concerns.

Find resources / people. Elena as the person who must not be surprised. A six-week evidence plan you can actually run.

Options. Chronology. Soft wind-up. BLUF. Wait for her to bring it up (she will not).

Risks. BLUF sounds presumptuous. Fog sounds like you are not ready. Asking her to invent criteria on the spot may be a gift or a burden.

Choose. BLUF + one fact + a decision question.

Communicate. "I want to be considered for the lead role, starting September. The work is already on my name — I ran the migration after the last lead left, and the November date sits with me. I am not asking you to say yes today. I am asking what you would need to see in the next six weeks to make it a yes or a no. I can send that as four lines after this meeting."

Act. Send the four lines the same hour. Copy Elena. Do not add a fifth paragraph of hunger.

Document. The four lines. Her criteria, in her words.

Adapt. If she says the role is not open, you have news. Then you negotiate interests (4.7) or you stop courting a closed door.

Reflect. Did you bury anything? Did you oversell? Did you ask a question she could answer?

Practice

Practice

Do this now. Ten minutes.

  1. Open the last long email or chat you sent that mattered. Paste it into a note.
  2. Highlight the actual news. If you cannot find it, that is the lesson.
  3. Rewrite with the stack: news, why, one fact, ask. Half the words.
  4. Write a subject line that is the news.
  5. Write the 90-second spoken version out loud. If you cannot say it, you cannot send it.
  6. Pick one message you will have to send in the next 48 hours and BLUF it before you need it.

Then do the negative drill: write a version that is only chronology. Read both. Feel the difference in your own body. You are teaching yourself to prefer the thing that can survive a skim.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the promotion ask. Situation: you want a role; the person who can move it has twelve minutes and a full inbox. What most people do: a narrative of worthiness. Why it fails: worthiness is not a decision. A date, a scope, and a test are a decision. Resourceful response: BLUF the ask, one proof, a six-week test you both can see. Words to use: "I want X. The evidence is Y. What would you need to see by Z?" Words to avoid: "Sorry to bother you," "I don't want to overstep," a three-year autobiography. Tool: BLUF. Room Sheet first if you do not know her weather. AI assist: the prompt above, then strip anything you would not say out loud. What could go wrong: you BLUF a demand with no path for her to say "not yet." Include the path.

Family — saying the plan. Situation: you cannot host the holiday this year. You write a long message about how much family means to you. The "no" is in paragraph four. What most people do: cushion until the no is deniable. Why it fails: they hear maybe. You hear that you were kind. Then a fight about the maybe. Resourceful response: "We can't host this year. We can come from 2 to 6 on the Saturday, or we can do a meal at ours in January. I want to see you; I can't do the full day." Words to use: the no, the alternative, the care. Words to avoid: a fake maybe, a medical excuse you do not have, a speech about who always hosts. Tool: BLUF with a pulse. Not a telegram. What could go wrong: news-first in a family that needs a longer on-ramp. Then: care sentence, then news, still early.

Digital — the exec update. Situation: a Friday written update. You have five bullets of activity. What most people do: list activity. Why it fails: activity is not news. News is what changed, what is stuck, what you need. Resourceful response: line 1: the one thing that changed. Line 2: the ask, if any. Then bullets. Words to use: "Stuck on X until Y decides." "No ask this week." Words to avoid: "Lots of progress," "circling back," "per my last."

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Same VP. Worse.

She says, before you start: "I only have four minutes and I already think we should hire the lead from outside." Elena looks at the table.

BLUF still. Now the news includes her objection, accurately, without a collapse.

"You'd rather hire out. I still want to be considered. I can run a six-week internal trial against the same bar you'd use on an external — November date, vendor SLA, the team not bouncing. If I miss the bar, you hire out and I'll help. If I meet it, you have a cheaper, faster owner. What is the bar?"

You have not argued with her preference as a moral error. You have put a test on the table. You have given her a way to keep her outside option. That is BLUF plus interests, which 4.7 will name.

If four minutes is really four, stop after "What is the bar?" Let silence work. Do not fill it with your childhood.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Where do you bury the lead — email, family, the ask that would make you visible?
  • What are you protecting by winding up: their feelings, or yours?
  • When has BLUF landed as a shove, and what would a face-saving first clause have cost you?
  • Can you say your current professional ask in one sentence, out loud, without a joke?
  • Which of your regular updates is a list of activity pretending to be news?
Remember

Remember This

If they only read one sentence, it had better be the news.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

A short history of the buried lead.

Journalism taught "don't bury the lead" because readers leave. The military taught BLUF because commanders are interrupted. Consulting taught the pyramid because clients bill for confusion. These are different institutions with the same scar: people with power and little time will not finish your unfolding.

Minto is worth reading if you write for executives. She is also a style, and styles become dogma. Not every document wants a pyramid. A scientific paper has a reason to delay the full interpretation until methods are in view. A novel that BLUFs is not a novel. The skill is knowing which genre you are in.

On culture: what U.S. business culture calls "direct" is often just low-context. Edward T. Hall's distinction between high-context and low-context communication is a useful caution, not a sorting hat for nations. Individuals vary. Rooms vary. If you work across cultures, ask a trusted person in that room: "Does a first-sentence ask read as respect or as a shove here?" That is a Room Sheet question.

On grief and medicine: breaking bad news is its own literature (SPIKES is one protocol used in clinical teaching). Do not import it casually. The steal for this course is small: when the news is heavy, be clear and present. Do not make the person hunt for the fact. Do not make the fact the only thing in the room.

If you want a practice beyond this lesson, take five sent emails from this month and rewrite only sentence one. Track whether the rest of the email can shrink. It usually can.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24 hours, send one real message — email, chat, or spoken — that leads with the news.

Afterward, four lines:

  1. The news sentence
  2. Where it used to hide
  3. What you cut
  4. How they responded (including silence)

If you could not send it because the room was grief or courtship, write the BLUF in your pocket anyway. Knowing the point is still the skill. Shipping it is a separate choice.
