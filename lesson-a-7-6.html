Calibrate Your Confidence

Be less sure, more often, on purpose

24 minThe Confidence Range3,954 words22 sections
Skill
Put a number on how sure you are
Tool
The Confidence Range
Result
You can say "60%" without feeling weak
In one line
"I'm 60%" is often the most honest sentence in the room.
The idea

The Hook

Jonah is 90% sure.

He says it like a personality. The vendor will deliver by the 12th. He has a feeling about their CTO. He has been in rooms like this. Elena needs a date she can put in a slide. Mara needs to know whether to promise two accounts a hyphen fix that depends on the vendor's import tool.

"Ninety percent," Jonah says again, because nobody asked him to be 60.

Two weeks later the vendor is late. Jonah says, "They surprised me." He does not say that 90% was a social number — the number you use when 60% would have forced Elena to plan a hedge and the VP to hear uncertainty.

The miss is not that Jonah was wrong. People are wrong. The miss is that the room used a confident voice as if it were a calibrated instrument.

This lesson is how you become the instrument: a range, a percent you would defend after the fact, and a habit of updating when the world talks. Not a new religion. A way to stop confusing volume with odds.

The room

The Scenario

Friday, 10:20 a.m. Go-live planning. The vendor's import tool is the dependency.

Elena: "Can I tell the VP we're good for the 12th?"

Jonah: "Yeah. I'm really confident. Like, ninety."

Mara: "The last two deliveries from them slipped. One of them slipped after a 'we're good.'"

Jonah: "That was a different team on their side. I've been on the calls. It's fine."

Elena looks at you. You are not the vendor expert. You are the person who has to turn Jonah's ninety into a plan that can survive a slip.

"I need a number I can plan around," Elena says. "Not a vibe. Are we locking the 12th?"

What do you do with Jonah's 90%?

Your move

Your Move

Write the number you would put on the 12th, and whether you lock. Then look at the families.

Common move. Defer to the expert's confidence. Jonah is closer to the metal. Ninety becomes the plan. No hedge. Or: dismiss Jonah because he is blunt, and pick a fear-number that is just as fake.

Better move. Ask for a range. "Best, worst, most likely." Better. Still easy to game: worst case becomes a cartoon, most likely stays 90.

Expert move. Separate the claim from the confidence. Force a percent Jonah would accept being scored on. Add a base rate (how often have this kind of vendor hit this kind of date). Put a hedge on anything that is not high-and-calibrated. Do not lock a one-way customer promise to an uncalibrated 90.

Resourceful move. Use the Confidence Range: a low/high, a percent, a base rate, a trigger to update, a plan that is legal at 60. You let Jonah keep his expertise. You do not let him keep a social number. Tetlock's work is the spine: the people who are best at this are not the most famous, and they update.

Why the common move fails: expertise and calibration are different skills. A person can be good at vendors and bad at odds. Overconfidence in experts is one of the most replicated patterns in judgment research. It is not an insult. It is a design feature of being the person who is asked to be sure.

What can go wrong with the expert move: you humiliate Jonah in public and the next 60% never arrives. Calibration needs a room that does not punish an honest percent. You can be rigorous about the number and still decent to the person.

The idea

The Big Idea

Say how sure you are, in a number you are willing to have been wrong at.

"Maybe 60%" is not weakness. It is a plan that still has a hedge. 90% is a plan that has no right to be surprised. The resourceful human is not the most certain person in the room. They are the most scorable.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Philip Tetlock spent years watching experts predict politics and public events. In Expert Political Judgment (2005) he showed that many experts, especially the famous, hedgehog-style ones with one big idea, were not much better than crude comparisons — and sometimes worse. In Superforecasting (2015, with Dan Gardner), built on the Good Judgment Project, he and colleagues showed that ordinary people, trained to think in probabilities, use base rates, and update, could outperform a lot of professional prediction in tournament conditions.

You are not going to become a superforecaster this afternoon. You are going to steal three habits:

  1. Put a number on it. Not "likely." A percent, even a coarse one: 20 / 40 / 60 / 80. Coarse is fine. Fake precision (87.5%) is not.
  2. Start from a base rate. How often does this kind of thing happen? Then adjust for this case. Do not start from the story of this case. Kahneman and Tversky's work on base-rate neglect is the warning: the vivid story eats the frequency.
  3. Update when the world talks. A slipped call, a new hire on their side, a hyphen test that fails — the percent should move. A person who is still at 90 after two slips is not confident. They are stuck.

Brier score, as a concept, not as homework: Glenn Brier (1950) scored probabilistic forecasts by how far the percent was from what happened. If you said 90% and it didn't happen, you are penalized more than if you said 60%. If you said 60% and it happened, you were not "wrong"; you were a person who left room. You do not need to compute Brier scores in this course. You need to feel why 90% is an expensive number.

Overconfidence in experts: people with status are asked for certainty, paid for certainty, and remembered for the times they were loudly right. The misses get a story ("they surprised me"). Calibration is the opposite culture: you are paid for numbers that match the world over a set of calls, not for one legendary call.

Simon: you will not have the true odds. A range is how a bounded mind tells the truth. Von Neumann, lightly: if you cannot weight the branches, do not speak as if you did. Buffett: be very sure in a small circle; outside it, say you do not know. That is calibration with a long-term accent.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Confidence Range

Use it on any claim that is about to become a plan, a promise, or a self-story.

  1. Write the claim as a scorable event. Not "the vendor is solid." "The import tool is in our hands, usable, by the 12th." A claim you could mark true/false on a date.
  2. Write a base rate, even a crude one. "In the last two years, vendors like this hit this kind of date about half the time" — and say how you know, or that you are guessing. Guessing is allowed. Smuggling a guess as a 90 is not.
  3. Adjust for this case, in public. Reasons to go up, reasons to go down. If all your reasons go up, you are campaigning. Force one down.
  4. Give a percent and a range. "I am at 55–65% that we have it by the 12th. Point estimate 60%. By the 19th I am at 80%." The range is the tool. The point is for people who need one number.
  5. State what would move you 15 points. A passed hyphen test. A named engineer on their side. A slip. If nothing would move you, you are not forecasting. You are identifying.
  6. Match the plan to the percent, and to the door. 60% on a two-way flag: ship with a kill switch. 60% on a one-way customer promise: do not lock the 12th; lock a range, or lock a hedge. 90% is for when you would be genuinely shocked, and you can say why.
  7. Write the review date. When will this claim be scored? Put it on a calendar. This is how you get better. Without it you only get more personality.

Card:

SCORABLE CLAIM → BASE RATE → PERCENT + RANGE → WHAT WOULD MOVE ME → PLAN THAT FITS THE NUMBER.

Worked on Jonah's 90%:

  • Claim: usable import by the 12th.
  • Base rate: last two deliveries slipped. Crude base: not 90.
  • Adjust: Jonah has been on the calls (up); different team then (maybe up); Mara's hyphen depends on it (doesn't change odds, changes blast radius); "it's fine" after a challenge (down — stuck).
  • Your number, if you are honest and not the expert: maybe 50–70%, point 60%. You are allowed to be less sure than Jonah. You are not allowed to pretend his 90 is yours.
  • Move me: a hyphen test on our data by Wednesday; a named owner at the vendor who has shipped this before.
  • Plan: do not lock the VP to the 12th. Lock an internal target, a customer range, a hedge (manual import for two accounts), an abort.
Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

When someone says "I'm confident" and a plan is about to lock:

"Give me a percent you'd be willing to score in two weeks. And a base rate."

If they cannot, you do not treat the claim as 90. You treat it as uncalibrated, which means you hedge.

Holding sentences:

  • "I'm at about 60%. I would not promise a customer a 90."
  • "What would move you down 20 points?"
  • "That's a 90. 90 means we have no hedge. Is that the door we want?"
The hack

The Hack

Ban the word "likely" in your own mouth for a week. Replace it with a number.

You will hate this. You will find that many of your "likelys" were 55 dressed as 80. That finding is the whole hack.

Do not perform the ban on other people in every conversation. You will become a pedant. Use it on claims that spend money, dates, or trust.

AI move

The AI Move

AI can help you find a base rate and stress-test a percent. It will also happily invent a base rate with a straight face.

Use AI for:

  • Turning a vague claim into a scorable event
  • Listing reasons to move the percent down
  • Suggesting where a human would look for a real base rate

Do not use AI for:

  • A fake "industry average"
  • Being 90% for you
  • A personality report on Jonah

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Role: Calibration clerk, not oracle.
Claim I need to forecast:
"""
[scorable event and date]
"""
What I know (facts):
[numbered]
What the most confident person in the room says:
[their percent and reasons]

Tasks:
1) Restate the claim so it is true/false on a date.
2) Propose a crude base rate and LABEL it a guess. Tell me where to verify.
3) Reasons to go UP and DOWN, equal number of each.
4) A percent and a range that fit MY facts, not the confident person's social number.
5) Three events that should move the percent by at least 15 points.
6) A plan that is sane at 60% vs at 90%.

Constraints: No invented statistics. No "trust your gut." Coarse percents only (tens).

What to verify: Every base rate. Search, ask a person who has the last ten cases, look at your own log. Level 6's Claim / Source / Date.

Do not paste: vendor contracts, customer names, anything that would identify a colleague as "the overconfident one" in a system you do not control.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "keep a scorable trail."

Category examples:

  • A note or sheet with date, claim, percent, outcome, one-line postmortem
  • Calendar reminders on the score date
  • For groups: a written percent in the meeting notes, not a vibe in the air
  • Simple spreadsheets, not forecasting platforms you will not open

When not to use technology: a dashboard of forecasts nobody scores is theater. Ten scored claims in a notebook beat a tool.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Using 90% as a social lubricant.

You want Elena to stop asking. You want to look like Jonah. You want the VP to have a slide. So you round up. Rounding up on a one-way door is how surprises get billed as fate.

The cousin mistake: using 50% for everything so you can never be wrong. That is not calibration. That is refusal. If you would take 4-to-1 that the sun rises, do not say 50.

Recovery: ask, "Would I take a bet at these odds with money I can afford to lose?" If you would not put $20 on Jonah's 90, it was not a 90.

Defense

The Defense

The Confidence Range fails when:

  • The event is not scorable ("the vendor is a good partner"). Rewrite or do not percent it.
  • The room punishes 60%. Then people will lie. If you lead, reward the update, not the swagger. If you do not lead, give your 60% with a hedge attached so it is useful even if they mock the number.
  • You treat Tetlock as a command to forecast your marriage. Some domains are not tournaments. Use ranges for empirical claims. Use values for wants (Decision 7, question 1).
  • Fake precision. 63.2% is a costume. Tens are enough.
  • You use "I'm only 60%" to avoid a two-way door you should walk through. 60% with a cheap undo is often a yes. 60% with ruin is a no. Door type still rules.

Limitation: a few scored forecasts will not make you Tetlock's fox. They will make you slightly harder to fool, including by yourself. That is enough for this course.

Council

The Masters' Table

Philip Tetlock. Score your certainty. Update. Foxes with many models beat hedgehogs with one. Superforecasting is a research program and a book, not a personality you must buy.

Daniel Kahneman. Overconfidence and base-rate neglect. The story of this vendor will eat the last ten vendors unless you write the ten down.

Herbert Simon. Bounded minds use coarse numbers. Coarse and honest beats fine and fake.

Warren Buffett. Circle of competence: high confidence only where you actually have a base rate. Outside it, "I don't know" is a calibrated sentence.

Annie Duke. Thinking in bets: a percent is a bet you'd take. If you wouldn't take it, it wasn't your number.

John von Neumann (lightly). Weighted branches need weights. A voice is not a weight.

Synthesis. The room does not need more certainty. It needs numbers that can lose.

Where the council could be wrong. Tetlock's tournaments are not your job. Some experts are calibrated in a narrow craft (a good anesthesiologist's sense of "this airway is bad" is not a hedgehog on cable news). Do not use this lesson to sneer at every person who knows a tool. Use it when their certainty is about to become your plan.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 10:20 a.m.

Calm. Pause. You are not here to win a status fight with Jonah.

Observe. Claim: the 12th. Jonah's 90. Two prior slips. Mara's accounts. Elena's slide. One-way if a customer hears "we're good."

Define. Get a scorable number, a hedge that is legal at 60, and a sentence Elena can take upstairs that is not a lie.

Verify. The two slips — dates, causes. Whether a hyphen test on our data is possible this week. Who at the vendor is actually on it.

Prioritize. Customer-facing lock last. Internal target first. Jonah's face in the middle: do not break the person who has the calls.

Find resources / people. Last ten vendor dates if they exist. The person who lived the last slip.

Options. Lock 12th at 90. Refuse to lock. Lock a range. Lock 12th with a named hedge and an abort.

Risks. Humiliating Jonah. A VP slide that becomes a one-way door. A 60% that is used as a no when a two-way hedge would have been enough.

Choose. Do not lock the customer to the 12th. Set Jonah a scorable test. Plan at 60.

Communicate. "Jonah, I want your expertise, and I want a number we can score. Given two slips, I cannot take 90 to a customer. I'm at about 60% for the 12th unless we get a hyphen test on our data by Wednesday. Elena, tell the VP we are targeting the 12th internally, customers get a range, and we have a manual path for two accounts if the tool is late. Jonah, what would move you to 70, and what would move you to 50?"

Act. Book the test. Write the percents in the notes. Calendar the 12th as a score date.

Document. Claim, percent, reasons, trigger.

Adapt. Wednesday test fails → drop the percent, run the hedge. Test passes → move up, maybe still not to 90.

Reflect. Did you need Jonah to be wrong? If yes, your 60% is also social. Check it.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the expert's 90% is about to become a date. Situation: a specialist is sure; a customer or VP will hear it. What most people do: copy the 90, or fight the expert's status. Why it fails: you either inherit uncalibrated risk or you make the expert hide the next 60. Resourceful response: scorable claim, base rate, your percent, a hedge, a test that would move the number. Words to use: "A percent we can score." "What would move you down 20?" Words to avoid: "So you're not actually sure" (status attack). Tool: Confidence Range + Door Type. AI assist: down-reasons and a scorable rewrite. What could go wrong: you delay a two-way test because you are busy forecasting. If a test is cheap, run the test.

Money — "this can't miss." Situation: a purchase, a fund, a side bet, a friend with a tip. What most people do: catch the certainty; skip the base rate. Why it fails: tips arrive with stories, not frequencies. Resourceful response: write a scorable claim ("up or not in 12 months" is still crude); ask for a base rate; size the bet so a miss is a bruise. Words to use: "How often has this kind of thing worked?" "I would only bet a bruise." Words to avoid: "I'm 100% on this." Tool: Confidence Range + ruin check (7.3). AI assist: where to look for a base rate; verify everything. What could go wrong: you use 60% as a reason to nibble at a ruinous tail. 60% of ruin is still no.

Family — the plan that needs a 90 to be kind. Situation: a partner wants certainty about a move, a school, a job, a parent’s care. What most people do: perform 90 to soothe, then get blamed when the 40% shows up. Why it fails: you sold a number you did not have. Resourceful response: "I'm at 60% this works the way we hope. Here is the hedge if it doesn't. I will not fake 90 to end this conversation." Words to use: the percent and the hedge in the same breath. Words to avoid: "Trust me" as a substitute for a range. Tool: Confidence Range + Decision 7 (want vs option). AI assist: none in the fight. After, maybe a scorable rewrite of the claim. What could go wrong: using calibration to sound cold. Warmth and a 60% can live in the same sentence.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Elena: "The VP is on in four minutes. Jonah said ninety. I need you on side." Jonah: "Don't you dare put a 60 in that call." Mara's accounts are waiting. You can feel the loyalty test.

Pause. Then a sentence that is true at speed:

"I will not say ninety to a customer. I can say we are targeting the 12th, we have a hyphen test Wednesday, and we have a backup for two accounts. That is a 60% plan with a hedge, which is the adult version of Jonah's expertise. If the VP needs ninety, he is asking for a feeling. I will not be the person who sold it."

If they put you on the call anyway, you do not sandbag Jonah in public. You also do not launder 90. BLUF: "Internal target the 12th. Confidence is moderate, not high — we've had slips. Hedge in place. I will update Wednesday." Then you get off the claim.

If your job is threatened by a 60%, that is information about the room (7.8). It is not information about the odds.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Where do you round up because the room likes 90?
  • What is a domain where you are actually calibrated — and one where you only have stories?
  • When did an expert's certainty become your plan, and what did it cost?
  • What would a 15-point update look like on the thing you are currently sure about?
  • If we scored your last ten "I'm sure"s, would you like the Brier score, even as a metaphor?
Remember

Remember This

"I'm 60%" is not a flinch. It is a number a plan can survive.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Tetlock's fox/hedgehog split, borrowed from Isaiah Berlin, is a useful cartoon: hedgehogs know one big thing and force the world into it; foxes know many small things and stitch. Cartoons break. Some hedgehogs are right when the world matches their one thing. The operational steal is not "become a fox as a personality." It is: do not marry a model, start from frequencies, update.

The Good Judgment Project (with IARPA) is the empirical backbone of Superforecasting. Training, tracking, and teaming helped. You will not reproduce a tournament in your office. You can reproduce the smallest pieces: a scorable claim, a written percent, a score date, an update.

On Brier: it is mean squared error for probabilities. Say 70% (0.7) and it happens (1): you are penalized (0.3)². Say 90% and it does not: (0.9)², much worse. This is why "I was almost sure" is an expensive miss, and "I was 60%" is a survivable one. You do not need to compute it. You need to stop handing out 90s like mints.

On expert overconfidence: Paul Meehl's work on clinical vs actuarial judgment is the older, harder well — simple statistical rules often beat informal expert judgment in repeated, noisy domains. That finding has been chewed on for decades. The modest version for this course: if you have a base rate and a checklist, do not throw them out because Jonah has a feeling. Also do not throw out Jonah. He has the calls. Fuse them: his facts, your percent, a test.

A mastery drill: twenty forecasts, two weeks, coarse percents, scored. Work and life events that will actually resolve. No geopolitics you cannot score. At the end, look at the 80s that missed and the 50s that you treated like nos. That look is the education.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24 hours, put a Confidence Range on one live claim that is about to become a plan.

Write: scorable event, crude base rate, percent and range, what would move you, the plan that fits 60%.

Say the percent to one other person without apologizing. Put a score date on the calendar.

Sources to Explore

  • Philip E. Tetlock and Dan Gardner, Superforecasting, 2015 — updating, base rates, and the habits of people who are good at this in tournament conditions.
