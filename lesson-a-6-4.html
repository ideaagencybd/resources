Catch the Confident Lie

Fluency is not accuracy

26 minClaim, Source, Date, Math, Human3,345 words22 sections
Skill
Verify AI output like an adult
Tool
Claim, Source, Date, Math, Human
Result
You stop pasting plausible nonsense into the world
In one line
If it cannot survive five checks, it does not leave the chat.
The idea

The Hook

The dangerous sentence is not the one that sounds crazy.

The dangerous sentence is the one that sounds like it has already been checked. A statute number. A study from 2019. A precise percentage. A quote with an em dash and a famous name. Your eyes do the thing they do in a good bookstore: they relax.

Language models are very good at that bookstore feeling. They are trained to continue text that looks like knowledge. Looking like knowledge is a style. It can be attached to a fact, a guess, a mash-up, or a clean invention.

People call the inventions "hallucinations," which is a slightly too medical word for "fluent nonsense." The model is not seeing visions. It is producing the shape of a source without a source, the shape of math without the math, the shape of a date that would be convenient if it were true.

If you cannot catch that, you are not using AI. You are laundering it.

The room

The Scenario

Friday, 2:14 p.m. Elena needs a one-pager on whether a customer in another state can be recorded on a sales call. Jonah drops a model output into the shared doc:

"Under the Federal Two-Party Consent Act (2021), recording is lawful if one party consents, except in California, which follows Smith v. Audio (2018). I can draft a consent script."

It has a law. It has a case. It has an exception. It has a next step. It is the kind of paragraph that makes a meeting move.

You are pretty sure there is no "Federal Two-Party Consent Act (2021)." You are not a lawyer.

Elena says, "Good. Put it on letterhead and send it to the customer as our position."

Ten minutes. A document that will be treated as advice.

What do you do?

Your move

Your Move

Name, out loud, the first thing you would check.

Common move. Send it. It reads finished. You add "this is not legal advice" in 9-point gray. The customer will not read the gray. Your letterhead will.

Better move. Ask the model, "Are you sure?" It will say yes, with more statutes. Confidence is not a source.

Expert move. Take the paragraph apart: each claim, each source, each date, each bit of math, then a human who is actually allowed to say. Anything that fails is deleted, not decorated.

Resourceful move. You stop the letterhead. You tell Elena, without theater, that this is a fluent draft with unverified law. You replace it with a holding position: we do not give legal positions from a chat window; here is how we will get a real one. You still use the model — to list questions for counsel, not answers for a customer.

Why the common move fails: disclaimers do not un-send a fake statute. High-stakes domains — law, medicine, finance, safety — have professionals and official sources because fluent guessing kills people, money, or both.

What can go wrong with the expert move: you can vanish into a research hole and miss Elena's clock. Verification is a filter, not a PhD. If you cannot verify in time, you do not ship the claim. You ship "we don't know yet."

The idea

The Big Idea

Fluency is not accuracy.

The tool is Claim, Source, Date, Math, Human. Five checks. If a sentence cannot survive them, it does not get to go to a customer, a boss, a patient, a bank, or a group chat that will treat it as true.

You will use Facts vs Stories (2.1) and the Source Card (3.3) again here. This lesson is those tools pointed at a machine that is unusually good at sounding sourced.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Three failure modes, often stacked:

Hallucination. The model invents a plausible object: a paper, a quote, a product feature, a legal name. It may even invent a URL that looks right. Never fake a citation the model invented. If you cannot open it, it does not exist for your purposes.

Outdated training. The model is sure about a world that has moved. A 2023 tax rule, a 2024 product limit, a "current" price. Date is a check because the calendar is not a vibe.

Fluent nonsense. The grammar is perfect and the logic is hollow. A percentage with no denominator. A causal story that is actually a correlation. 6.6 is the full version; here, just do not let a pretty sentence skip math.

High-stakes rule, non-negotiable:

Law, medicine, finance, safety — you do not ship a model's answer as an answer. You use professionals and official sources. The model may help you ask a better question. It may help you find the name of the agency. It does not get to be the agency.

What's changing. Some 2026 models can search, fetch pages, and run code. That catches some lies and manufactures others: a real page, confidently misread; a real paper, attributed to the wrong year; a calculator used on the wrong inputs. Tool use is not a halo. You still run the five checks on anything you will use.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: Claim, Source, Date, Math, Human

Take the output. Split it into claims — one idea per line. Then:

  1. Claim. What, exactly, is being asserted? Write it in your own words, shorter. If you cannot, it was fog.
  2. Source. Where would this live if it were true? A statute, a paper, a company doc, a dataset, a person. Open it. If the model offered a citation, that citation is a lead, not a source. If you cannot find the object, the claim is unsupported. Delete or downgrade to "unverified."
  3. Date. When was the source current? When did the model say it was current? Is this the kind of fact that dies in a year (prices, features, guidance, news)? If the date is missing, you do not have a source. You have a vibe.
  4. Math. Any number, rate, "times more," "up 400%," sample size, or conversion. Recalculate, even roughly. Check the denominator. If there is no math but there is a quantity, that is a red flag, not a pass.
  5. Human. Who is allowed to own this if it is wrong? If the answer is "a lawyer, a clinician, a CPA, a structural engineer, a person whose child this is," you are done using the model as an authority. Escalate. If the answer is "me, and the cost of being wrong is a mildly embarrassing email," you can own it after 1–4.

Write CSDMH in the margin. It is ugly. It is fast.

Never: "the model said," as a source. Never: a citation you did not open. Never: a medical or legal position on your letterhead because the paragraph was tidy.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

One paragraph, ninety seconds:

Highlight every name, number, date, and "according to." That is your claim list. For each highlight, either (a) you can point to a source you have seen, or (b) it gets brackets: [UNVERIFIED]. If the paragraph is mostly brackets, you do not send a paragraph. You send questions.

Holding sentence, if Elena is waiting: "This reads finished and is not checked. I will not put it on letterhead. I can send a list of what we still need from counsel in twenty minutes."

The hack

The Hack

Search the most specific string.

Invented cases and papers often have distinctive titles. Put the title in quotes in a search engine. If the only hits are other chats, forum posts, or the same model, you have a ghost. Do not "almost" match it to a real case with a similar name. Similar is how fluent nonsense breeds.

Second hack: ask for a quote plus a locator — page, clause, URL — then open that locator and see whether the quote is there. Models will sometimes give you a real URL and a fake quote. The page is the boss.

AI move

The AI Move

Use a model to help you verify, with its hands kept off the gavel.

Use AI for:

  • Splitting a draft into claims
  • Suggesting where a real source would live (agency, handbook, statute name to look up)
  • Playing hostile reviewer: "Attack this paragraph as if you will be deposed"

Do not use AI for:

  • Confirming its own citations
  • Being the second source ("I asked another model")
  • Writing a fake bibliography you will format nicely

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Context: I need to verify a draft, not decorate it.
Role: hostile reviewer.
Draft:
"""
[paste the output]
"""
My stakes: [who will see this / what happens if a claim is wrong]

Goal: Split the draft into a table with columns:
Claim | Type (fact / definition / recommendation / invented-sounding) | Source the draft implies | Date sensitivity (high/low) | Math to check | How a human should verify | Risk if wrong

Constraints: Do not add new citations. If a citation is in the draft, copy it exactly and mark CITED-NOT-CHECKED. If a claim has no source, mark UNSOURCED. Do not reassure me.

Output: the table, then the three most dangerous claims, then a rewritten version with every unchecked claim removed (holes left visible).

Verification: List what I should open with my own eyes, in order.

What to verify: The table is a map. Open the things. Especially anything legal, medical, financial, or safety-related.

Do not paste: the client's confidential facts, medical details, children's data, credentials.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "catch fluent error before it becomes someone else's reality."

Category: verification aids, not oracles. Current examples (will change): search engines; the model's own browse/tool mode, used as a lead generator; official databases (Congress.gov, agency sites, court sites, PubMed, your country's equivalents); your spreadsheet for the math; a human.

When not to use another model as a check: when both were trained on similar slop, they can agree on a ghost. Agreement is not a source. Independent documents are.

What's changing. Citation features will get better and still fail. Some products will show a footnote that points to a page that does not contain the claim. The footnote is a UI. The page is the evidence.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

People only check the parts that "look suspicious."

The invented statute is sometimes obvious. The dangerous one is the real statute with the wrong year, or the real paper with the opposite finding, or the true percentage from a different population. Your suspicion is not a detector. The five checks are.

The other mistake: polishing a lie. You take the fake case, fix the formatting, add a DOI-looking string, and now it is a better fake. If you cannot open it, you do not format it. You delete it.

Recovery: if you already sent an unverified claim, the resourceful follow-up is short. "I sent a position that I had not verified. I am retracting claim X until I have a source. Here is what I know is true." Do it faster than your shame would like.

Defense

The Defense

The five checks fail when:

  • You are outside your competence and do not know what a source would even look like. Then Human is the whole tool. Find the person. Do not play lawyer.
  • Time is too short to check, and the room wants a number. You give no number, or you give a range labeled unverified. Silence is better than a ghost statute.
  • The claim is about the future ("this will work"). There is no source for a prophecy. Label it a forecast and own the uncertainty (Level 7).
  • You wanted the lie. The paragraph that saves the deal, flatters the boss, or confirms the family theory. Feynman is waiting in the Masters' Table. This is the expensive case.

Limitation: you will not catch everything. You will catch the load-bearing parts more often, which is the job.

Council

The Masters' Table

Richard Feynman. The first principle is that you must not fool yourself. A model that produces the shape of science is cargo-cult output until the experiment — here, the source — is real.

Claude Shannon. A message can be perfectly formed and still carry no information about the world. Fluency is a property of the signal. Truth is a property of the match to something else.

Daniel Kahneman. Through the fast-system lens: a complete-looking paragraph is a cognitive ease trick. Ease is not evidence. Slow down on the highlights.

Harry Frankfurt. Through the lens of On Bullshit (2005): the bullshitter does not care whether the line is true, only whether it works. A model is structurally close to that. You are not allowed to be.

Grace Hopper. Debug. The bug is often in the part that "couldn't be wrong." Check that part first.

Synthesis. The council has no patience for pretty ghosts. If it matters, open it. If you cannot open it, it does not ship.

Where the council could be wrong. Obsessive verification can become a way to never decide. Not every line of a birthday poem needs a source. Calibrate to stakes. The error is using birthday-poem standards on a consent law.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 2:14 p.m.

Calm. You do not laugh at Jonah in front of Elena. You also do not format the paragraph.

Observe. Load-bearing claims: a federal act, a year, a case name, a California rule, a permission to draft a script. Stakes: customer-facing legal position.

Define. The job is to prevent fake law from becoming "our position."

Verify. You cannot verify law in ten minutes to a professional standard. So you will not.

Prioritize. Human (counsel) over speed. Retract over polish.

Find resources / people. Official sources for recording-consent are state statutes and counsel, not a chat. A search can find the category (all-party vs one-party consent states). It cannot be the memo.

Options. Send with a disclaimer. Quietly delete the case name. Or stop the letterhead and change the job to "questions for counsel."

Risks. Slowing Elena. Being wrong about the ghost and looking pedantic. Being right about the ghost after the customer has it.

Choose. Stop. Downgrade. Escalate.

Communicate. "I can't stand behind this as written. The federal act and the case name look invented, and even if the conclusion were accidentally right, we should not send accidentally-right law. I'll send counsel three questions and a holding line for the customer: we will not record without a process legal has approved."

Act. Bracket every claim. Search the distinctive strings. Write the questions. Do not draft a "consent script" as if you were licensed.

Document. What you checked, what you could not, who owns the next step.

Adapt. If counsel later says the conclusion was roughly right, you still do not un-learn the lesson. Process was the failure, not the coin-flip.

Reflect. Which check did you want to skip because the paragraph was useful?

Practice

Practice

Do this now. Fifteen minutes.

  1. Take any recent model output you have — or generate a short "explain the law/science of X" paragraph on a topic you half-know.
  2. Split it into claims.
  3. Run CSDMH. Make a five-column table.
  4. Search one citation or proper noun in quotes.
  5. Recalculate one number, or write "no math present" if the draft used quantities without math.
  6. Write the Human: who would be allowed to own this.
  7. Rewrite the paragraph with holes left visible. That rewrite is the honest document.
Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the legal-ish paragraph. Situation: recording, HR, contracts, "can we say this in the EU," a model that will answer. What most people do: paste the answer, add "not legal advice." Why it fails: the recipient hears the answer, not the footnote. Resourceful response: CSDMH, then a human who is actually counsel. Words to use: "Unverified. I will not put this on letterhead." Words to avoid: "According to AI," fake case names, "the Federal Whatever Act." Tool: CSDMH + Source Card. What could go wrong: counsel is slow and Elena is not. Holding position, not ghost law.

Family — the health claim. Situation: a supplement, a symptom, a confident chat, a scared relative. What most people do: argue studies you also have not opened. Why it fails: you are now two unlicensed people. Resourceful response: "This is not a source. Let's take these questions to a clinician. Official guidance is [agency], not a chat." Words to use: questions, appointment, official site. Words to avoid: dosing advice, "I found a study" you did not read. What could go wrong: delay of real care. Do not let the chat be a waiting room.

Money — the invented rate. Situation: a model says a tax rate, a return, a "typical" salary. What most people do: put it in a spreadsheet. Why it fails: one wrong rate poisons every downstream cell. 6.5 will not save a fake input. Resourceful response: Math check, then the official table or a CPA. What could go wrong: a precise number feels more true than a range. Prefer an ugly official range.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

The one-pager already went to the customer on letterhead. They forwarded it to their counsel. Their counsel replies: there is no such act, and they would like to know whether your company fabricates law.

Elena is pale. Jonah says, "The tool must have had a glitch."

You do not defend the tool. You do not defend the letterhead. You contain.

"We retract that paragraph. It should not have been sent. We will send a corrected position from counsel, and until then we have no legal position on recording. I will also write down how this left the building so it does not happen on a bigger topic."

Then you do the unglamorous work: retract in writing, log the failure (skipped Human), and put a checklist on customer-facing claims. Checklists Beat Heroics was 5.3. This is why.

Do not offer a new fake statute as an apology gift.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Which check do you skip when the writing is good?
  • Have you ever formatted a citation you did not open?
  • Where are you the Human — and where are you pretending to be?
  • What claim did you believe this month because it was specific?
  • If Elena asked "where does this live?", could you point?
Remember

Remember This

If you did not open it, it is not a source. If you cannot own it, it is not yours to send.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

"Hallucination" is a metaphor that hides a design fact: next-token prediction does not have a built-in "I was not there" flag. Some systems add confidence scores, retrieval, or citations. Those are bolts. Bolts loosen.

A useful historical rhyme is the footnote that cannot be found. Scholars have always padded, mis-cited, and copied from each other. The new thing is speed and volume, plus a machine that will generate a plausible footnote on demand, without the social fear that used to constrain a human forger. Frankfurt's point about bullshit — indifference to truth — is closer than the medical metaphor.

Feynman's "Cargo Cult Science" (1974) is required reading for this lesson even if you never run an experiment. The cargo-cult scientist performs the gestures of science. The model performs the gestures of scholarship. Your job is to require the cargo: the actual plane, the actual paper.

On high-stakes domains, this course will not give you a workaround. There isn't one. Medicine, law, finance, and safety-critical engineering have licenses and official channels because the cost of fluent error is not "a bad blog post." Use the model to prepare questions. Use the professional and the official text to answer.

If you want a harder drill: take a model-written bibliography on a topic you know and try to obtain every item. Count ghosts. That number is your personal skepticism calibration.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24–48 hours, take one piece of AI-assisted output that was about to leave your world — or already did — and run Claim, Source, Date, Math, Human.

Send yourself a note with one line per check. If a citation fails, delete it at the source, not just in your memory.

If you work in a high-stakes domain, add one line to your personal checklist: no model output on letterhead without a named human owner.
