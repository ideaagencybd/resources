Case 6 — The Client Who Wants You to Bend a Rule

523 words

The Situation

A major client asks you to interpret a contract clause more liberally than your legal team has approved. Doing what they want would probably fly under the radar for years. The client is directly hinting that future renewal depends on your flexibility here.

Your Move — Decide Before Reading On

Option A

Do what they want. Everyone does it. It's not really breaking anything.

What likely happens:

The interpretation becomes standing practice. In two years, it comes up in an audit. The company faces a large fine. Multiple people including you are asked to account for how the practice developed. Careers are damaged. In some regulatory contexts, personal liability applies.

What you gained:

Client happiness. Short-term revenue.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Regulatory exposure. Personal liability risk. Career damage when the practice is eventually discovered.

What you should have noticed:

'Everyone does it' is not a legal defense. Regulatory expectations are that individuals refuse pressure to bend rules.

Option B

Refuse. 'I hear the pressure, but I can't interpret it that way — my legal team has been explicit. Let me see if there's a legitimate path to the outcome you're looking for.'

What likely happens:

The client is disappointed. They may or may not renew. If they don't, you have lost a specific customer. If they do renew (which happens often — sophisticated clients respect clean partners), the relationship is now more durable. Either way, your reputation internally and externally is intact.

What you gained:

Integrity. Legal safety. Long-term credibility.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Possible short-term revenue. Possible friction with the specific client's operating team.

What you should have noticed:

Clients who require unethical flexibility often become unmanageable clients over time. The 'loss' may be more of a filter.

Option C

Escalate. Take the request to your legal team, your account executive, and possibly your compliance officer. Let institutional process handle it.

What likely happens:

The institution now owns the response. If the client's request is genuinely impossible, the answer comes back from senior authority — which is easier for the client to accept than from you personally. If there's a legitimate creative solution, it emerges from a wider team. Your reputation as a serious professional grows.

What you gained:

Institutional protection. Wider input. Better final answer. Preserved relationship if possible.

What you missed / what could go wrong:

Some time. Some awkwardness with the specific client contact who was hoping you'd handle it quietly.

What you should have noticed:

'Escalate the awkward request' is one of the highest-leverage moves in a career. Most companies pay for people who do it correctly.

The Wrap

B and C combined. Refuse in the moment (B) so the client hears your position clearly. Then escalate (C) so your institution can decide what to do next. Never do A. Regulatory environments increasingly hold individuals personally responsible for rule-bending they permit, and the 'my client asked me to' defense has essentially no purchase. This is a case where the short-term cost of doing the right thing is real, and the long-term cost of not doing it is career-defining.
