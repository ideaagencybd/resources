Digital, Scams & Reputation

Someone threatens to send intimate images of you to your contacts

critical The No-Payment Ruleblackmailextortionprivacycritical
1

What is happening

Images or video exist — sent willingly, recorded without consent, or in some cases faked. A stranger or an ex now has them, has named your family and colleagues, and has given you hours to pay.

2

What is actually going on

  • This is a crime being committed against you and you are the victim, however the material came to exist. That framing matters because shame is the mechanism the offender is relying on.
  • Paying almost never ends it. It confirms you will pay and the demands return, usually larger. This is the consistent finding of every agency that handles these cases.
  • The deadline is manufactured. Panic prevents you doing the two things that work: stopping contact and getting help.
  • Most of these are organised, high-volume operations working from a script. It is not personal, even though it could not feel more personal.
3

Do this, in this order

  1. Do not pay. Do not send more material.Not one payment, not one further image, no matter what is threatened. This is the single most important decision in the whole event.
  2. Stop replying, but do not delete anythingScreenshot the profile, the usernames, the messages, the payment details, the URLs. Then stop engaging entirely.
  3. Lock your accounts down before anything is sentSet profiles to private, restrict who can see your friends and followers, and tighten who can message you. This reduces their reach immediately.
  4. Report to the platform as intimate-image abuseEvery major platform has a dedicated, fast route for this and it is far faster than a general report.
  5. Report to the police and get a reference numberThis is extortion. Many countries also have specialist units and hotlines for exactly this crime.
  6. Tell one person you trust, tonightIsolation is what makes this dangerous. Saying it out loud to one human being reduces the threat's power more than any technical step.
4

Words you can borrow

To the offender, if anything at all
Nothing. Silence is the correct response. Do not negotiate, threaten, or explain.
To someone you trust
"Something bad is happening and I need you to know so I am not dealing with it alone. I am not paying and I have reported it."
Pre-emptively, to close family if you choose
"Someone may send you something about me. It is blackmail, the police have it, and I am fine."
To yourself
"I did not cause this. The person threatening me committed the crime."
5

What makes it worse

  • Paying "just this once to make it stop."
  • Sending more material to buy time or prove compliance.
  • Deleting your accounts entirely before capturing evidence and reporting.
  • Trying to identify or confront them yourself.
  • Making an irreversible decision about your life while inside the panic window. This feeling does pass and support exists.
6

Once the pressure drops

  • Use the established takedown routes — StopNCII.org for adults and equivalents for minors — which can pre-block matching images across major platforms.
  • Set a search alert on your own name so you are informed rather than ambushed.
  • Talk to a specialist support service. This event has a real psychological tail and it responds well to help.
  • Audit what is stored where: cloud backups, old devices, shared albums.
Where this stops being yours to handle

If the target is under 18, this is child sexual exploitation — contact the police and a child-protection hotline immediately; do not attempt to handle it privately. If you are having thoughts of ending your life, call a crisis line now. People survive this and go on to be entirely fine, and the offender is counting on you not believing that.

A note on this library

These are general principles written to be useful under pressure, not legal, medical, or financial advice. Laws and emergency procedures differ by country. If life is at risk, contact your emergency services first and read this afterwards.
