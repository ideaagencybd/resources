Deep Dive 1: Systems Thinking

784 words

Seeing the whole rather than the parts.

Everyone talks about 'systems thinking.' Few practice it well. The gap between the concept and the discipline is where most of the value lives.

What a system actually is

A system is a set of interconnected parts whose behavior emerges from the interactions, not from the parts individually. A team, a business, a family, a supply chain, an immune system — all systems. The key properties of every system:

  • Structure produces behavior. Change the connections and you change the outputs, even if the parts stay the same.
  • Feedback loops amplify or dampen change. Notice them or be surprised by them.
  • Delays make cause and effect hard to see. The action you took six months ago is producing today's result.
  • Systems have bottlenecks. Optimizing anywhere except the bottleneck usually makes things worse.
  • Systems have inertia. They resist change in ways that are not immediately visible.

The seven questions of the systems thinker

Note

THE TOOL — Every consequential system-affecting decision

Note

1. What is the system I'm actually acting on? 2. What are the feedback loops in this system — which reinforce and which balance? 3. Where is the bottleneck — the constraint that actually limits throughput? 4. What delays exist between my action and the visible outcome? 5. What are the second-order effects — what will happen as a consequence of the first-order change? 6. What are the unintended consequences a well-designed person would predict? 7. What signal will tell me if my intervention is working or is producing an unexpected outcome?

Feedback loops in practice

Reinforcing loops amplify: a growing business attracts more customers, which grows revenue, which enables more investment, which grows the business further. Also: a declining relationship generates less connection, which produces less warmth, which produces less desire to invest — until it collapses.

Balancing loops stabilize: a thermostat, a self-correcting team dynamic, a market mechanism. When you push in one direction, forces push back.

The key insight: most 'unexpected' consequences are actually the visible operation of a loop you didn't map. If you find yourself repeatedly surprised, you have unmapped loops.

The bottleneck principle

From Eli Goldratt's Theory of Constraints: every system has ONE constraint at a time. Improvements elsewhere are wasted or even harmful. The task of the systems thinker is to (1) identify the constraint, (2) exploit it (get the most out of it before adding capacity), (3) subordinate everything else to the constraint, (4) elevate the constraint (add capacity), (5) find the new constraint.

Common systems mistakes

  • Local optimization. Making one part more efficient while the whole system runs worse.
  • Missing the delay. Judging an intervention prematurely, before its effects had time to appear.
  • Fixing the symptom, not the structure. Removing the visible problem while leaving the underlying dynamic in place. The problem returns in a slightly different form.
  • Assuming linearity. Doubling the input rarely doubles the output. In many systems, small changes produce large effects and vice versa.
  • Blaming individuals for systemic outputs. The person may or may not be part of the problem, but the system produced this pattern reliably before them and will reliably after.

The systems-thinking workflow

Note

THE TOOL — For any system you want to change

Note

1. Draw the system. Not the org chart. The actual flow of work, information, and decisions. 2. Identify the loops — reinforcing (R) and balancing (B). Mark them. 3. Identify the delays — where does a change now not show up for weeks or months? 4. Identify the bottleneck. This is where a small improvement produces disproportionate impact. 5. Propose the leverage move — the one intervention at the bottleneck that would matter most. 6. Predict second-order effects and unintended consequences. 7. Design the signal that would tell you if it's working — early, before the delay unfolds.

Note

THE GREAT MINDS COUNCIL — On the systems view

Note

Through Donella Meadows's lens: Meadows's 'Twelve Leverage Points' framework argues that the highest-leverage interventions in any system are usually the least visible — changing paradigms, changing goals, changing who has power. Changing individual parameters is the lowest-leverage. Through Peter Senge's lens: The Fifth Discipline: 'Today's problems come from yesterday's solutions.' Most persistent problems are the reappearance of solved problems whose solutions changed the system. Through Eli Goldratt's lens: Theory of Constraints: focus on the bottleneck. Everything else is noise. Through W. Edwards Deming's lens: A system is greater than the sum of its parts. Optimizing the parts to their maximum will damage the whole. Where the Council might be wrong: Systems thinking can become paralysis when every action is presented as unknowable. At some point, you act with imperfect understanding, watch the signals, and adjust.

Remember This

“Change the structure, not the symptoms.”
