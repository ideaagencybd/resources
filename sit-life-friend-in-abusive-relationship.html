A friend is in a relationship you believe is abusive

critical The Open Doorabusefriendshipsafetycritical
1

What is happening

The cancelled plans, the checked phone, the money they no longer control, the way they watch their partner before answering. You have hinted. They defended them, and now you see them less.

2

What is actually going on

  • Leaving is the most dangerous period, statistically. Pushing someone to leave before they have a plan can increase the risk to them, not reduce it.
  • People leave abusive relationships an average of several times before it holds. Returning is part of the process, not a failure of your advice.
  • Criticising the abuser directly usually backfires: it forces a defence and confirms the abuser's narrative that friends are hostile and should be avoided.
  • Isolation is the mechanism. Staying in contact — even shallow, cheerful contact — is the single most useful thing you can provide.
3

Do this, in this order

  1. Stay in contact, unconditionallyDo not make your support conditional on them leaving. That is exactly what the abuser predicted you would do.
  2. Describe behaviour, not character"He read your messages and you looked frightened" lands where "he is abusive" cannot.
  3. Ask, and then believe, and then stay"Are you okay? You can tell me anything and I will not tell you what to do."
  4. Offer concrete, specific helpA spare key, a bag kept at yours, a lift, a phone, money for a taxi, your address written down.
  5. Help them prepare rather than push them to actDocuments, money, a code word, a plan for the children, a packed bag elsewhere. Preparation is the actual work.
  6. Learn the local specialist service and share the numberThey can advise safely and confidentially, including on risk. You are not the expert; they are.
4

Words you can borrow

The open door
"I am not going anywhere and I am not going to lecture you. Whatever happens, ring me at any hour and I will come."
Naming behaviour
"When you said you had to ask him about Saturday, you looked scared. That is what worries me."
Offering the practical
"Keep a bag at mine with your documents in it. No questions, no pressure. It just sits there."
Agreeing a signal
"If you ever text me the word 'kettle', I will call you and say there is an emergency at mine, and I will come and get you."
5

What makes it worse

  • Ultimatums — "if you go back I am done." It removes their last outside support.
  • Confronting the abuser. It escalates risk for your friend, not for you.
  • Telling them what you would do. It reads as judgement and it is not informed by their risk.
  • Sharing what they told you with the wider friend group. It can reach the abuser.
  • Giving up because they defended them. That defence is a survival behaviour.
6

Once the pressure drops

  • Keep a private dated note of what you observe and are told, in case it is ever needed for police or court.
  • If they leave, expect a hard few months and stay close. Practical help beats advice.
  • Look after yourself too. Supporting someone through this is heavy and you need your own outlet.
  • Never say "I told you so," in any form, ever, including with your face.
Where this stops being yours to handle

If you believe there is immediate danger — strangulation, threats to kill, a weapon, threats to children, or escalation around pregnancy or separation — call emergency services. These are recognised markers of severe risk and they justify overriding their wish for privacy.

A note on this library

These are general principles written to be useful under pressure, not legal, medical, or financial advice. Laws and emergency procedures differ by country. If life is at risk, contact your emergency services first and read this afterwards.
