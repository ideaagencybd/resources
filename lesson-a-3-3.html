Verify Before You Trust

Confident sentences are not evidence

24 minThe Source Card4,253 words22 sections
Skill
Check sources, dates, incentives, and agreement
Tool
The Source Card
Result
You stop forwarding plausible nonsense
In one line
Confidence is a tone. Verification is a checklist.
The idea

The Hook

A sentence can be well-dressed and still be empty.

It has a number. It has a date that feels recent. It has a graph, or a screenshot of a graph. It arrived from someone you like, or from a model that has never once looked embarrassed. You feel the little click of now I know. That click is not knowledge. It is fluency.

Level 2 taught you to split facts from stories. This lesson is what you do to the "facts" before you let them into the room. Who said it. When. Why they might want you to believe it. Whether they were in a position to know. Whether anyone competent agrees. What would make it false.

If you skip this, the Figure-It-Out Loop becomes a machine for spreading polished rumors. Resourceful people are not more cynical. They are harder to rent.

The room

The Scenario

Monday, 11:06 a.m. Elena drops a link into the project channel.

"FYI — this is going around. We should probably change the vendor one-pager before Friday. Mara, can you fold it in?"

The link is a LinkedIn post from a consultant with 40,000 followers. The post says, with a red circle on a screenshot: "As of this month, any company over 500 employees must publish quarterly third-party spend or face fines. Most teams are not ready." There is a chart. There is the phrase "according to the new guidance." There is no link to the guidance.

Jonah reacts with a thumbs-up. A client-facing person asks, "Are we exposed?" The Room, even in Slack, starts to tilt.

Mara has twenty minutes before Elena's next huddle. She can fold a rumor into a leadership page, or she can find out whether the rumor is a thing.

What does she check, in order, before she types?

Your move

Your Move

Write your first three checks. Then look.

Common move. Trust the chart and the follower count. Rewrite the one-pager with a new "compliance" box. You are now the internal amplifier of a consultant's lead-generation post.

Better move. Google the sentence. Find three more posts saying the same thing. Agreement among copies is not agreement among sources. You have measured virality.

Expert move. Find the named instrument — the statute, the regulator page, the actual "guidance." Read the date, the scope, the who-it-applies-to. Check whether the consultant sells the fix they are alarming you about.

Resourceful move. Fill a Source Card on the post before you argue or comply. Six fields. Five minutes. Then a sentence Elena can use: "The post is from [who], dated [when], selling [what]. I cannot find a primary. I found [this official page] that says [X]. Recommend we do not change Friday's page until compliance confirms."

Why the common move fails: leadership pages have a long half-life. A rumor that gets into Friday's deck becomes "what we told the steering committee." Correcting it later feels like a confession.

What can go wrong with the expert move: you can vanish into legal PDFs and miss Elena's huddle. The card has a clock. Verification is a pass/fail on the claim in front of you, not a master's degree in administrative law.

The idea

The Big Idea

A source is not a vibe. It is a set of properties you can write down.

Who. Date. Incentive. Primary or not. Who agrees. What would disconfirm.

Until those are filled, you do not have a fact. You have a sentence with good posture. AI makes this worse, because it will invent the posture for free. Recency makes it worse, because "posted this morning" feels like "true this morning." Bias makes it worse, because the sentence that flatters your side does not trigger the card.

You already know Facts vs Stories. This is the stamp you put on a fact-candidate before it is allowed to travel.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Six fields. If you cannot fill them, that is the result.

Who. A named person or institution, with a role that could know. "Researchers say" is not a who. "A consultant who sells vendor-compliance workshops" is a who. "The regulator's own page" is a better who. Anonymity is allowed in journalism for a reason; it is not allowed in your one-pager.

Date. Of the claim, and of the underlying thing. A 2026 post describing 2019 guidance is not current. A paper's publication date is not the data date. Software docs dated last week can still describe a version you do not run. Recency is a clue, not a halo.

Incentive. What happens to them if you believe. Sales. Status. Politics. A genuine public-health duty. Mixed motives are normal; hidden ones are the problem. You do not need a conspiracy. You need to notice who gets the invoice.

Primary? Did this source do the measuring, issue the rule, or stand there, or are they retelling? LinkedIn is almost never primary. A meta-analysis is not the original trial. A model summarizing a law it cannot fetch is not primary. Get one step closer. If you cannot, label the claim "unverified."

Agreement. Who competent and independent says the same thing? Three blogs recycling one screenshot is one source with extra fonts. Two labs, two methods, same direction is interesting. Official text plus your counsel is often enough to act in a company. Official text plus a consultant's alarm is not.

What would disconfirm. The adult question. If the rule exists, it has a name and a scope; absence of both disconfirms the post. If a supplement "reverses inflammation," a trial with a pre-registered outcome could fail; if nobody ran that trial, you do not have a finding. Karl Popper's name gets used as a bumper sticker here. The useful piece is smaller: a claim that cannot say what would prove it wrong is not doing work.

On AI hallucinations, specifically: large language models generate plausible continuation. They will fabricate paper titles, case names, quotes, URLs, and "the statute says." They do not know they are fabricating. Your card still applies. Who = a model, not a witness. Date = its training cutoff plus whatever it invented. Primary? No. Agreement? Unknown until you open a real page. You will go deeper in Level 6. The rule for this level: no model output enters a document until a human-opened source backs the line.

Bias is not only other people. Confirmation is the usual thief: you fill the card generously for the side you like. Run the same six fields on the claim you want to be true. That is intellectual honesty from Level 2, with a form.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Source Card

One claim per card. Not a whole article. The sentence you are about to trust, forward, or put in a deck.

FieldYour notes
Claim (verbatim)
WhoName, role, ability to know
DateOf this telling / of the underlying data or rule
IncentiveWhat they gain if you believe
Primary?Yes / no / one step closer is…
AgreementIndependent sources that actually agree
DisconfirmWhat would make this false, and did I look?
StatusUse / use with caveat / do not use
Next checkHuman or primary I still need

How to fill it in five to eight minutes:

  1. Copy the claim verbatim. Paraphrase is how charts grow extra teeth.
  2. Who and date, from the page itself, not from memory. If the page has no date, write "undated" — that is already a finding.
  3. Incentive in one line. "Sells the software that fixes this." "Wants a promotion." "Public agency, mandate is X." You can be wrong about motive. You cannot skip the attempt.
  4. Primary? If no, spend three minutes on the closest official or original. site: from 3.2. If you cannot find it, status is "do not use" or "use as rumor."
  5. Agreement. One independent source, not a retweet. If the claim is strong ("fines starting this month"), you want the issuer.
  6. Disconfirm. Write one thing you would accept as a no. Then look for it. The consultant's post dies if the regulator's page, updated this month, has no such duty.
  7. Status and next check. Be boring. "Do not fold into Friday's page. Ask compliance, send them this card."

Mara's card, filled as a scenario:

  • Claim: "Companies over 500 employees must publish quarterly third-party spend or face fines, as of this month."
  • Who: LinkedIn consultant, not a regulator.
  • Date: post from Sunday; "new guidance" unnamed.
  • Incentive: the comments pitch a paid workshop.
  • Primary?: no. Closest: the procurement regulator's page, last updated six weeks ago, describes existing thresholds, not a new public-reporting duty.
  • Agreement: three other consultants repeating the same screenshot. Zero official notices.
  • Disconfirm: an official instrument with an effective date this month. Not found.
  • Status: do not use.
  • Next check: one email to compliance with the post and the official page.

That is verification. It is not a personality. It is a form.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

When Elena is waiting and you have ninety seconds:

Three questions, out loud if you can:

  1. Who benefits if I believe this?
  2. Can I open the original in the next minute?
  3. What would this look like if it were false?

If you cannot open an original, your public sentence is: "I have not verified this. I will not put it in the page yet." That sentence is professional. "I saw online" is not.

A holding line for the huddle: "The post is secondary and selling a fix. I cannot find a primary. I am not changing the one-pager until compliance says the rule is real."

The hack

The Hack

Check the date and the "last updated" and the screenshot's own date.

A common trick — sometimes lazy, sometimes done on purpose — is an old chart in a new post. The social feed is a time machine with the clocks removed. Reverse-image the chart if the claim is doing a lot of work. If you cannot, write "screenshot, undated" on the card. That is often enough to keep it out of The Room.

Second hack: search the most surprising noun in quotes plus the word hoax or fact check — not because fact-checkers are infallible (they have incentives too), but because it is a cheap way to find the counter-claim you should put on the card. Then verify the fact-check with the same six fields. This is not cynicism. It is two-sided looking.

AI move

The AI Move

Treat the model as a clerk who has never been fired for lying.

Use AI for: extracting a claim verbatim from a long post; proposing who/incentive hypotheses you then check; listing what a primary source would look like ("if this regulation exists, it should appear on X kind of site"); turning your filled card into a four-line brief for Elena.

Do not use AI for: confirming that a paper exists; quoting a law; "is this true?"; generating a bibliography you have not opened; medical, legal, or financial conclusions.

Prompt (copy, then fill):

Context: I am filling a Source Card. Do not decide the truth for me.
Claim, verbatim:
"""
[paste]
"""
What I know about the speaker and the channel:
[who, platform, date of the post]
What I have already opened:
[links I actually opened, plus dates I saw]

Goal:
1) Restate the claim in one falsifiable sentence.
2) Propose entries for: Who, Incentive, Primary?, What would disconfirm — label each as FACT FROM MY NOTES or HYPOTHESIS.
3) Tell me the nearest primary I should look for, and a search query (no invented URLs).
4) List ways a model like you might hallucinate about this topic.

Constraints: Do not invent citations, case names, paper titles, or official bulletin numbers. If you are not sure a document exists, say you are not sure.
Output: Source Card fields, short.
Verification: Which of your lines must I confirm by opening a page?

What to verify: every proper noun, number, and date the model adds. If it "helpfully" names the guidance, search that name. If nothing comes up, you just caught a hallucination. Put "model-invented title" on the card. That is a useful scar.

Do not paste: other people's medical details, personnel allegations, unreleased filings, anything that would harm someone if the prompt leaked.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "make verification cheaper than forwarding."

Current examples of the category: the site's "About" and "last updated"; a reverse-image search for viral charts; a browser's reader view so you can see the date without the popups; official gazettes and regulator search boxes; for academic claims, the journal page or a recognized index, not a screenshot of an abstract; version history on a wiki, which at least shows that humans fought over the sentence.

When not to use technology: a fact-checking site is another source, not a pope. Put it on a card too. A browser extension that "flags misinformation" is a black box with a vendor. Useful as an alarm. Useless as a verdict.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Verifying the speaker's vibe instead of the claim.

The consultant seems professional. The model sounds calm. The uncle is sincere. The journalist is from a paper you like. None of that fills Date, Primary, or Disconfirm. Charisma is not a field on the card.

The sibling mistake: "I looked it up" meaning "I found other people saying it." That is the better move from Your Move, and it is still a failure. Copies are not corroboration.

Recovery: if you already forwarded the post, send the correction with the same energy, not a quiet edit. "I passed this along before checking. Official page does not show a new duty. Withdrawing it." That sentence has more dignity than a week of hoping nobody noticed.

Defense

The Defense

The Source Card fails when:

  • You need a decision in seconds and the cost of delay is physical. Then you use the protocol you already trust (evacuate, stop the bleed, follow the runbook) and verify later.
  • The only sources are classified, paywalled, or in a language you cannot check. Say "I cannot verify" rather than laundering a rumor through your voice.
  • You fill the card as a prosecutor. Six fields used to demolish a claim you dislike, and three fields used to protect a claim you like, is not verification. It is stationery for a side.
  • The claim is about a person's character. Cards work poorly as gossip machines. If it is an HR or legal matter, you are in the wrong lesson.

Limitation: a filled card is not certainty. It is a documented reason to use, caveat, or drop. You can still be wrong. You will be wrong less often than the person who trusted the red circle.

Council

The Masters' Table

Richard Feynman. Cargo cult science, again, because it belongs here more than anywhere in this level: the appearance of rigor without the substance. A chart is a ritual. A citation you did not open is a ritual. Through Feynman's lens, the first principle is not to fool yourself, and you are the easiest one.

Marie Curie. Replication and record. A result that cannot be found again, in the notebook or in another lab, does not get to be a fact yet. The disconfirm field is her world, domesticated for a Slack channel.

Benjamin Franklin. Inoculation against being used. He printed rumors and retractions; he also ran experiments. Through his lens, public claims are a marketplace. Bring a scale.

Herbert Simon. You will not complete a full literature review before Elena's huddle. Satisficing verification: closest primary, date, incentive, a yes/no on whether Friday's page should change. That is enough for this decision. It is not enough to write a paper.

Daniel Kahneman. The fast system loves a coherent story. A consultant's post is coherent. Coherence is not evidence. The card is how the slow system gets a vote — the same gap the Pause trained, applied to incoming information.

Andrew Grove. Output, again. The output of Mara's twenty minutes is not "I did research." It is a recommendation Elena can act on: change nothing pending compliance, with two links attached.

Synthesis. Prefer the original, write the date, notice the invoice, leave a trail.

Where the council could be wrong. Feynman's allergy to fooling yourself can become a pose of universal doubt that never ships. Curie's standard of replication is the wrong standard for "is the office open on Monday." Simon's "good enough" can rubber-stamp a convenient source. Kahneman popularizations get over-applied to every disagreement. Use the card. Do not join a church of skepticism.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to Monday, 11:06.

Calm. Mara does not rewrite the one-pager in the adrenaline of Elena's FYI. Pause. The claim is a thing in the world, not an order.

Observe. A LinkedIn post, consultant, unnamed "guidance," chart with a red circle, no primary link. Jonah's thumbs-up. A question about exposure. Friday page still the job.

Define. In twenty minutes, classify the claim: use / caveat / drop. Tell Elena in four lines. Do not become the company's interpreter of a screenshot.

Verify. That is this whole lesson. She opens a card.

Prioritize. Primary and date over arguing with Jonah in the thread.

Find resources / people. Regulator's site via site:. Compliance person. The consultant's own "about" page (workshop sales). Search / Compare / Verify from 3.2 for the named instrument.

Options. Fold it in. Argue in Slack. Or card + four lines + a compliance ping.

Risks. Ignoring a real new rule. Amplifying a fake one. Either can be expensive. The card reduces both better than speed.

Choose. Card, then communicate.

Communicate. In the channel, because the rumor is already public: "Looked at this. The post is a consultant selling a workshop; it does not link a primary. The [regulator] page updated [date] does not show a new public-reporting duty for companies our size. I am not changing Friday's one-pager on this. [Compliance], can you confirm so we can close it?"

Act. Sends compliance the post, the official page, and her card. Keeps building the actual vendor page from verified numbers.

Document. Card saved next to the one-pager. If this rumor returns in The Room on Friday, she has a paper trail, not a memory.

Adapt. Compliance replies at 2:00: no such rule; a different disclosure exists for a different class of firm, already handled. Mara adds one footnote, not a new section. The consultant's chart was a cousin of a real thing, stretched.

Reflect. The thumbs-up was social proof, not agreement among sources. Next time she will fill Incentive before she feels the click of now I know.

That is a resourceful human in a feed. Not a debunker for sport. A person who would not let a red circle into a steering deck.

Practice

Practice

Do this now. Twelve minutes.

  1. Pick a claim you almost believed in the last week — work, health, money, politics, a model’s sentence. One sentence, verbatim.
  2. Fill all six fields plus status. If a field is empty, write "unknown," not a guess dressed as a fact.
  3. Spend five minutes trying to open a primary or an official page.
  4. Write the sentence you would send if a manager asked you to act on it today.
  5. If you used AI to find the claim, run the prompt and then open whatever it names. Note whether the name was real.

The exercise is the card, not a new opinion. You should be able to photograph the table.

Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — a confident forward. Situation: a manager pastes a post and asks you to "fold it in" to a real deliverable. What most people do: fold it in, because saying no feels like extra work and a status risk. Why it fails: your name is now on the rumor. Resourceful response: Source Card, then a four-line status. Offer a path: "I can add a caveat box today or a real section after compliance confirms." Words to use: who, date, primary missing/found, recommendation. Words to avoid: "This is fake news," "Trust me." Tool: Source Card. AI assist: turn the filled card into the four lines — then check the lines against the card. What could go wrong: you are blunt in a public channel and Elena hears it as showing her up. Send her the card privately and a short public "checking before we fold in."

Digital — an AI-written brief. Situation: you asked a model for "the current rule on X" and it produced a clean paragraph with a citation. What most people do: paste it into the deck, maybe change the voice. Why it fails: invented citations are common; fluency hides the hole. Resourceful response: every citation opened or removed. Card on the model's paragraph: Who = model; Primary? = no. Words to use: "I cannot confirm this citation; dropping it." Words to avoid: "According to my research" when you mean "according to a chatbot." Tool: Source Card + the Level 6 habit, started early. What could go wrong: the citation is real but the model summarized it backwards. Open and read the part it pointed at.

Family — a health claim with love attached. Situation: someone who cares about you wants you to change a treatment because of a video. What most people do: comply to keep peace, or mock the video. Why it fails: peace becomes an uncontrolled experiment, or mockery becomes the topic. Resourceful response: card the claim together if they will; take the best source you can find to a clinician; no dose changes in the kitchen. Words to use: "Let's bring this to [clinician] before anyone changes a dose. I could not find a primary / I found a small study on [population]." Words to avoid: a lecture on cognitive bias at the table. Tool: Source Card, then a professional. What could go wrong: you delay real care. If someone is in acute trouble, emergency care first, cards later.

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Elena, in the huddle, with Jonah and a finance lead: "We're going to look slow if this is real and we didn't move. Just put a slide in. We can always correct it."

The Room wants speed as insurance. That is how rumors become minutes of the meeting.

Mara, after a Pause: "If it's real, we should move. If I put an unverified fine on a steering slide, that becomes our position. I spent ten minutes: no primary, consultant is selling a workshop, official page doesn't show the duty. I can add a slide that says 'claim under review, compliance by Wednesday' or I can add nothing and send the card. I will not write 'we are exposed to new fines' as if we checked."

She has given them a Minimum Useful Version of honesty. If they order the slide anyway, she labels it "unverified — source: LinkedIn post [date]" so The Room can see its own decision. Verification does not always win the meeting. It can still refuse to launder the sentence.

Reflect

Reflection

  • Which field of the card do you skip when you like the claim?
  • When did you last forward something you had not dated?
  • What incentive do you personally have to believe the last confident sentence you heard at work?
  • How do you currently treat model output — as a draft, as a source, or as a fact?
  • What would disconfirm a belief you are using to make a decision this week?
Remember

Remember This

If you cannot fill the card, you do not have a fact. You have a sentence you are about to launder.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

A little more on "primary," "recency," and "agreement," because people weaponize all three.

Primary is a relationship, not a medal. A regulator's page is primary for "what we issued." It is not primary for "what happens in the market." A randomized trial is primary for its measured outcome. It is not primary for "you should take this." Your job is to know what question the source is entitled to answer.

Recency: new is not true. Old is not true. Match the date to the question. For a live outage, a five-year-old page is suspect. For a mathematical proof, last week is irrelevant. For medical guidance, you want the current protocol and the evidence it sits on, which may be older. "Updated 2026" on a blog can mean they changed the header.

Agreement: independence matters more than count. Ten influencers, one screenshot, one incentive. Two agencies, two methods, same direction. Also notice forced agreement: a company line, a party line, a model repeating its own first guess in five voices because you asked it to "steelman and then confirm." That last one is a parlor trick. Independent means independent.

If you want a harder variation, keep a Source Card on your own recommendation before you send it. Who are you. What is your incentive. Are you primary. What would disconfirm. Intellectual honesty with a form is less romantic than a confession and more useful on a Tuesday.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 24 hours, fill one Source Card on a claim that is trying to enter your work, your family, or your phone.

Send the status line to a real human if the claim would have changed an action — even if the status is "do not use."

Keep the card. You will need the habit in 3.B, when the brief is unclear and the internet is happy to invent a brief for you.

Sources to Explore

  • Richard P. Feynman, "Cargo Cult Science," 1974.
  • Karl Popper, Conjectures and Refutations (1963) — on falsifiability as a demand you can make of a claim, not as a full philosophy you must join.
  • Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011) — coherence versus evidence; use as a gateway, with the usual caveats about popular dual-process language.
  • C. Thi Nguyen, "Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles," Episteme, 2020 — on why more agreement can mean worse information.
  • Your regulator's or professional body's actual site — practice opening a primary this week instead of a summary of one.
