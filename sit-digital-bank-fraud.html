Money has left your account and you did not authorise it

high The First Hourfraudbankmoneyrecovery
1

What is happening

A notification, or a balance that is wrong. One large payment or a run of small ones. Some you recognise, some you do not, and there may be a new payee you have never set up.

2

What is actually going on

  • Recovery odds are governed almost entirely by speed. Funds sit briefly in a receiving account before being moved on, and after that they are usually gone.
  • Small test charges are reconnaissance. They precede the large one, often by a day or two.
  • Whether you "authorised" it matters legally: unauthorised card fraud is normally refunded, while payments you were tricked into making follow a different route. Describe what happened accurately and let them classify it.
  • Fraud on the account is often downstream of a compromised email or phone number, so fixing only the bank leaves the door open.
3

Do this, in this order

  1. Phone the bank now, using the number on your cardNot email, not chat if a phone line exists. Ask them to freeze the card and the account and to log a fraud case.
  2. Ask explicitly for a recall or chargebackUse the words "please attempt to recall the payment." A recall within hours can succeed; a day later it usually cannot.
  3. Check for changes you did not makeNew payees, changed address, changed phone number, raised transfer limits, new device registrations.
  4. Secure the email attached to the accountNew unique password, two-factor on, sessions signed out, forwarding rules checked.
  5. Check whether your phone number was portedIf your SIM stopped working around the same time, this is likely SIM-swap fraud. Tell both the bank and the mobile provider.
  6. Report to the national fraud service and get a referenceInsurers and banks often require it, and it supports any later dispute.
4

Words you can borrow

Opening the call
"I need to report fraud on my account. Please freeze the card and the account, then log a formal fraud case and give me the reference."
Asking for recall
"The payment went out at 14:20 today. Please attempt an immediate recall from the receiving bank."
If you were tricked into paying
"I was deceived into making this payment. I want it treated as authorised push payment fraud and assessed for reimbursement."
If refused
"Please log this as a formal complaint and send me your final response in writing, with the details of the ombudsman."
5

What makes it worse

  • Waiting until the morning. The overnight delay is often the whole difference.
  • Speaking to anyone who called you about the fraud. That is frequently the fraud itself.
  • Emailing the bank as your only action.
  • Accepting a first refusal as final. These decisions are appealable and appeals succeed regularly.
  • Guessing on the phone about what you did. State plain facts; do not speculate.
6

Once the pressure drops

  • Get everything in writing: the case reference, the timeline, and the decision with its reasoning.
  • Escalate to the financial ombudsman or regulator if refused. It is free and it works more often than people expect.
  • Set up alerts on every transaction, not just large ones.
  • Keep a small separate account for online payments so the exposed surface is never your main balance.
A note on this library

These are general principles written to be useful under pressure, not legal, medical, or financial advice. Laws and emergency procedures differ by country. If life is at risk, contact your emergency services first and read this afterwards.
