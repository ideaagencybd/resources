Deep Dive 4: AI Safety and Strategy

707 words

How to think about AI in your career and life over the next decade.

Level 6 covered practical use of AI. This section is about the longer question: how to think about AI as it reshapes work, careers, and the environment you'll operate in over the next 10-20 years.

Everything below is deliberately category-level rather than brand-specific, because specifics will change. The categories are more stable.

What AI is currently good at (and getting better at)

  • Generation of plausible text, code, and images.
  • Pattern-matching across large amounts of text.
  • Structured synthesis — summarization, classification, extraction.
  • Explaining and rephrasing.
  • Well-specified problems with clear success criteria.
  • Role-play and rehearsal.

What AI is currently bad at

  • Genuinely novel reasoning — as opposed to recombining patterns it has seen.
  • Anything that requires access to real-time, local, or proprietary information not in its training data.
  • Long-horizon planning where the plan must adapt to reality as it unfolds.
  • Confidence calibration — AI does not know what it doesn't know.
  • Judgment about your specific human situation, relationships, or organizational context.
  • Ethical reasoning in the deep sense (as opposed to producing the shape of ethical arguments).
  • Physical presence, accountability, or trust-building.

The five career implications you should think about

Note

THE TOOL — For anyone whose work touches AI

Note

1. Move UP the abstraction ladder AI is best at execution of well-specified tasks. Value increasingly lives in problem-definition, judgment, cross-functional synthesis, and human relationships. These are the Levels 1-8 of this book. 2. Build public artifacts Write. Publish. Speak. Reputation portable across companies matters more in a fast-changing economy than tenure at any single one. 3. Use AI daily in your current work Not experimentally. Daily. The people who benefit are the ones who have discovered what AI actually changes about their workflow — which requires using it enough to know. 4. Invest in judgment-heavy skills Anything that requires human trust, personal accountability, or presence. These are less compressible by AI. 5. Build the discipline of verification As AI outputs multiply, the value of humans who can reliably distinguish accurate from plausible grows. Being that human is a career.

The three ethical frames worth carrying

  • PRIVACY: what personal, corporate, or client data do you send to AI? What do you not? Match the sensitivity of the data to the trust profile of the tool.
  • TRANSPARENCY: when is it appropriate to disclose that AI was involved in producing something? Legal contracts typically require disclosure. Personal notes typically don't. Most other cases involve judgment.
  • RESPONSIBILITY: AI produced it; you're accountable. This applies to code, reports, emails, and analyses. The 'AI told me so' defense does not work.

The organizational implications

If you're in a leadership position, the AI question isn't 'how do we use it' — it's 'how do we redesign our work so that AI amplifies our human strengths rather than eroding them?' Three principles:

  • Do not replace human trust with AI-mediated trust. Customer relationships still run on humans. Team relationships still run on humans.
  • Do use AI to eliminate the drudge work that keeps people from doing what only they can do. This is the 'centaur' pattern.
  • Do teach verification as a core discipline. Every professional in the organization should know when to trust AI output and when to check.
Note

THE GREAT MINDS COUNCIL — On the AI moment

Note

Through Stuart Russell's lens: The purpose of AI is to help humans achieve their goals, including goals we haven't fully specified. The specification is our job. Through Douglas Engelbart's lens: 'Augmenting Human Intellect' — the frame from 1962 still holds. AI is at its best as an amplifier of human capability, not a substitute. Through Ethan Mollick's lens: Co-Intelligence: the professionals who thrive will be those who use AI as a genuine collaborator — not those who avoid it, and not those who over-rely on it. Through Nassim Taleb's lens: Fragile systems become more fragile under automation. The critical question: what happens when the automation fails? Design for that moment first. Where the Council might be wrong: All predictions about AI's near-term impact — including the ones in this section — will look somewhat wrong in five years. Use them as frames for thinking, not as predictions.

Remember This

“AI everywhere, not everything. Judgment stays with you.”
