Checklists Beat Heroics

Memory is a terrible operating system

The idea

The Hook

You are good at this. That is the risk.

The fifth time you ran the launch, your hands knew the path. The twelfth time, you skipped the dumb step because you have never forgotten it — until the day you did. A name not on the list. A setting left in test. Nobody was new. You were competent, busy, and human.

Heroics feel like the adult response to complexity: try harder, remember. High-risk fields learned that excellence does not scale across Tuesday, fatigue, and interruption. They built checklists. Not because their people were stupid. Because their people were experts.

If your process still depends on you being "on," you do not have a process. You have a mood.

The room

The Scenario

Thursday, 6:40 p.m. Mara is shipping a customer demo at 9:00 tomorrow.

She has done this before. Environment, data set, accounts, the slide that always breaks, the backup laptop, who speaks first. Elena wants a dry run at 8:15 a.m. The building Wi-Fi is a known traitor.

Jonah, already late: "You've got this. You always do."

She also has a half-written email, a child to pick up, and a brain already in the car. She will remember the backup dongle. She always does.

At 11:20 p.m. she thinks of the dongle, then of sleep. At 8:16 a.m. the dongle is on the kitchen counter, the slide is the old deck, and the demo account is pointing at staging.

What should she have built last night — not as a personality, as a tool?

Your move

Your Move

Common move. Trust memory plus a burst of evening virtue. Maybe a chaotic note titled "demo!!!!" with seven items and a grocery list.

Better move. A long SOP nobody will read at 11 p.m. A hero staying until midnight to "just go through it once."

Expert move. A short checklist of the steps that, if skipped, blow the event — not a novel of how to do the job. Dongle. Deck version. Account environment. Wi-Fi fallback. Who opens. Who owns the backup laptop. Verb plus object a second person could tick.

Resourceful move. The short checklist, used tonight as do-confirm (do the step, then tick) for packing, and as read-do (read the line, then do it) in the morning when the room is hot. Card on the laptop, not in a folder named "process." Two minutes with Jonah for the one step only he remembers.

Why the common move fails: expertise hides skips. Tired experts skip the obvious step, not the hard one.

What can go wrong: a 40-line anxiety document. People stop using it, then blame "checklists." Short, tested, embarrassing. That is the design.

The idea

The Big Idea

A checklist is a prosthetic for a brilliant, limited brain.

It is not an insult. It is how you stop paying for the same miss twice. Pull the must-not-skip steps out of memory and into a place a tired person can obey.

Explained

The Simple Explanation

Atul Gawande's The Checklist Manifesto (2009) is the flagship case, and the actual point is easy to miss.

The point is not "doctors are careless." The point is that modern work contains more necessary steps than even experts reliably remember under time pressure, interruption, and fatigue. In surgery, a skipped step can kill. In your job, it can demo-fail, mis-bill, or leak data. Same species of error. Different stakes.

Gawande draws on aviation: the Boeing Model 299, ancestor of the B-17, crashed on a 1935 evaluation flight after the pilot left the gust locks engaged. Too much airplane for one man to fly. The response was not a better hero. It was a checklist.

He also tells the medical story that made the idea travel: Peter Pronovost's short central-line checklist at Johns Hopkins, then a Michigan ICU program. Pronovost and colleagues reported in the New England Journal of Medicine (2006) a large drop in catheter-related bloodstream infections associated with a bundle of practices plus a checklist and a culture that let nurses stop a doctor who skipped a step. In 2009, Haynes, Gawande, and colleagues reported in the NEJM that introducing the WHO's 19-item surgical safety checklist in eight hospitals was associated with a drop in deaths from 1.5% to 0.8% and in inpatient complications from 11.0% to 7.0%. That was a prospective before-after study, not a perfect experiment. Later implementation has been mixed — a checklist dumped on a team without the culture is a laminated sermon. Treat the numbers as a strong signal, not a spell.

Aviation and medicine have a clear "wrong" and a pause the institution can require. Knowledge work is messier. Your checklist has to be even shorter, or it will lose to Slack.

Steal the design principles, not the costume. Pause points. Must-not-skip steps. A tool people actually tick. Permission for the junior person to stop the line.

The tool

The Practical Tool

Name: The Checklist

Build it for recurring work that can fail in a boring way.

  1. Pick one job. "Customer demo," "close the shop," "leave the house with a child." Not "how we work."
  2. List only the skippable disasters. What have you actually missed in the last five runs? Those lines earn their place.
  3. Write verbs a second human could tick. "Confirm deck is v4 on the USB and the cloud." Not "be prepared." Five to nine lines for knowledge work. If you need twenty, you have two checklists or a process that needs mapping (5.4).
  4. Choose the pause point. Night before. At the door. Before "go live." A checklist without a pause is a document.
  5. Pick read-do vs do-confirm. Read-do for unfamiliar or morning-of. Do-confirm for familiar packing. Aviation uses both.
  6. Put it where the work is. Card on the laptop. First page of the runbook. Not a wiki someone will "look at later."
  7. After one real use, cut. If nobody ticked it, it dies.

Fast path

The Fastest Reliable Method

Ten minutes, a recurring failure: five lines on a card. Each line is a miss you have already made. Put the card on the object that travels. Use it once.

The hack

The Hack

Make the junior person, or tired future you, the audience. If the checklist only makes sense to the hero who wrote it, it is a diary. Read it out loud. If a competent newcomer could not tick it, rewrite the line.

AI move

The AI Move

AI is useful for a first draft. It will also invent steps that sound professional and have never failed in your actual run. That is how you get a novel.

Use AI for: turning "last five misses" into verb-object lines; suggesting pause points; drafting a one-page SOP after you have a working checklist.

Do not use AI for: a 40-line "best practice" list; safety-critical medical, legal, or aviation procedures.

No corporate language.

WHAT GOOD OUTPUT LOOKS LIKE: A card, not a manual.

Verify every line against a real run. Do not paste: patient data, credentials, unpublished security steps, HR files.

Tech

The Technology Move

The job is "put the must-not-skip steps in the path of the work."

Category: a physical or in-flow tick, not a documentation graveyard. Examples: a paper card; a checklist on the ticket; a form with required fields; a deploy pipeline that refuses to ship without tests. Do not automate a judgment you have not named. A 30-field playbook trains people to alt-tab.

Mistake

The Common Mistake

Writing a manifesto and calling it a checklist. Listing everything reduces anxiety. The team nods. Nobody uses it on the day. Then a miss occurs and someone says "we have a process."

Recovery: print the novel. Circle the five lines that have ever failed. Run the five. Add back only what the misses demand.

Defense

The Defense

Checklists fail when:

  • The work is truly novel. A checklist cannot invent a first-principles answer (2.5). Use the Figure-It-Out Loop. After the second run, write the card.
  • The culture punishes the person who pauses. Pronovost's result was not ink. It was permission to stop the line.
  • You use a checklist to avoid skill. A pilot still flies. The card catches skips; it does not replace judgment.
  • You never update after the machine changes. A stale checklist is a false floor.

Limitation: this will not make you careful as a personality. It will catch the boring miss when you are not.

Council

The Masters' Table

Atul Gawande. The problem of many fields is ineptitude under complexity, not ignorance. You know the step. You still skip it.

W. Edwards Deming. The system produces the result. If the demo fails every third time, stop hunting a more conscientious Mara. Put a pause and a card in the system. You will map this in 5.4.

Andy Grove. Standard work raises task-relevant maturity. A checklist is a cheap trainer that does not require you in the room.

James Clear. You fall to your systems. A hero's memory is not a system. A card on the laptop is.

The aviation designers after Model 299. Not a better speech about vigilance. A tool that assumed a good pilot could still miss a lock.

Synthesis. Experts fail in ordinary ways. Design for that, or keep paying.

Where the council could be wrong. Checklist culture can become compliance theater: ticks without looking. Keep the card brutally short and pair it with one real pause. A tick is not a thought.

Best move

The Best-Possible Response

Back to 6:40 p.m.

Calm. She does not stay until midnight. Twelve minutes on a card.

Observe. Known misses: dongle, deck version, staging account, Wi-Fi, speaking order. Constraint: child pickup.

Define. Packing checklist tonight (do-confirm). 8:10 a.m. run checklist (read-do) before Elena's dry run.

Verify. Voice note to Jonah: "What did I forget last time?" Extra login for the client observer. That line goes on the card.

Prioritize. Must-not-skip over nice-to-have. "Bring good energy" does not make the card.

Find / Options / Risks. Jonah owns backup laptop. Elena owns the 8:15 pause — Mara will ask for two minutes of silence. Frame it: "This is for 8 a.m. me, not a comment on you."

Choose / Communicate / Act. Two short lists. Five ticks in the channel, who owns which, where the dongle lives. Pack against the card. Dongle on the strap.

Document / Adapt / Reflect. Card stays in the sleeve. After the demo, one line: what was missing. Did she try to add twenty items? Cut.

Practice

Practice

Fifteen minutes.

  1. Pick one recurring job you actually do in the next week.
  2. Write the last three misses. If you claim there are none, ask a person who watches you work.
  3. Draft five to nine verb-object lines. Mark each read-do or do-confirm.
  4. Write the pause point and the physical home of the card.
  5. Cut one line that is really a value ("be thorough"). Replace it with a behavior.
  6. Read it to someone. If they frown, rewrite the line.
Real life

Real-Life Challenge

Work — the launch or demo. Situation: a recurring event with boring failure modes. What most people do: trust the person who is "good at this." Why it fails: that person has a kitchen counter. Resourceful response: a 5–9 line card; pause point; named owners. Words to use: "Ninety seconds on the card before we go live." Words to avoid: "We've done this a hundred times." Tool: The Checklist. AI assist: draft from misses; you cut. What could go wrong: theater ticks. Assign a person who can say "stop."

Home — leaving the house. Situation: keys, medication, charger, child's thing, stove. What most people do: a shouted inventory at the door, then a U-turn. Why it fails: shouted inventory is memory in a worse mood. Resourceful response: a five-line door card, or a bowl that is the checklist. Words to use: the five nouns. Words to avoid: "Why do we always…" Tool: Checklist as environment (1.6). What could go wrong: a card nobody stands still for. Attach it to a pause you already take (locks, lights).

Bad room

Bad-Room Version

Same Thursday. Worse.

The demo is for a client who can cancel. Jonah's train is cancelled. Elena is in a mood. Mara's child is sick. She is packing in eight minutes.

This is not the night to write a beautiful SOP. This is the night the card earns the rent.

Read-do, out loud, even if it feels stupid: dongle on strap; deck v4 on USB and cloud; account = production; hotspot tested; backup laptop in the bag — she owns it now; observer login on a paper slip.

If Elena tries to start the 8:15 without the pause: "Two minutes. I'm running the card so we don't demo staging. Then I'm yours."

That is how experts sound when they have a process instead of a prayer. If something still fails, the card gets a new line. Blame the hole, then the system, then — if it is still true — the person. Deming's order. Next lesson.

Reflect

Reflection

  • What did you last miss that you are "too experienced" to miss?
  • Where does a checklist already exist in your life that you trust?
  • Who in your workplace would be punished for pausing to tick a card?
  • Which of your current "processes" is a novel nobody runs?
  • If you left for two weeks, what would break because it lives only in your head?
Remember

Remember This

Memory is not a process. If it must not be skipped, it must be ticked.

Deep dive

Optional Deep Dive

Aviation versus knowledge work, without cargo-culting the cockpit.

Cockpit checklists work because the airplane is a stable machine, the failures are well-known, the pause is institutionalized, and two people can cross-check. White-collar cargo-culting copies the aesthetic — callouts, a 40-page "playbook" — and skips the conditions. Your work has unstable machines, political pauses, and Slack instead of a copilot.

Steal principles, not costumes: pause points; must-not-skip steps from real misses; a second pair of eyes when the skip is expensive; permission to halt.

Gawande distinguishes problems of ignorance (we do not know) from problems of ineptitude (we know and fail to apply). Checklists are for the second. If you do not know, you are in Level 3. Writing a checklist to hide ignorance is how bureaucracies get thick and still fail.

On the WHO evidence: celebrate the direction, keep the caveats. Before-after studies can move with extra attention, not only with the card. Implementation research since 2009 has found that how you introduce the list — leadership, the right to speak up — often dominates the ink. A card in a frightened culture is a prop.

Harder variation: take one checklist you already have and watch someone use it. Do not help. Note where they stall, skip, or laugh. Rewrite those lines. A checklist is a user interface for a tired human.

You will need this in 5.B when the machine is on fire. Improvisation without a five-line floor is how fires become traditions.

Mission

Field Mission

In the next 48 hours, build one 5–9 line checklist for a real recurring job and use it once.

After: the job; the lines people actually ticked; the line everyone skipped (cut or rewrite it); the miss you will not make the same way twice.

Put the card where the work is. A checklist in a drawer is a wish.
